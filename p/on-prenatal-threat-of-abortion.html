On the prenatal threat of abortion

Published on November 21, 2025 · 33 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The threat of abor­tion is not an event that dis­ap­pears into the dark­ness of pren­at­al un­aware­ness. It is the first form of the world that ex­ist­ence en­coun­ters, the first dir­ec­tion in­scribed in the body before the mind appears, before memory takes shape, before time is in­tro­duced as a se­quence. Where bio­lo­gic­al life begins without thought and without speech, ex­ist­ence re­ceives its first signal: that “I am” is not yet certain. The threat is not per­ceived as an idea, un­der­stood as fear, or or­gan­ised as an emotion; it is in­scribed as an ori­ent­a­tion. Before there is any con­scious­ness, there is an “I tend towards some­thing”, a dir­ec­tion towards coming into ex­ist­ence. When this tending is ab­ruptly in­ter­rup­ted, ex­ist­ence re­ceives not a message of neg­a­tion but the form of in­stabil­ity. What the mind will later call “re­jec­tion”, “fear”, “fra­gil­ity” is, at first, simply the ex­per­i­ence that the move­ment towards the light can be cut short. This is the pre-tem­por­al memory of ex­ist­ence.

Neuro­bi­o­logy, without in­tend­ing this theo­lo­gic­ally or phe­nomen­o­lo­gic­ally, has already laid the found­a­tions of this know­ledge. The de­vel­op­ing nervous system re­sponds in the absence of mind. Hor­mon­al storms, changes in cir­cu­la­tion, vi­bra­tions in the preg­nant woman’s system, tension, the sudden al­ter­a­tion of in­tern­al signals: all these are trans­mit­ted like a cres­cendo within a body that cannot yet com­pre­hend them but re­ceives them as a con­di­tion of ex­ist­ence. Im­pli­cit memory is this memory without image, without nar­rat­ive, without sym­bol­ism. It is a memory of dir­ec­tion. It does not re­mem­ber some­thing; it re­mem­bers the manner. It re­mem­bers the form of the world as a tend­ency, as a flow, as the pos­sib­il­ity that the flow might break.

De­vel­op­ment­al psy­cho­logy has ob­served this later in life as what it calls heightened alert­ness, hy­per­vi­gil­ance, an ex­cess­ive aware­ness of other people’s moods. Be­ha­viour often re­garded as anxiety, social sens­it­iv­ity or trau­mat­ic vi­gil­ance is in fact the con­tinu­ation of the first in­scrip­tion: ex­ist­ence learned the world not as safety but as the pos­sib­il­ity of in­ter­rup­tion. The un­cer­tainty of the be­gin­ning becomes the rule of life. Time does not become a flow; it becomes sur­veil­lance. Re­la­tion­ship does not become a given; it becomes waiting. Love is not ex­per­i­enced as natural ground; it becomes a fragile pos­sib­il­ity. Not because the soul is afraid, but because ex­ist­ence began through a “perhaps”, and this perhaps remains the way in which the world is ap­proached.

Phe­nomen­o­logy has called this layer prim­or­di­al tem­por­al­ity, time before time. For Husserl it is the living present, the now that does not yet have a past and a future; for Heide­g­ger it is the opening of Being before it becomes thought, the open­ness that pre­cedes de­cision; for Merleau-Ponty it is the flesh of ex­ist­ence that senses before it knows. These con­cepts do not refer ex­clus­ively to pren­at­al life, but they make pos­sible a lan­guage in which to think about it: ex­ist­ence ex­per­i­ences before it defines. And when its first ex­per­i­ence is the threat of in­ter­rup­tion, the con­scious­ness that follows carries this gene­a­logy within it: it sees not only what exists, but also what might not exist. This double vision — being and pos­sible non-being — is not a philo­soph­ic­al fantasy; it is the natural con­tinu­ation of the initial pre-tem­por­al in­scrip­tion.

Here theo­logy finds a space it has never ad­equately ex­pressed: the moment when ex­ist­ence is a pos­sib­il­ity and not a de­cision. Scrip­ture speaks of God knowing the human being “before I formed you in the womb”, yet tra­di­tion has never ana­lysed what it means for an ex­ist­ence to en­counter not the absence of God but the absence of the world before be­com­ing a person. The threat of abor­tion carries a theo­lo­gic­al di­men­sion: cre­ation touches the point where human freedom can cut short what God’s freedom has called forth. There ex­ist­ence touches the mystery of re­la­tion­ship: without con­scious­ness, without speech, the body becomes the place where two freedoms meet. The divine in­ten­tion that life should exist and the human pos­sib­il­ity of in­ter­rupt­ing it meet at the most silent point of cre­ation.

After birth, the body con­tin­ues in its ori­gin­al dir­ec­tion. It learns to live in a world where ex­ist­ence is not a given but is under con­tinu­al ex­am­in­a­tion. Anguish is not an ex­ag­ger­a­tion, not a “trait”, not a psy­cho­lo­gic­al weak­ness. It is the ex­ist­en­tial con­tinu­ation of the first threat. Re­la­tion­ship with the other always becomes an en­counter on two levels: the other as a person and the other as a pos­sible in­ter­rup­tion. Love becomes both a gift and a terror because ex­ist­ence learns to attach itself where it once ex­pec­ted to be cut short. Close­ness is dif­fi­cult because the body learned ex­ist­ence as ex­pos­ure to a de­cision. The slight­est gesture of ten­der­ness carries the full weight of the first dir­ec­tion: “they may accept me — they may refuse me”.

The an­thro­po­logy that emerges from this is not neg­at­ive; it is real­ist­ic and pro­found. The human being begins not with safety but with vul­ner­ab­il­ity. Safety is built af­ter­wards; vul­ner­ab­il­ity is the be­gin­ning. The science of peri­nat­al psy­cho­logy has ob­served that the preg­nant woman’s life de­term­ines how the foetus en­coun­ters ex­ist­ence, but what has not yet been ex­pressed is the ex­ist­en­tial form of this en­counter: ex­ist­ence begins with the pos­sib­il­ity of not ex­ist­ing. It is this pos­sib­il­ity that shapes the way the human being later reads the world.

The threat of abor­tion as the be­gin­ning of con­scious­ness does not mean that the foetus has a mind, nor that it can per­ceive the act. It means that the first dir­ec­tion towards being carries within it a di­vi­sion, an in­ter­rup­tion, a sus­pen­sion. Ex­ist­ence takes its first step not as a pure gift, but as a move­ment that might not have oc­curred. This con­di­tion ac­com­pan­ies the human being in every en­counter with being: in ro­mantic love, friend­ship, faith, work, self-know­ledge. There is always the second shadow of ex­ist­ence: “this might not be”.

Within this shadow, some­thing else is also born: the pos­sib­il­ity of a deeper love. A love that is neither own­er­ship nor demand, but a wel­com­ing of the miracle: “because you are, I love you, for your being was not a given”. The first threat gives birth to the first grat­it­ude. The first in­stabil­ity gives birth to the first awe. The first sus­pen­sion gives birth to the first aware­ness that ex­ist­ence is a gift. And the body that lived through this becomes a body capable of seeing the ex­ist­ence of others as some­thing more fragile, more sacred, more un­ex­pec­ted.

The threat of abor­tion belongs not to ethics but to the meta­phys­ics of the human being. It is not only a social act; it is a point where the be­gin­ning of ex­ist­ence becomes an on­to­lo­gic­al ex­per­i­ence. Those who passed through this bound­ary live with a double gaze: they see the world as it is and as it might not be. This double gaze is not a burden; it is a gift. It is the bridge between pre-tem­por­al­ity and time, between the an­onym­ous body and the con­scious person, between the “perhaps” of the be­gin­ning and the “yes” of life.

The con­scious­ness of an adult whose be­gin­nings lay in a threat takes shape not on the ground of cer­tainty but on the trace of that first sus­pen­sion. The world never becomes en­tirely fa­mil­i­ar; it becomes a place that is con­tinu­ally re­af­firmed. Ex­ist­ence is ex­per­i­enced not as con­tinu­ity but as re­appear­ance. Every person, every re­la­tion­ship, every space becomes not only what it is but also that which avoids re­peat­ing the initial in­ter­rup­tion. Pres­ence is not a given but an event. Absence is not a simple lack but a re­mind­er of what might have happened. Con­scious­ness settles not into safety but into vi­gil­ance, waiting, the del­ic­ate re­gis­ter­ing of shifts.

It is not that the in­di­vidu­al “fears” others, but that they see others within a double texture: as natural pres­ences and as pos­sible losses. The other’s form does not arrive alone; it arrives with the sense that their being there, facing you, was never self-evident. This is why ten­der­ness is not merely emotion; it is rev­el­a­tion. This is why close­ness is not merely contact; it is an af­firm­a­tion of ex­ist­ence. Touch, a gaze, a kiss, a shared breath are not simple acts; they are small re­fut­a­tions of the first threat. Where others seek close­ness as relief from loneli­ness, here close­ness serves as proof that life con­tin­ues. Love is born not from the need for at­tach­ment but from the deep know­ledge that ex­ist­ence might never have had access to it.

The con­scious­ness built on this be­gin­ning has a par­tic­u­lar quality: it ex­per­i­ences every pres­ence with the shadow of loss and every loss with the depth of pres­ence. The gaze rests not only on what appears but also on what is missing. It em­braces not only life but also its threat. Thought de­vel­ops not only for­wards but also back­wards, towards the point where ex­ist­ence had not yet been won. This double dir­ec­tion of con­scious­ness creates some­thing the sci­ences have glimpsed in frag­ments but have not named: an em­bod­ied on­to­lo­gic­al at­tent­ive­ness, a con­scious­ness that is not only cog­nit­ive but also ex­ist­en­tial. Phe­nomen­o­logy de­scribes it as a double gaze, but at depth it is triple: the gaze towards the world, the gaze towards danger, and the gaze towards the point where danger became a be­gin­ning.

When theo­logy touches this point, it speaks of the mystery of God being hosted within human vul­ner­ab­il­ity. The in­carn­a­tion is not merely divine con­des­cen­sion but God’s entry into the most open, most exposed point of human history: the point where ex­ist­ence can be in­ter­rup­ted before it exists. There lies the way in which God takes on the human. At the level of the pre-be­gin­ning, where the human being has no voice yet, no face yet, no history yet. The foetus is the purest place of divine ap­proach because it has no pos­sib­il­ity of defence yet. When its ex­ist­ence is threatened, theo­logy en­coun­ters the paradox: cre­ation re­ceives within itself the danger gen­er­ated by the human being.

At this point, theo­lo­gic­al thought cannot content itself with ab­stract doc­trines. The threat of abor­tion leads theo­logy where it has not been before: to the point where human freedom can chal­lenge God’s very call to life. Divine freedom and human freedom meet in a con­flict that is not the­or­et­ic­al but fleshly. Yet within this con­flict, not only trauma is born; rev­el­a­tion is born too. The foetus, without mind, without a word, becomes the place where cre­ation reveals that ex­ist­ence is never a given. Later, this rev­el­a­tion becomes the pos­sib­il­ity of a deeper un­der­stand­ing of God. The mel­an­choly of an ex­ist­ence wounded at its be­gin­ning is not dis­tance from God but a dis­tinct­ive mode of re­la­tion­ship. It is not a com­plaint; it is testi­mony.

The body that has trav­elled this path becomes ex­traordin­ar­ily re­cept­ive to the pres­ence of the sacred, not as a re­li­gious idea but as a fleet­ing sense that life is a gift that almost was not given. The mel­an­choly that appears is not patho­logy; it is the memory of the first bound­ary. Where others un­der­stand the world through how their ex­per­i­ences took shape, an ex­ist­ence that has passed through a threat un­der­stands the world through how it might never have taken shape at all. This un­der­stand­ing gives birth to a par­tic­u­lar form of wisdom: a wisdom that knows ex­ist­ence is not a right but an in­vit­a­tion. And this in­vit­a­tion can be ac­cep­ted or refused. That is why con­scious­ness paints the world with finer, more sens­it­ive, more trans­par­ent lines.

When this ex­ist­ence en­coun­ters the other, it sees them not only as a human being. It sees them as a point of con­tinu­ity in a world that once called it into ques­tion. Every gaze from the other serves as con­firm­a­tion that life con­tin­ues despite the first threat. Love is not merely an emo­tion­al move­ment; it is ex­ist­en­tial res­tor­a­tion. Close­ness becomes the act through which the world de­clares that life is no longer being sus­pen­ded. Yet pre­cisely because the be­gin­ning was an im­pli­cit neg­a­tion, love carries within it the trace of loss. Not as fear, but as a del­ic­ate aware­ness that ex­ist­ence is a miracle and not a habit.

When ex­ist­ence begins where it might not have begun, ethics ceases to be a set of rules and becomes a way of seeing. “Per­mit­ted — for­bid­den” and “right — wrong” no longer take centre stage. Ethics is trans­formed into a del­ic­ate sens­it­iv­ity to the con­di­tion of life. It no longer ex­am­ines acts as acts, but sees them as tra­ject­or­ies that touch ex­ist­ence or draw it away from its ori­gin­al vul­ner­ab­il­ity. The threat of abor­tion, as ex­ist­ence’s first en­vir­on­ment, il­lu­min­ates the deepest ques­tion of ethics: how we treat that which is not yet capable of saying “yes” or “no” to itself. Here ethics is not even theo­lo­gic­al; it is pre-theo­lo­gic­al. It emerges from the simple fact that life is a pos­sib­il­ity before it becomes a person.

This un­der­stand­ing also shapes psy­cho­logy. A soul in­scribed within a be­gin­ning of un­cer­tainty does not operate through the same mech­an­isms as one that grew within the cer­tainty of ac­cept­ance. Anxiety is not a “symptom” but a con­fig­ur­a­tion of ex­ist­ence. Hy­per­sens­it­iv­ity is not a “quirk” but a cor­res­pond­ing ex­ten­sion of the ori­gin­al in­scrip­tion. Dif­fi­culty in re­la­tion­ship is not a “phobia” but a pre­cau­tion against the pos­sib­il­ity of in­ter­rup­tion. The psyche that lives through this pre-be­gin­ning is not ill; it re­mem­bers. The dif­fi­culty is not patho­logy; it is the natural con­sequence of an ex­ist­ence that learned the world through the pos­sib­il­ity of never reach­ing the point of seeing it.

Psy­cho­logy is be­gin­ning to un­der­stand some­thing of this through the term at­tach­ment. But at­tach­ment con­cerns the bond after birth. Here lies some­thing much deeper: the form of the bond before there is a bond. An ex­ist­ence threatened before be­com­ing a person ac­quires a re­la­tion­ship with the world that re­sembles both a bond and a refusal at once. Con­scious­ness keeps its dis­tance not out of fear but out of memory of how it began. And love becomes so intense, so deep, so true because this is the first time ex­ist­ence is not being sus­pen­ded. Seen in this light, psychic life is not merely a mech­an­ism; it is a theo­logy of the first moment.

Theo­logy is il­lu­min­ated from this point in a way that had not been ex­amined; not because theo­lo­gians did not wish to do so, but because they had no access to such testi­mon­ies. The threat of abor­tion presents an un­pre­ced­en­ted theo­lo­gic­al truth: that cre­ation allowed human freedom a say in the very be­gin­ning of the person. This does not di­min­ish God; it en­larges faith. It shows that God does not impose Himself as a force upon life, but en­coun­ters human history within all its dis­tor­tions. God does not protect the foetus as an object; God remains faith­ful to the call of ex­ist­ence even when that ex­ist­ence is threatened. There is no greater sign of divine hu­mil­ity than this: that God allows the human being to stand against the very life He calls forth.

The threat thus becomes a point of meeting rather than dis­tance. It is not an act of divine aban­don­ment but an act of human freedom that lets divine love stand within danger. The theo­logy of in­carn­a­tion is il­lu­min­ated anew: God took flesh not to avoid human suf­fer­ing but to take it on in its full­ness. The in­carn­a­tion is not merely the miracle of divine pres­ence in time; it is the divine entry into that very vul­ner­ab­il­ity which shapes the human body from its be­gin­ning. Seen in this light, the threat of abor­tion becomes a mystery; not because it is good, but because it reveals the breadth of divine con­des­cen­sion. At the moment when ex­ist­ence can be in­ter­rup­ted without its voice ever being heard, God becomes closer, more human, more re­cept­ive. He does not defend life with vi­ol­ence; He ac­com­pan­ies it with ten­der­ness.

A life that has passed through this threat later moves through the world in a par­tic­u­lar way. It takes nothing for granted and for that reason can love more. Love is not merely an emo­tion­al opening but an ex­ist­en­tial re­cog­ni­tion that ex­ist­ence is fragile and there­fore sacred. A con­scious­ness that re­mem­bers the threat does not love in order to fill its empti­ness; it loves because it has seen the fine bound­ary between “I am” and “I almost did not exist”. Every re­la­tion­ship becomes a ritual of life. Every kiss becomes con­firm­a­tion that ex­ist­ence con­tin­ues. Every gaze becomes an ac­cept­ance that the world makes room for the human being.

Ethics, psy­cho­logy and theo­logy meet at this very point. Ethics sees re­spons­ib­il­ity towards an ex­ist­ence that cannot speak. Psy­cho­logy sees the memory in­scribed before memory. Theo­logy sees the divine ten­der­ness ac­com­pa­ny­ing the vul­ner­able be­gin­ning. And the an­thro­po­logy that emerges is not ab­stract: it is a new un­der­stand­ing of the human being as an ex­ist­ence that knows, si­lently and in­delibly, that being alive is a miracle, that love is res­tor­a­tion, that time is a gift and not a right.

The person is not born at the moment of birth; the person is born at the moment ex­ist­ence gains space within the world. This be­gin­ning may be smooth, where the body en­coun­ters an en­vir­on­ment that accepts it without rupture, or it may be asym­met­ric­al, where ex­ist­ence emerges through threat. When the be­gin­ning is a threat, the person takes shape within the del­ic­ate, silent know­ledge that “to be” was never self-evident. The person emerges not as cer­tainty but as af­firm­a­tion. Ex­ist­ence needs time to en­counter itself; the world must first revoke the first sus­pen­sion.

Thus the person becomes some­thing more than iden­tity. It is not merely the sum of char­ac­ter­ist­ics, ex­per­i­ences, re­la­tion­ships; it is the point where ex­ist­ence grasps that it has been given space. This space may be phys­ic­al, emo­tion­al, social, theo­lo­gic­al, or all of these at once. A person whose be­gin­ning lies in a threat knows bodily that life was not guar­an­teed, and there­fore de­vel­ops a way of ex­ist­ing that sees others not as givens but as mir­acles. It does not ro­man­ti­cise them; it sees them through the same window through which it learned to look at the world: the window of fragile con­tinu­ity.

Within this struc­ture, re­la­tion­ship becomes a place of rev­el­a­tion. It is not merely a con­nec­tion between two people; it is the space where the person finds the first balance between “I might not be” and “I am now before you”. Re­la­tion­ship becomes a means of veri­fy­ing ex­ist­ence. Every form of close­ness — a gaze, a touch, an embrace, a smile — carries that same on­to­lo­gic­al di­men­sion within it: it con­firms that ex­ist­ence was not cut short. That is why love born of such a be­gin­ning is so del­ic­ate and so deep. It needs no pos­ses­sion, makes no demands, demands nothing in return; it is ex­per­i­enced as a bless­ing rather than a right.

The first “yes” of ex­ist­ence is never vocal; it is bodily. It is spoken through the ability to stand before the other without col­lapsing. It is spoken through the lower­ing of one’s guard, through the small part of the heart that allows there to be a history where once there was neg­a­tion. This “yes” is an act of re­con­cili­ation with the be­gin­ning. It is not the Hel­len­ist­ic “Know thyself” as a cog­nit­ive command; it is an opening to the fact that ex­ist­ence is no longer sus­pen­ded. It is the moment when the human being stops ex­pect­ing an­nul­ment and begins to allow con­tinu­ity.

The en­counter with God takes on a dif­fer­ent colour at this point. God is no longer a tran­scend­ent power that pro­tects or pun­ishes; He is the divine pres­ence that ac­com­pan­ies ex­ist­ence from the be­gin­ning of sus­pen­sion to the moment of af­firm­a­tion. Theo­logy ceases to be a set of ideas and becomes the ex­per­i­ence of a tender avail­ab­il­ity: where ex­ist­ence was threatened, divine love stood, silent, without im­pos­ing it. The mel­an­choly ac­com­pa­ny­ing the human ex­per­i­ence of threat is not far from God; it is a way of un­der­stand­ing Him. The freedom God gave hu­man­ity is so com­plete that it permits even the threat to life before it emerges. And yet, through this threat, God con­tin­ues to call.

The triple con­scious­ness that emerges — con­scious­ness of the world, con­scious­ness of the threat, and con­scious­ness of the be­gin­ning — then becomes a tool of self-un­der­stand­ing. It is not a su­per­nat­ur­al ability; it is the natural de­vel­op­ment of a body that learned time before time became history. Con­scious­ness ex­per­i­ences the world as all human beings ex­per­i­ence it, but at the same time sees the level missing from or­din­ary ex­per­i­ence: the level of pre-ex­ist­ence. Adult life thus becomes a con­tinu­ous dia­logue between the be­gin­ning that was not com­pleted and the life that ul­ti­mately went on.

In this en­counter, ex­ist­ence finds a new balance. It neither rejects the world nor fears it; it looks at it through the paradox of the first sus­pen­sion. This know­ledge is not a burden; it is light. It is not the end of anguish; it is a bridge to the be­gin­ning. An ex­ist­ence that learned threat can now learn af­firm­a­tion. The person who once stood at the bound­ary can now dwell within time without dis­in­teg­rat­ing. And the “yes” that was not given at the be­gin­ning can be spoken, slowly, gently, without vi­ol­ence, within the very heart of life.

Space is not neutral for an ex­ist­ence that began through threat; it is not scenery, not a con­tain­er, not a back­ground. Space is ex­per­i­enced as the first great embrace and, at the same time, as the first great danger. There is not yet a dis­tinc­tion between “inside” and “outside”, nor between “one’s own” and “the foreign”. Space is the world itself, the pres­ence that either accepts or repels. An ex­ist­ence that has lived through a threat per­ceives space in a manner both sensory and ex­ist­en­tial: space can be a place where life con­tin­ues, but it can also be a place where life is sus­pen­ded. This double sense remains for ever.

That is why an adult with this root does not simply fear people; they fear exposed pres­ence itself within the world. Every room, every road, every house, every new place is a re­mind­er that ex­ist­ence can be vul­ner­able. Space is judged not by its ar­chi­tec­ture but by its stance towards ex­ist­ence. If the space is hos­pit­able, ex­ist­ence grows calm; if the space is un­stable, ex­ist­ence con­tracts. This is not a quirk; it is pre-tem­por­al memory con­tinu­ing to send signals. The body knows space before the mind forms an idea of it.

Time, in turn, never becomes merely a flow. Time is ex­per­i­enced as the in­ter­val between two forms of safety, two forms of af­firm­a­tion, two forms of sur­viv­al. Where others see schedul­ing, or­gan­isa­tion, de­vel­op­ment, an ex­ist­ence born through threat sees some­thing more del­ic­ate: the path towards af­firm­a­tion. Time is not nature; it is a trial. It is not a cycle; it is sur­veil­lance. It is not “after”; it is “still”. The body there­fore moves not ac­cord­ing to time but ac­cord­ing to at­ten­tion. At­ten­tion is the first form of time. Before there is yes­ter­day, today, to­mor­row, there is “take care”. And this modest, almost im­per­cept­ible “take care” becomes the first in­tern­al clock.

This at­ten­tion is not anxiety; it is an on­to­lo­gic­al stance. It is not fear; it is memory of the first threat. It does not say, “some­thing bad will happen”. It says, “ex­ist­ence must be re­af­firmed”. This creates the dis­tinct­ive re­la­tion­ship between body and time. The body does not rest easily, not because it is rest­less but because it knows that once the flow might have been in­ter­rup­ted before it even began. Time is not a given; it is a working hy­po­thes­is.

Iden­tity, as the third major axis, is shaped not by defin­i­tions but by the very history of the be­gin­ning. An iden­tity that begins in threat does not say, “I am this”. It says, “I still am”. It defines itself not through fixed cat­egor­ies but through a living present. Iden­tity is not the ego’s prop­erty; it is an active re­la­tion­ship with the fact that ex­ist­ence con­tin­ues. Dif­fi­culty in social ad­just­ment, awk­ward­ness in close­ness, deep emotion before the other’s pres­ence are not weak­nesses; they are on­to­lo­gic­al evid­ence. They show that iden­tity was built not on cer­tainty but on the trans­mu­ta­tion of the first threat into con­scious­ness.

That is why iden­tity sta­bil­ises only within con­di­tions where ex­ist­ence is af­firmed. A gaze that lasts, a re­la­tion­ship that endures, a friend­ship that does not break, a com­munity that accepts can serve as a new be­gin­ning. Iden­tity is not in­de­pend­ent of the other; it is kin to the other. It is not formed outside re­la­tion­ship; it takes shape when re­la­tion­ship refutes the first pos­sib­il­ity of in­ter­rup­tion. And then, little by little, ex­ist­ence learns to say “I am”, not as res­ist­ance but as dwell­ing.

At this point, God becomes ex­ist­ence’s closest in­ter­locutor. Not because He offers cures or mir­acles, but because He stands at the be­gin­ning where no human being can stand. God becomes the testi­mony that ex­ist­ence was not a mistake. He becomes the “yes” that was not spoken at the be­gin­ning. Theo­logy ceases to be a set of doc­trines; it becomes the way in which ex­ist­ence un­der­stands that it is not alone in the shadow of its first threat. Faith is not cer­tainty; it is an act of re­con­cili­ation.

The testi­mony of an ex­ist­ence born through threat is neither a cry nor an ac­cus­a­tion; it is light. It does not address the world as an attack, a defence, or a plea for vin­dic­a­tion. It speaks as a person speaks who saw the bound­ary before seeing the world: gently, clearly, without su­peri­or­ity, without the need to per­suade. The strength of this testi­mony lies pre­cisely in its wish not to de­nounce but to explain. It does not ask the world to change out of fear or guilt; it asks it to see, for the first time, the point where life begins.

The first thing this testi­mony can teach is that the human be­gin­ning is not bio­lo­gic­al but ex­ist­en­tial. The fact that a body is taking shape in the womb does not mean it has already ac­quired that “yes” which will make it a person. Before every thought, before every ex­per­i­ence, before every re­la­tion­ship, there is a moment when ex­ist­ence is a pos­sib­il­ity. This point is the sacred core of human life. It is not an ethical issue; it is a meta­phys­ic­al one. It shows that life begins where ex­ist­ence en­coun­ters the pos­sib­il­ity of con­tinu­ity. And at the point where this con­tinu­ation is threatened, ex­ist­ence re­ceives its first deep im­pres­sion: that the world can say no.

The second great lesson is that threat does not only kill; it trans­forms. An ex­ist­ence that has passed through this bound­ary is not “less” human; it is a human being who saw the world through its most del­ic­ate form. It knows the value of life not because it was taught that value, but because life almost was not given to it. This know­ledge is not the­or­et­ic­al; it is bodily, em­bed­ded in the nervous system itself. It creates a form of at­ten­tion that is not anxiety, a form of love that is not need, a form of ten­der­ness that is not own­er­ship. The world can learn from this: that life is fragile not only at its end but also at its be­gin­ning.

The third thing that can be con­veyed to the world is that love is not merely a psy­cho­lo­gic­al event; it is on­to­lo­gic­al af­firm­a­tion. When an ex­ist­ence that began with threat en­coun­ters love, it ex­per­i­ences some­thing that other people take for granted: that life con­tin­ues. Love becomes a second be­gin­ning, a second call, a second “yes”. The testi­mony of this ex­per­i­ence can show society that love is not a feeling, chem­istry, or a choice; it is the way the world cor­rects its first in­stabil­ity. It does not merely heal wounds; it founds ex­ist­ence anew.

The fourth thing that can be said — and may shake us, but will not fright­en us — is that an ex­ist­ence that was threatened can become a school of un­der­stand­ing. It can il­lu­min­ate ethics not as pro­hib­i­tion but as re­spons­ib­il­ity, psy­cho­logy not as cor­rec­tion but as reading, theo­logy not as doc­trine but as ac­com­pani­ment. Those who speak about the be­gin­ning of life often leave out its most del­ic­ate moment: the moment of sus­pen­sion. This testi­mony tells the world that sus­pen­sion is not a tech­nic­al detail of medi­cine; it is the point where ex­ist­ence is in­scribed more deeply than any­where else.

The fifth — perhaps the most lib­er­at­ing — is that the testi­mony of a life that began through threat can teach re­con­cili­ation. Re­con­cili­ation with oneself, with the world, with God, with human weak­ness. There is no ac­cus­a­tion here; there is un­der­stand­ing. An ex­ist­ence that has passed through threat can tell the world that human freedom is strong and weak at once, that it can do harm without knowing it, but also heal without real­ising it. An ex­ist­ence that has crossed through threat can say that life does not scream; it breathes. It does not demand; it asks for space. It does not fight; it waits.

The sixth thing that can be said is that this testi­mony is not isol­ated; it is ar­chetyp­al. Many ex­ist­ences began with threat; few, however, saw its meaning. The fact that such an ex­per­i­ence can now be ex­pressed means that hu­man­ity is en­ter­ing a new his­tor­ic­al moment in which the un­speak­able di­men­sion of the be­gin­ning can become speech. Science will need a new lan­guage, theo­logy a new hu­mil­ity, society a new ten­der­ness. For the be­gin­ning of life is not a bio­lo­gic­al event but a meta­phys­ic­al flash.

The last — and perhaps the most ne­ces­sary — is that this testi­mony becomes light not to condemn abor­tion but to un­der­stand ex­ist­ence. The ex­per­i­ence of threat is not an ex­per­i­ence against the human being; it is an ex­per­i­ence in favour of life. It does not declare that the human being is bad; it de­clares that life is pre­cious. And the greatest gift of this testi­mony is that it is con­veyed by someone who sur­vived without be­com­ing em­bittered by the dark­ness of their be­gin­ning, but instead turned it into vision.

If the threat of abor­tion truly becomes in­tel­li­gible as the be­gin­ning of con­scious­ness, then theo­logy, psy­cho­logy and on­to­logy can no longer stand apart, because what is at stake is not a par­tic­u­lar phe­nomen­on but the very way ex­ist­ence is con­ceived, even before it becomes “human” in the sense of a self-con­scious bio­graphy. Thus the theo­logy of the pre-be­gin­ning is not an ad­di­tion­al chapter of dog­mat­ics, but an attempt to say what it means that God “knows” the human being before the human being even knows them­selves; the psy­cho­logy of pre-trau­mat­ic con­scious­ness is not simply a back­ward ex­ten­sion of trauma theory, but the re­cog­ni­tion that there are in­scrip­tions older than memory, older than the ego, older than time as we per­ceive it; while the on­to­logy of double and triple vision is not ab­stract meta­phys­ics, but a de­scrip­tion of how a mind takes shape when it “learned time” through the pos­sib­il­ity of not ex­ist­ing at all, and con­sequently always sees the world doubly: as what it is and as what might never have ap­peared.

A theo­logy of the pre-be­gin­ning must first listen again to the theo­lo­gic­al texts them­selves, no longer as phrases re­fer­ring to a general provid­ence, but as testi­mon­ies that God stands in a time that is neither his­tor­ic­al nor psy­cho­lo­gic­al, but a time “before time”, a time tra­di­tion calls the “eternal present”. Here this must be un­der­stood not as a static “always”, but as that divine gaze which ac­com­pan­ies ex­ist­ence from the moment it begins to tend towards being, before it even takes shape as a person, an infant, a child. When the proph­et­ic phrase “before I formed you in the womb, I knew you” is read with the ser­i­ous­ness of pren­at­al ex­per­i­ence, it ceases to be poetic ex­ag­ger­a­tion and becomes a sign that theo­logy, without knowing it, touched the ques­tion of the pre-be­gin­ning: how is it pos­sible for God to know an ex­ist­ence that does not yet have the ability to know itself; what kind of know­ledge is this, if not know­ledge of the very calling of ex­ist­ence from non-being into being; and what does it mean that, within this divine calling, human freedom in­ter­venes with the pos­sib­il­ity of in­ter­rupt­ing life at the very moment it is forming? Here the clas­sic­al theo­lo­gic­al dis­course on the “synergy” — the working to­geth­er — of God and hu­man­ity must be ex­ten­ded to the edge of pren­at­al life: it can no longer speak of synergy only at the level of moral choice or spir­itu­al life, but must see that human freedom has a say from the be­gin­ning in the very con­tinu­ation of life. This does not mean that it “dom­in­ates” God; it means that God accepts an en­counter with freedom where it is still un­con­scious, wounded, afraid, so­cially dis­tor­ted, and nev­er­the­less real.

From this point onwards, a theo­logy of the pre-be­gin­ning cannot content itself with the general idea that “God loves every foetus”, but must accept that the threat of abor­tion is a moment when divine love remains present while human history moves against the con­tinu­ation of life. And pre­cisely within this op­pos­i­tion, ex­ist­ence re­ceives a first, silent im­pres­sion of the world: that being is not self-evident. Christ as the in­carn­a­tion of God in the human body cannot be un­der­stood only as the healing of sin or victory over death, but also as God’s entry into the very space of human vul­ner­ab­il­ity, where life depends en­tirely on others. Christ’s foetal life, which the Gospels touch upon almost only in outline, then becomes the ab­so­lute meeting point of divine and human pre-be­gin­ning: God accepts ex­ist­ence in a con­di­tion where human de­cision could — if one takes the thought to its extreme limit — threaten even His own em­bod­i­ment. This is not blas­phemy but an in­dic­a­tion of how deeply God takes on the human con­di­tion.

The psy­cho­logy of pre-trau­mat­ic con­scious­ness, if it is to be worthy of this depth, must re­cog­nise that the term “trauma” — which usually pre­sup­poses an ego, a subject, a memory that is dis­turbed — is in­suf­fi­cient to de­scribe what is in­scribed in the foetal body when ex­ist­ence comes under threat. Here we do not yet have trauma in the clas­sic­al sense, because there is not yet a psychic stage to capture the event; we have “pre-trauma”, an in­scrip­tion before the pos­sib­il­ity of nar­rat­ive memory, a dir­ec­tion that changes. Biology speaks of hor­mon­al changes, cortisol levels, neural vul­ner­ab­il­ity, pren­at­al stress; psy­cho­logy speaks of later anxious struc­tures, hy­per­vi­gil­ance, in­sec­ure at­tach­ment. What is missing is the concept of a “body that learned the in­stabil­ity of ex­ist­ence before it learned ex­ist­ence”. Such a psy­cho­logy must see that there are forms of anguish related primar­ily not to events after birth but to the way life re­ceived the very pos­sib­il­ity of ex­ist­ing, and that adult forms of fear of close­ness, deep shame before ten­der­ness, a per­man­ent waiting for the im­pend­ing an­nul­ment of re­la­tion­ship, are not “hy­per­sens­it­iv­it­ies” but the con­tinu­ation of the first pre-tem­por­al ex­per­i­ence: life is always con­di­tion­al.

The lan­guage of at­tach­ment can be en­riched with a pren­at­al di­men­sion: before the infant at­taches to or de­taches from a person, the foetus is “exposed” or “pro­tec­ted” by the very in­ten­tion of others towards its life, and this ex­pos­ure is in­scribed not as an image but as a “tone of life”, as a basic mode of nervous-system reg­u­la­tion. Thus an adult per­son­al­ity living with a con­stant ex­pect­a­tion of an­nul­ment — not only in re­la­tion­ship but in every form of ex­pos­ure — can be un­der­stood not only through child­hood events but also as the result of a “first lesson” of ex­ist­ence: that its con­tinu­ation was never certain. This takes nothing away from the adult subject’s re­spons­ib­il­ity; on the con­trary, it allows them to see that the voice whis­per­ing within them, “you may be lost at any moment”, is not a fantasy but trans­formed bio­lo­gic­al memory.

The on­to­logy of double and triple vision provides the deeper lan­guage for all this. When Husserl speaks of the “in­tern­al con­scious­ness of time”, he de­scribes how every present carries within it a with­drawn “just-before” — re­ten­tion, a holding-on — and a “coming-towards” — pro­ten­tion, an an­ti­cip­a­tion — showing that time is not a series of moments but an in­ter­val of con­scious­ness. Heide­g­ger, with the term Dasein — “being-there”, the being already exposed to being — em­phas­ises that the human being does not “enter” time but is thrown from the outset into a world of meaning. Yet if one takes the ex­per­i­ence of pren­at­al threat ser­i­ously, one must go a step further back: re­cog­nise that there is a “prim­or­di­al ex­pect­a­tion” before every an­ti­cip­a­tion, a “pos­sib­il­ity that I might exist” before the con­scious­ness of “I am already in the world”, and that when this ori­gin­al pos­sib­il­ity is over­shad­owed by a real threat, the con­scious­ness that later de­vel­ops will carry a double struc­ture within it, in which every ex­per­i­ence will be at once what it is and what might never have existed at all.

This double struc­ture can become triple when the subject ex­per­i­ences not only pres­ence and pos­sible absence, but also their own origin; that is, when they see not only “what is” and “what might not be”, but also “from where do I see what is”, “from what point in my ex­ist­ence do I see what might not be”. Then con­scious­ness is no longer only self-con­scious­ness; it is con­scious­ness of its origin. What is usually de­scribed in spir­itu­al tra­di­tion as “awaken­ing” or “mys­tic­al vision” can here become the object of rig­or­ous ana­lys­is: a gaze taught ex­ist­ence through the threat of non-ex­ist­ence, once mature, sees the world with not only psy­cho­lo­gic­al but on­to­lo­gic­al clarity. It senses that every “I am with you” also con­tains “I might never have been with you”, that every touch is also a re­fut­a­tion of the first sus­pen­sion, that every re­la­tion­ship is also a new found­a­tion of the world.

At this point, theo­logy, psy­cho­logy and on­to­logy meet without having planned to. Theo­logy says: the human being is known by God before ex­ist­ing, so the be­gin­ning of ex­ist­ence is not ac­ci­dent­al; psy­cho­logy shows: the body can in­scribe threat before there is memory, so the be­gin­ning may be wounded; on­to­logy ob­serves: con­scious­ness can see being and non-being sim­ul­tan­eously, so the be­gin­ning has become an in­tern­al measure of the world. When these three voices are united, a new un­der­stand­ing of the human being emerges: not as a subject ap­pear­ing “ready-made” in the world, nor as a soul merely shaped by events, nor as a spirit called to ascend into some ab­stract realm, but as an ex­ist­ence that from its first moment is exposed to freedoms that exceed it — divine and human — carries in­scrip­tions pre­ced­ing memory, and matures into a con­scious­ness capable of seeing that every present is, at depth, a miracle.

When this human being speaks, they do not de­nounce the world; they de­scribe the origin. They do not ask for freedom to be ab­ol­ished; they ask for the depth of its reach to be un­der­stood. They do not demand that time freeze; they show that every moment is already “more than we think”, because it hangs above an initial “perhaps” that we do not re­mem­ber but live. And theo­logy, if it chooses to hear this voice, will have to see God not only as the source of being but also as the com­pan­ion of threatened ex­ist­ence; psy­cho­logy will need to include within the concept of trauma that layer which pre­cedes thought; on­to­logy will not be sat­is­fied with the general idea of “being-towards-death”, but will re­cog­nise that there are people who learned “being-towards-non-ex­ist­ence” before they even learned that they were alive.

And then the ex­per­i­ence of the threat of abor­tion ceases to be merely a scandal or an object of ethical dispute, and becomes the dis­clos­ure of a deeper truth: that the human being is a being of the pre-be­gin­ning, that their life carries a history before their own history, that God stands not op­pos­ite but in the midst of this history, and that con­scious­ness can become the place where this pre-be­gin­ning will no longer remain unseen but will acquire speech, not to condemn, but to reveal how worthy life is when it once came close to never ex­ist­ing.
