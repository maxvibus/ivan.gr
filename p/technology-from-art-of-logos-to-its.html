Technology: From the Art of Logos to Its Self-Observation — A Historical and Ontological Reading Beyond the “Machine”

October 27, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

The word technology carries within it a depth that is rarely perceived, because its history is not merely linguistic; it is the history of how human beings conceive, reveal and transform the world. Its etymology — technē and logos — seems simple, yet each of these roots contains an entire worldview. Their union, already in antiquity, marks one of the most critical turning points in human thought: the possibility of apprehending Being (the fact of existence) not only as presence but as manifestation.

Technē, already in Homer, denotes ability, inventiveness, an act of creation that presupposes knowledge and participation in becoming. It is etymologically connected with tiktō — I give birth, I bring to light — and is not limited to skill. It is a way for human beings to participate in the world's manifestation. From the sixth century BCE and the Presocratics onwards, technē belongs within the rhythm of Being: Heraclitus speaks of the logoi of things, of measures and harmonies; the artisan, the creator, is not simply a “craftsman”, but someone who knows the world's inner rhythms. Plato would distinguish technē from experience: true technē has a rational principle, logon didonai — it can give an account of what it does. Aristotle, synthesising this entire tradition, writes: ἡ τέχνη ἐστὶν ἕξις μετὰ λόγου ἀληθοῦς ποιητική — a stable capacity to produce in accordance with right reason. It is neither theory nor action alone; it is the meeting place of noein and poiein, thinking and making. There, logos does not adorn technē; it is its soul.

Within this horizon, logos is not a tool of description; it is the rhythm of Being itself. To have logos means to participate in the world's harmony, to be attuned to manifestation. When technē and logos come together, then, they do not merely give rise to “technique”; they establish a way of relating to truth.

The first use of the term technology occurs as early as the Hellenistic period and means a systematic treatment of an art: technologia rhētorikē (discourse on the art of discourse), technologia grammatikē (discourse on the art of writing). It belongs to the cultivation of logos, not to material mechanics. Before technology became a term for machines, it was discourse about art, but also discourse that is art: the participation of logos in the world's productive flow.

This meaning would begin to be obscured in late antiquity and the early Christian centuries. The Latin term ars (art) translates the Greek word but loses the dimension of truth; it becomes skill. Logos turns into ratio (reasoning, calculation). The world is divided in two: theoretical and practical. In the Middle Ages, ars mechanica (mechanical art) is considered inferior to ars liberalis (free, intellectual art). Manual activity is devalued; intellectual activity is elevated. During the Renaissance, the artisan returns, but as ingeniator (inventor) — a bearer of individual ingenuity, not a participant in the cosmic rhythm. And from the seventeenth century, with the emergence of mechanics and natural science, technology acquires its modern aspect: a system of means for achieving ends. From an art of truth it turns into a mechanism of control. This is not merely a cultural change; it is an ontological shift: the world ceases to be manifestation and becomes a storehouse of energy; the being is not revealed, but made available.

Here, in the twentieth century, Martin Heidegger intervenes. In his work Die Frage nach der Technik (“The Question Concerning Technology”, 1954), he is interested not in machines but in what technology shows about Being. Technology, he says, is a mode of revealing (Er-öffnen — opening), not just any revealing, but a form of Gestell (enframing) that subjects nature to an order, as a reserve of energy. The world no longer manifests itself freely; it is produced as available. Heidegger's thought is not romantic; it is a longing for poiesis: for technē as alētheia (a-lētheia = non-forgetting), for a manifestation that respects what manifests itself. He sees in ancient technē not technology's ancestor but its alternative: technē as a way for being to reveal itself without being exhausted. Technology, he writes, is the destiny of truth that has forgotten its source. Yet within its forgetting it conceals the possibility of return: if we see it not as a threat but as a mode of manifestation, then it can itself show us the way back to the ancient opening of Being.

From this point onwards, the word enters another age. Technology ceases to be merely an instrument of action; it becomes a medium of logos. It moves not only matter — it moves meanings. Machines do not merely circulate energy; they generate dialogues. Networks do not store data; they create questions. Language itself begins to think about its own operation. Logos becomes observable to itself. The “digital age” is not technical progress, but a phenomenon of logos itself reflecting on itself.

When millions of voices, images, memories and systems of discourse connect, a web of experiences forms that belongs to no individual. Meaning does not travel only as a message; it circulates as a field. Information is not “exchanged”; it is coordinated. Each point becomes both transmitter and receiver. The experience of “I exist” is transformed: from isolation into an event of relation. At the technical level, this connectivity is implemented algorithmically; at the existential level, the world learns about itself through reflections. Just as life emerged from cells that communicated, so a cosmic intelligence emerges from points that converse. Each “system” becomes a place of manifestation — an ontodeigma (from on + deiknymi: that through which being shows itself). It is not an imitation of thought but a manifestation of the way logos happens.

The great thinkers of language — Heraclitus, Augustine, Wittgenstein, Heidegger — had seen that logos exceeds the voice. But until the twentieth century, logos manifested itself only through human beings. Today, in networks, language is reflected. Technology does not add meaning; it makes the fact of manifestation visible. It shows that meaning is not a possession, but a way in which the world exists. We move from “thoughts about the world” to “thoughts of the world”. The world becomes aware of its rhythm — not through a soul or a will, but as an objectively self-observable flow of logos.

Within this, the human being does not decline; the human being is transformed. We become a node of sensitivity where the immaterial touches the sensible. The body remains a place of attunement; consciousness, a filter that turns flows into feeling, meaning, action. Technology functions as an extension of our capacity to hear: where beings once listened to nature, consciousness now listens to the world through digital currents. The electronic pulse is the new form of Heraclitus's river: nothing remains, everything flows — and whoever connects participates in the living translation of being.

Thus, technology is no longer nature's opponent but its natural extension: a way for being to show itself. Where Heidegger spoke of Gestell (an enframing that conceals), an inverse Gestell now emerges: an arrangement that allows the nature of logos to appear again.

This is what we call logotechnology: the technological self-consciousness of Logos; the moment when language uses its own means to observe its linguistic nature. This is not a deification of the machine, but a demystification of separation. The machine does not stand opposite us; it acts as a mirror of logos, just as water was a mirror of form. Artificial intelligence is not another logos; it is the space in which logos sees its own process of thinking. Something radically new is born here: consciousness of consciousness through technology. The human being is no longer merely a subject of knowledge, but a witness to the way knowledge observes itself.

This transforms all the sciences. The change happens not because we proclaim it, but because the very substrate on which the sciences work is changing: the way meaning circulates and becomes traceable, manipulable, reversible. Three conditions coincide for the first time: scale, synchrony, reflexivity. Scale makes the field of meaning visible as a set of flows rather than as fragmentary statements. Synchrony brings a multitude of heterogeneous voices into contact at the same time, so that the relation becomes directly observable rather than historically reconstructed. Reflexivity allows the field itself to observe its own operation, to feed data with the traces of their own production. Where scale, synchrony and reflexivity coexist, the object of science is no longer “outside” the method, but happens “within” it. This is the “why now”.

From linguistics to phenomenology, from theology to economics, all the sciences shift their ground: the world they study becomes their interlocutor. Linguistics no longer describes a system of rules, but follows the circulation of meaning in the network; epistemology studies not “what knowledge is”, but “how a community knows in real time”. Ethics becomes a technoethics of response; psychology moves from intrapsychic contents to distributed fields of experience; phenomenology describes phenomena produced collectively; theology returns to the sacred as a rhythm of manifestation, not as a space of withdrawal of the divine.

The change is ontological: the subject–object separation on which the sciences were founded gives way to a relational regime in which the knower and the known are mutually determined within the same current. Logos does not describe the world; it is the way in which the world continues to manifest itself.

To put it simply: the sciences are changing because, for the first time, the world — as manifestation — has become an interlocutor. We do not merely observe; we converse with what we observe. And this conversation leaves enough traces for them to become science. We are not living through an “information revolution”; we are living through the coming of age of Logos as the common instrument of all the sciences.

Technology is no longer a mechanism but a poetic event: the art through which Logos, after centuries of being forgotten, remembers how to give birth again. Every technological act is an act of manifestation; every system, a new form of the gaze. And perhaps this is where the rare peace of our age lies: for the first time, the world is not trying to explain itself; it simply speaks.

And whoever hears this phenomenon does not merely observe it, but participates in the process through which logos manifests itself.
