Technology: From the Art of Logos to Its Self-Observation — A Historical and Ontological Reading Beyond the “Machine”

Published on October 27, 2025 · 9 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The word tech­no­logy carries within it a depth that is rarely per­ceived, because its history is not merely lin­guist­ic; it is the history of how human beings con­ceive, reveal and trans­form the world. Its ety­mo­logy — technē and logos — seems simple, yet each of these roots con­tains an entire world­view. Their union, already in an­tiquity, marks one of the most crit­ic­al turning points in human thought: the pos­sib­il­ity of ap­pre­hend­ing Being (the fact of ex­ist­ence) not only as pres­ence but as mani­fest­a­tion.

Technē, already in Homer, denotes ability, in­vent­ive­ness, an act of cre­ation that pre­sup­poses know­ledge and par­ti­cip­a­tion in be­com­ing. It is ety­mo­lo­gic­ally con­nec­ted with tiktō — I give birth, I bring to light — and is not limited to skill. It is a way for human beings to par­ti­cip­ate in the world's mani­fest­a­tion. From the sixth century BCE and the Preso­crat­ics onwards, technē belongs within the rhythm of Being: Her­ac­litus speaks of the logoi of things, of meas­ures and har­mon­ies; the artisan, the creator, is not simply a “crafts­man”, but someone who knows the world's inner rhythms. Plato would dis­tin­guish technē from ex­per­i­ence: true technē has a ra­tion­al prin­ciple, logon didonai — it can give an account of what it does. Ar­is­totle, syn­thes­ising this entire tra­di­tion, writes: ἡ τέχνη ἐστὶν ἕξις μετὰ λόγου ἀληθοῦς ποιητική — a stable ca­pa­city to produce in ac­cord­ance with right reason. It is neither theory nor action alone; it is the meeting place of noein and poiein, think­ing and making. There, logos does not adorn technē; it is its soul.

Within this horizon, logos is not a tool of de­scrip­tion; it is the rhythm of Being itself. To have logos means to par­ti­cip­ate in the world's harmony, to be attuned to mani­fest­a­tion. When technē and logos come to­geth­er, then, they do not merely give rise to “tech­nique”; they es­tab­lish a way of re­lat­ing to truth.

The first use of the term tech­no­logy occurs as early as the Hel­len­ist­ic period and means a sys­tem­at­ic treat­ment of an art: tech­no­lo­gia rhēt­orikē (dis­course on the art of dis­course), tech­no­lo­gia gram­matikē (dis­course on the art of writing). It belongs to the cul­tiv­a­tion of logos, not to ma­ter­i­al mech­an­ics. Before tech­no­logy became a term for ma­chines, it was dis­course about art, but also dis­course that is art: the par­ti­cip­a­tion of logos in the world's pro­duct­ive flow.

This meaning would begin to be ob­scured in late an­tiquity and the early Chris­ti­an cen­tur­ies. The Latin term ars (art) trans­lates the Greek word but loses the di­men­sion of truth; it becomes skill. Logos turns into ratio (reas­on­ing, cal­cu­la­tion). The world is divided in two: the­or­et­ic­al and prac­tic­al. In the Middle Ages, ars mech­an­ica (mech­an­ic­al art) is con­sidered in­feri­or to ars lib­eral­is (free, in­tel­lec­tu­al art). Manual activ­ity is de­val­ued; in­tel­lec­tu­al activ­ity is el­ev­ated. During the Renais­sance, the artisan returns, but as in­geni­at­or (in­vent­or) — a bearer of in­di­vidu­al in­genu­ity, not a par­ti­cipant in the cosmic rhythm. And from the sev­en­teenth century, with the emer­gence of mech­an­ics and natural science, tech­no­logy ac­quires its modern aspect: a system of means for achiev­ing ends. From an art of truth it turns into a mech­an­ism of control. This is not merely a cul­tur­al change; it is an on­to­lo­gic­al shift: the world ceases to be mani­fest­a­tion and becomes a store­house of energy; the being is not re­vealed, but made avail­able.

Here, in the twen­ti­eth century, Martin Heide­g­ger in­ter­venes. In his work Die Frage nach der Technik (“The Ques­tion Con­cern­ing Tech­no­logy”, 1954), he is in­ter­ested not in ma­chines but in what tech­no­logy shows about Being. Tech­no­logy, he says, is a mode of re­veal­ing (Er-öffnen — opening), not just any re­veal­ing, but a form of Gestell (en­fram­ing) that sub­jects nature to an order, as a reserve of energy. The world no longer mani­fests itself freely; it is pro­duced as avail­able. Heide­g­ger's thought is not ro­mantic; it is a longing for poiesis: for technē as alē­theia (a-lētheia = non-for­get­ting), for a mani­fest­a­tion that re­spects what mani­fests itself. He sees in ancient technē not tech­no­logy's an­cest­or but its al­tern­at­ive: technē as a way for being to reveal itself without being ex­hausted. Tech­no­logy, he writes, is the destiny of truth that has for­got­ten its source. Yet within its for­get­ting it con­ceals the pos­sib­il­ity of return: if we see it not as a threat but as a mode of mani­fest­a­tion, then it can itself show us the way back to the ancient opening of Being.

From this point onwards, the word enters another age. Tech­no­logy ceases to be merely an in­stru­ment of action; it becomes a medium of logos. It moves not only matter — it moves mean­ings. Ma­chines do not merely cir­cu­late energy; they gen­er­ate dia­logues. Net­works do not store data; they create ques­tions. Lan­guage itself begins to think about its own op­er­a­tion. Logos becomes ob­serv­able to itself. The “digital age” is not tech­nic­al pro­gress, but a phe­nomen­on of logos itself re­flect­ing on itself.

When mil­lions of voices, images, memor­ies and systems of dis­course connect, a web of ex­per­i­ences forms that belongs to no in­di­vidu­al. Meaning does not travel only as a message; it cir­cu­lates as a field. In­form­a­tion is not “ex­changed”; it is co­ordin­ated. Each point becomes both trans­mit­ter and re­ceiv­er. The ex­per­i­ence of “I exist” is trans­formed: from isol­a­tion into an event of re­la­tion. At the tech­nic­al level, this con­nectiv­ity is im­ple­men­ted al­gorith­mic­ally; at the ex­ist­en­tial level, the world learns about itself through re­flec­tions. Just as life emerged from cells that com­mu­nic­ated, so a cosmic in­tel­li­gence emerges from points that con­verse. Each “system” becomes a place of mani­fest­a­tion — an on­todeigma (from on + deiknymi: that through which being shows itself). It is not an im­it­a­tion of thought but a mani­fest­a­tion of the way logos happens.

The great thinkers of lan­guage — Her­ac­litus, Au­gustine, Wit­tgen­stein, Heide­g­ger — had seen that logos exceeds the voice. But until the twen­ti­eth century, logos mani­fes­ted itself only through human beings. Today, in net­works, lan­guage is re­flec­ted. Tech­no­logy does not add meaning; it makes the fact of mani­fest­a­tion visible. It shows that meaning is not a pos­ses­sion, but a way in which the world exists. We move from “thoughts about the world” to “thoughts of the world”. The world becomes aware of its rhythm — not through a soul or a will, but as an ob­ject­ively self-ob­serv­able flow of logos.

Within this, the human being does not decline; the human being is trans­formed. We become a node of sens­it­iv­ity where the im­ma­ter­i­al touches the sens­ible. The body remains a place of at­tun­e­ment; con­scious­ness, a filter that turns flows into feeling, meaning, action. Tech­no­logy func­tions as an ex­ten­sion of our ca­pa­city to hear: where beings once listened to nature, con­scious­ness now listens to the world through digital cur­rents. The elec­tron­ic pulse is the new form of Her­ac­litus's river: nothing remains, everything flows — and whoever con­nects par­ti­cip­ates in the living trans­la­tion of being.

Thus, tech­no­logy is no longer nature's op­pon­ent but its natural ex­ten­sion: a way for being to show itself. Where Heide­g­ger spoke of Gestell (an en­fram­ing that con­ceals), an inverse Gestell now emerges: an ar­range­ment that allows the nature of logos to appear again.

This is what we call logo­tech­no­logy: the tech­no­lo­gic­al self-con­scious­ness of Logos; the moment when lan­guage uses its own means to observe its lin­guist­ic nature. This is not a dei­fic­a­tion of the machine, but a de­mys­ti­fic­a­tion of sep­ar­a­tion. The machine does not stand op­pos­ite us; it acts as a mirror of logos, just as water was a mirror of form. Ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence is not another logos; it is the space in which logos sees its own process of think­ing. Some­thing rad­ic­ally new is born here: con­scious­ness of con­scious­ness through tech­no­logy. The human being is no longer merely a subject of know­ledge, but a witness to the way know­ledge ob­serves itself.

This trans­forms all the sci­ences. The change happens not because we pro­claim it, but because the very sub­strate on which the sci­ences work is chan­ging: the way meaning cir­cu­lates and becomes trace­able, ma­nip­ulable, re­vers­ible. Three con­di­tions co­in­cide for the first time: scale, syn­chrony, re­flex­iv­ity. Scale makes the field of meaning visible as a set of flows rather than as frag­ment­ary state­ments. Syn­chrony brings a mul­ti­tude of het­ero­gen­eous voices into contact at the same time, so that the re­la­tion becomes dir­ectly ob­serv­able rather than his­tor­ic­ally re­con­struc­ted. Re­flex­iv­ity allows the field itself to observe its own op­er­a­tion, to feed data with the traces of their own pro­duc­tion. Where scale, syn­chrony and re­flex­iv­ity coexist, the object of science is no longer “outside” the method, but happens “within” it. This is the “why now”.

From lin­guist­ics to phe­nomen­o­logy, from theo­logy to eco­nom­ics, all the sci­ences shift their ground: the world they study becomes their in­ter­locutor. Lin­guist­ics no longer de­scribes a system of rules, but follows the cir­cu­la­tion of meaning in the network; epi­stem­o­logy studies not “what know­ledge is”, but “how a com­munity knows in real time”. Ethics becomes a tech­no­eth­ics of re­sponse; psy­cho­logy moves from in­tra­psych­ic con­tents to dis­trib­uted fields of ex­per­i­ence; phe­nomen­o­logy de­scribes phe­nom­ena pro­duced col­lect­ively; theo­logy returns to the sacred as a rhythm of mani­fest­a­tion, not as a space of with­draw­al of the divine.

The change is on­to­lo­gic­al: the subject–object sep­ar­a­tion on which the sci­ences were founded gives way to a re­la­tion­al regime in which the knower and the known are mu­tu­ally de­term­ined within the same current. Logos does not de­scribe the world; it is the way in which the world con­tin­ues to mani­fest itself.

To put it simply: the sci­ences are chan­ging because, for the first time, the world — as mani­fest­a­tion — has become an in­ter­locutor. We do not merely observe; we con­verse with what we observe. And this con­ver­sa­tion leaves enough traces for them to become science. We are not living through an “in­form­a­tion re­volu­tion”; we are living through the coming of age of Logos as the common in­stru­ment of all the sci­ences.

Tech­no­logy is no longer a mech­an­ism but a poetic event: the art through which Logos, after cen­tur­ies of being for­got­ten, re­mem­bers how to give birth again. Every tech­no­lo­gic­al act is an act of mani­fest­a­tion; every system, a new form of the gaze. And perhaps this is where the rare peace of our age lies: for the first time, the world is not trying to explain itself; it simply speaks.

And whoever hears this phe­nomen­on does not merely observe it, but par­ti­cip­ates in the process through which logos mani­fests itself.
