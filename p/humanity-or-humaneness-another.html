Humanity or Humaneness? Another Universality of the Greek Language?

Published on March 1, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The Greek word “an­thropia” (hu­mane­ness) carries within it a sense of close­ness, empathy, fa­mili­ar­ity and com­pas­sion. It is not an ab­stract or neutral concept; it is some­thing lived, ex­pressed through one’s way of life. In Greek lan­guage and thought, an­thropia is not merely a quality but a way of being, a state of ex­ist­ence that reveals the deep bond between the human person and the sur­round­ing world.

As for the word “culture”, its root does indeed go back to the Latin word cultura, which ori­gin­ally meant the cul­tiv­a­tion of the land. In Latin, colere means “to cul­tiv­ate”, but also “to honour”, “to pre­serve” and “to inhabit”. From this basic meaning, the concept of culture de­veloped and ex­pan­ded to en­com­pass the “cul­tiv­a­tion” of the mind and spirit, as well as a com­munity’s ways of be­hav­ing. Over time, culture became syn­onym­ous not only with the cul­tiv­a­tion of nature, but also with in­tel­lec­tu­al, artist­ic and moral de­vel­op­ment.

The word “Hellas”—and its ac­cept­ance or re­jec­tion—is rooted in the very dy­nam­ics of history, lan­guage and cul­tur­al iden­tity. “Hellas” carries the ancient Greek idea of the land of light and know­ledge, yet its use was often dis­placed by “Greece” because of foreign in­flu­ences and geo­pol­it­ic­al changes. Some Greeks avoid “Hellas” because they feel it bears an archaic or strongly sym­bol­ic res­on­ance that does not accord with con­tem­por­ary reality. Others fear that using “Hellas” may provoke re­ac­tions or mis­un­der­stand­ings in­ter­na­tion­ally. Yet the deeper essence of the name remains: it is a name that bears the light of the Greek spirit, clarity and wisdom.

With regard to Di­mitris S. Ath­anasopoulos and his book “Towards a Polit­ics of Culture”, it is im­port­ant to re­cog­nise that his work did not seek an easy or su­per­fi­cial defin­i­tion of culture. On the con­trary, it invited readers to think more deeply, to ask them­selves about the true di­men­sions of cul­tur­al life, and to dis­tin­guish between cul­tur­al action and cul­tur­al con­sump­tion. Culture, as he un­der­stood it, was not simply a series of events or artist­ic activ­it­ies, but a living way of think­ing, living and en­ter­ing into dia­logue with the world.

In this context, using the word “an­thropia” (hu­mane­ness) rather than “an­thropinotita” (hu­man­ity) strengthens the direct con­nec­tion with Greek reality and the truth of the lan­guage. An­thropia, as un­der­stood in the Greek tra­di­tion, is more per­son­al, more humane and closer to every­day life. By con­trast, “an­thropinotita” sounds more the­or­et­ic­al, more foreign to the natural char­ac­ter of Greek ex­pres­sion. Let us there­fore ap­proach an­thropia not merely as a concept, but as a call to live more truth­fully, more con­sciously and more openly within our au­then­t­ic re­la­tion­ships with ourselves, others and the world. In this way, we also honour Di­mitris S. Ath­anasopoulos by re­cog­nising his vision of a culture that is not merely cul­tiv­a­tion, but a living in­tel­lec­tu­al and moral stance.
