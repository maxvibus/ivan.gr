Humanity or Humaneness? Another Universality of the Greek Language?

March 1, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

The Greek word “anthropia” (humaneness) carries within it a sense of closeness, empathy, familiarity and compassion. It is not an abstract or neutral concept; it is something lived, expressed through one’s way of life. In Greek language and thought, anthropia is not merely a quality but a way of being, a state of existence that reveals the deep bond between the human person and the surrounding world.

As for the word “culture”, its root does indeed go back to the Latin word cultura, which originally meant the cultivation of the land. In Latin, colere means “to cultivate”, but also “to honour”, “to preserve” and “to inhabit”. From this basic meaning, the concept of culture developed and expanded to encompass the “cultivation” of the mind and spirit, as well as a community’s ways of behaving. Over time, culture became synonymous not only with the cultivation of nature, but also with intellectual, artistic and moral development.

The word “Hellas”—and its acceptance or rejection—is rooted in the very dynamics of history, language and cultural identity. “Hellas” carries the ancient Greek idea of the land of light and knowledge, yet its use was often displaced by “Greece” because of foreign influences and geopolitical changes. Some Greeks avoid “Hellas” because they feel it bears an archaic or strongly symbolic resonance that does not accord with contemporary reality. Others fear that using “Hellas” may provoke reactions or misunderstandings internationally. Yet the deeper essence of the name remains: it is a name that bears the light of the Greek spirit, clarity and wisdom.

With regard to Dimitris S. Athanasopoulos and his book “Towards a Politics of Culture”, it is important to recognise that his work did not seek an easy or superficial definition of culture. On the contrary, it invited readers to think more deeply, to ask themselves about the true dimensions of cultural life, and to distinguish between cultural action and cultural consumption. Culture, as he understood it, was not simply a series of events or artistic activities, but a living way of thinking, living and entering into dialogue with the world.

In this context, using the word “anthropia” (humaneness) rather than “anthropinotita” (humanity) strengthens the direct connection with Greek reality and the truth of the language. Anthropia, as understood in the Greek tradition, is more personal, more humane and closer to everyday life. By contrast, “anthropinotita” sounds more theoretical, more foreign to the natural character of Greek expression. Let us therefore approach anthropia not merely as a concept, but as a call to live more truthfully, more consciously and more openly within our authentic relationships with ourselves, others and the world. In this way, we also honour Dimitris S. Athanasopoulos by recognising his vision of a culture that is not merely cultivation, but a living intellectual and moral stance.
