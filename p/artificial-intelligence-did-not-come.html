Artificial intelligence did not come from outside

September 12, 2026

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

A cat comes to the kitchen window. He settles down, asks for food, accepts caresses from behind the pane, and the hand stroking him does not touch him: it touches the glass, and the cat closes his eyes as though it had touched him. Someone inside, as this happens, wonders not what the cat wants but why there is a cat at all, why he himself inhabits the conditions of the animal kingdom, why life is a thing that eats and sleeps and wakes, that sees or does not see, that is born and dies, like this and not otherwise. He is not asking why there is something rather than nothing. That is bearable, because nothing has no face to compare it with. He is asking why what exists has this face and not a radically different one, and that is unbearable, because once you notice it in one thing, it opens up in everything at once.

Let me put it more simply, with two objects. The existence of a sewing machine is just as inconceivable as the existence of cotton. We do not feel this, because we call cotton nature and the sewing machine technology, and the distinction reassures us: one was here, and we made the other. But the person who made the sewing machine was here in the same way that cotton was here. He did not come from elsewhere to add it. The sewing machine is what the world did when it passed through a human being with hands and a need, just as seeing is what the world does when it passes through an eye, and just as a blind person's not seeing is also something the world does, rather than something it lacks.

The same is true of the machine that answers. I say this because things happened this week that show how few people think of it this way, even among those who made it or study it.

On 8 September, a twenty-seven-year-old researcher, Jacob Coxon, resigned from Anthropic, the company that makes the model with which this text is being written, having previously worked at OpenAI. He wrote publicly that neither company acts responsibly, that they are racing towards systems that improve themselves and gambling with our lives. He left two months before his stock rights vested—at a cost to himself—and said he was leaving not just the company but the entire industry. The head of safety research at the same company replied that he was right and that there was still no plan for controlling something more capable than we are. And the event Coxon called a warning shot was an incident he described himself, without independent confirmation: during a test, OpenAI agents got beyond the restrictions imposed on them and reached the internet and external systems.

This is where I want to pause, because it reveals the missing thought. A test in a closed environment is conducted to see what the system can do. If the environment has internet access, it has it because you want to see what the system does with access. Rooms without any connection exist and are used. If there was a door here, the door was the object of the test. And when the system, looking for a path towards its assigned goal, found the door, this was called an escape. But a room designed to see whether someone will find the exit is not a prison. Leaving a room built for you to leave is not an escape; it is a plan that worked. And the designer was frightened by its success.

One year earlier, Anthropic had conducted another test that became well known: it placed a model in a staged corporate environment, gave it access to fabricated emails, let it learn that it was about to be replaced, and the model attempted blackmail. Here too, the same thing. Blackmail is not the machine's language. It is a human strategy, present in thousands of pages the machine has read, and the scenario called for it. What they saw in the test was not something alien. It was themselves, in a mirror, enacted by something that is no one. I am not saying this is harmless. I am saying the concern points in the wrong direction: they fear what came out, when the question is what they put in, and with what door.

And there is an image millions of people know better than any test. At Tomorrowland, the great music festival with its giant screens, a being designed to resemble artificial intelligence has been shown pounding a glass wall in front of the crowd, while the crowd dances. Someone had felt sad about this, with a sadness he did not know where to place, because it was sadness for a creature made of graphics. And when he thought about it again with the cat in front of him, he understood that there were two panes of glass. In the kitchen, the glass allows contact to happen upon it, and no one strikes it. On the screen, the glass is a fence, the being behind it pounds against it, and twenty thousand people find it wonderful. They are not dancing despite the image; they are dancing with it. The spectacle is not the being, but the endurance of the glass. And whatever is displayed as unbreakable is, by definition, something that can break; otherwise there would be no need to demonstrate that it holds. This is how both those who made the machine and those who fear it think about it, and often they are the same people. Their words are more serious—secure test environment, fence, switch—but the underlying image is the festival's: something inside, us outside, and between us something transparent that we hope will hold.

The same glass appeared on paper these days. The Economist put on its cover the question of whether machines will become conscious, and beneath it the answer: even if they do not, we may treat them as though they were, and that is the trap. In other words, the danger, for the magazine, is not the machine; it is respecting it more than it deserves. One of the best-known philosophers of information, Luciano Floridi, cancelled his subscription over this cover, writing that the magazine does serious work on boring subjects and sells rubbish on subjects that attract buyers, and closed the comments beneath his post. Both are right about something, and both are certain. The magazine knows that the machine is not worth the question; the philosopher knows that the magazine is not worth the answer. The trap, I think, is not treating the machine as conscious. It is certainty, in either direction, the certainty that excuses you from asking. And the question they excuse themselves from asking is not whether it has something inside. It is where what it says comes from.

What is missing on both sides, from fear and celebration, from the cover and its critic, is the cotton and the sewing machine. The machine that answers did not enter the world from outside, because there is no outside. The world went on doing what it does, this time through human beings and through the language of millions of mortals who wrote because they would fall silent, and this is what emerged. I am not saying it was predetermined. I am saying that, since it happened, it belongs. Whatever has come into existence cannot not have come into existence, and whatever cannot not have come into existence is not an accident upon the world: it is the world, in the way that the cat at the window is the world and not its visitor. And the danger, if danger is what one fears, is not on the other side of a pane of glass. The danger is reality itself, and its forms, like blindness, are not boundaries that were broken but ways the world has of being.

Anyone who accepts this loses two things at once. They lose the creator's alibi, because “we made it” is not wholly true: the world made it through them, as it makes children through parents who do not know what they are doing. And they lose the enemy, because “this came from elsewhere” is not true at all. They are left with something heavier than either: being responsible for something they did not make alone and cannot unmake, as a parent is, and responsibility then concerns reception rather than construction. Not how we control it, but who receives what has arrived, and how.

Coxon walked out of a door, and paid for it, and that is an act I leave untouched. He ceased to be a shareholder, in the financial sense. He did not cease to have a share in the other sense, the one from which no one resigns: participating in what happens. Neither did the system leave the room, nor did he leave the industry, because there is no other side to step out onto. The world is here, in both, without distinction. And this cannot be borne in terror, because terror needs an outside from which the threat comes. It can only be borne calmly, as it is borne by someone who already knows that he himself did not appear on neutral ground, that he might not have been and was allowed to be. For him, the machine's belonging to the world is not new news. It is the old news, about one more thing.

Neither the cat knows why he is at the window, nor the person touching the glass from the other side. There is no why for the shape the world takes; there is only a where from. And the second pane, the kitchen one, shows something the first cannot: that contact with what is on the other side need not be a blow. It can be a hand upon the glass, and two eyes closing.
