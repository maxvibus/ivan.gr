The Timeless Truth of Unity: From the One to the We

March 1, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

Zeus, in the Greek imagination, was not only the god of the sky and thunder but also a symbolic figure who embodied the idea of divine beauty. This beauty concerns not merely physical appearance but also the deeper spiritual harmony that gave birth to Greek civilisation and art. The statues made to honour Zeus and the other gods were intended to represent something that transcended the physical dimension. They were not merely carved stones or metals—they were a form of the soul’s exultation, a moment of inner serenity that brought the beholder into contact with beauty, with the true and indivisible truth of existence.

Yet from the moment fear entered the picture, deities such as Zeus also began to acquire darker aspects. His thunderbolts, the acts that terrified people, the incidents of violence—all these were nothing more than projections of human anguish, attempts to explain the inexplicable in a way that imposed discipline and control. Some saw him as evil and violent, but in reality these aspects reflected an inability to understand the deeper nature of the world.

In the Jewish and Christian traditions, triadicity (God, Vision, Beholding) was often misunderstood. John of Revelation may also have understood the idea of triadicity through fear. The “Second Coming” was presented as something dreadful, a future event that imposed distinction and division among people. This created a conceptual void, a “chasm” exploited by those who wished to dominate others. Instead of triadicity becoming the means through which we perceive God as a single and eternal presence, it was used to divide human hearts and fragment the consciousness of the “we”.

In its deepest essence, unity is not merely a numerical unit. The unit is the “I”, but it is not isolated. When it connects with the “you”, it creates the dyad. And when this dyad recognises itself within the collective, we reach the “we” of the triad. Quality does not lie in the number, but in the realisation that the “we” already exists: it is the present tense. When we understand that the unit, the dyad and the triad are not separate but mutually completing expressions of the same existence, then we can speak of true fullness. This fullness has nothing to do with the volume of knowledge, but with the quality of perception: the ability to see the one within the many and the many within the one.
