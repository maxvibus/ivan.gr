The Timeless Truth of Unity: From the One to the We

Published on March 1, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Zeus, in the Greek ima­gin­a­tion, was not only the god of the sky and thunder but also a sym­bol­ic figure who em­bod­ied the idea of divine beauty. This beauty con­cerns not merely phys­ic­al ap­pear­ance but also the deeper spir­itu­al harmony that gave birth to Greek civil­isa­tion and art. The statues made to honour Zeus and the other gods were in­ten­ded to rep­res­ent some­thing that tran­scen­ded the phys­ic­al di­men­sion. They were not merely carved stones or metals—they were a form of the soul’s ex­ulta­tion, a moment of inner serenity that brought the be­hold­er into contact with beauty, with the true and in­di­vis­ible truth of ex­ist­ence.

Yet from the moment fear entered the picture, deities such as Zeus also began to acquire darker aspects. His thun­der­bolts, the acts that ter­ri­fied people, the in­cid­ents of vi­ol­ence—all these were nothing more than pro­jec­tions of human anguish, at­tempts to explain the in­ex­plic­able in a way that imposed dis­cip­line and control. Some saw him as evil and violent, but in reality these aspects re­flec­ted an in­ab­il­ity to un­der­stand the deeper nature of the world.

In the Jewish and Chris­ti­an tra­di­tions, tri­adi­city (God, Vision, Be­hold­ing) was often mis­un­der­stood. John of Rev­el­a­tion may also have un­der­stood the idea of tri­adi­city through fear. The “Second Coming” was presen­ted as some­thing dread­ful, a future event that imposed dis­tinc­tion and di­vi­sion among people. This created a con­cep­tu­al void, a “chasm” ex­ploited by those who wished to dom­in­ate others. Instead of tri­adi­city be­com­ing the means through which we per­ceive God as a single and eternal pres­ence, it was used to divide human hearts and frag­ment the con­scious­ness of the “we”.

In its deepest essence, unity is not merely a nu­mer­ic­al unit. The unit is the “I”, but it is not isol­ated. When it con­nects with the “you”, it creates the dyad. And when this dyad re­cog­nises itself within the col­lect­ive, we reach the “we” of the triad. Quality does not lie in the number, but in the real­isa­tion that the “we” already exists: it is the present tense. When we un­der­stand that the unit, the dyad and the triad are not sep­ar­ate but mu­tu­ally com­plet­ing ex­pres­sions of the same ex­ist­ence, then we can speak of true full­ness. This full­ness has nothing to do with the volume of know­ledge, but with the quality of per­cep­tion: the ability to see the one within the many and the many within the one.
