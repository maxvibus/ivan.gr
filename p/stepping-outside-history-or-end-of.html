Stepping outside History, or the End of History?

March 19, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

History has always been more than a record of events. It has been the very substance of our consciousness, personality, society, even our theology. To be within history is to live within a narrative fabric in which each event acquires meaning through its connection to other events. But what happens when a person, or an entire society, realises that they have reached the limits of the very concept of “history”? What does it mean to step outside history?

From a philosophical perspective, the end of history is not the end of events, but the end of the way we perceive life as a succession of events. It is an exit from the idea that everything we experience must fit into an overarching narrative, a goal or an ultimate meaning. Hegel and later Fukuyama spoke of such an end—the end of the dialectical movement towards a final truth, the point at which History fulfils its purpose and reaches completion. Yet in reality, what was called “the end of history” is simply the beginning of the realisation that History has never existed as anything other than a narrative.

Sociologically, stepping outside history is a moment of liberation and, at the same time, radical uncertainty. Societies are built upon the idea of history—they need beginnings, origins, roots and purposes. But what happens when a society awakens from the need to define its existence through past events? Identity then changes from historical to experiential, from narrative to existential. Such a society would have to exist on the basis not of “who we were”, but of “who we are now”. This means a radical shift in the way we relate to the world and to others.

On a personal level, stepping outside history may be the deepest transformation possible. Personality is usually formed around stories we tell ourselves about who we are, where we come from and where we are going. But what happens when you stop believing that your life must have a narrative? You then experience the moment as wholly present, without the constant need to connect it to the past or the future. Personality becomes less historical and more existential. It stops seeking meaning in the before and after, and lives within the pure, undivided present.

Theologically, the end of history signals something deeper than any eschatological expectation. History, as a theological concept, has often been used as a means of control through the promise of a future that will vindicate or condemn. But what happens when one realises that the divine does not arrive at the end of history, but is always present, already here, in the eternal now? God then becomes not the end of the narrative, but the end of every need for narrative. Theologically, stepping outside history is entering presence, encountering what was always there but that we could not see because we looked only at the temporal framework.

Ultimately, stepping outside history does not mean abandoning life, but seeing it for the first time without the filters of narrative. Recognising it in its absolute, timeless presence, as something that needs no meaning in order to be true.
