Intentionality, “society”, and the technique of the word

Published on February 22, 2026 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Rarely, almost im­per­cept­ibly, a person some­times ceases to ex­per­i­ence the world merely as a given and begins to ex­per­i­ence it as a ques­tion: what is natural, and what has been ar­ranged so that it can no longer be dis­tin­guished from the natural and is ex­per­i­enced not merely as natural, but as ne­ces­sary.

When this ques­tion arises, it reveals that the “ne­ces­sary” is not always a prop­erty of things, but often the result of an earlier act that re­mained unseen. Before the ma­nip­u­la­tion of mean­ings, there is some­thing more fun­da­ment­al: a dir­ec­tion of the will. An in­ten­tion­al­ity, if the word fits here, that is not yet rhet­or­ic and not yet an in­sti­tu­tion; it is the ele­ment­ary move to make the other person pre­dict­able, avail­able, meas­ur­able, and there­fore man­age­able. Power does not begin with the word “power”. It begins where the other person’s freedom ceases to be tol­er­ated as an in­de­term­in­ate pos­sib­il­ity and is turned into order. This de­cision may be clothed in a thou­sand pre­texts — se­cur­ity, pro­gress, unity, prosper­ity, sal­va­tion — but the pattern remains: the few de­term­in­ing the form of life of the many.

What is his­tor­ic­ally para­dox­ic­al is not that there are strong and weak people; dif­fer­ences in strength also exist in nature. The human paradox is that the power­ful are not sat­is­fied with strength. They want some­thing higher: they want le­git­im­acy. They want im­pos­i­tion to appear not as im­pos­i­tion, but as “the way things are”. This is where the tech­nique of dis­course is born. Vi­ol­ence re­quires pres­ence. Le­git­im­a­tion allows absence. Once people are per­suaded, the one im­pos­ing power can be absent and yet remain in force.

For this le­git­im­a­tion to work, its object must first be created. And here enters the word you iden­ti­fied: “society”. “Society” is not a natural being like a moun­tain or the sea. It is a name that makes the mul­ti­tude appear to be a single thing. In reality, the mul­ti­tude con­sists of count­less lives, count­less stories, count­less ob­jec­tions. Yet the word “society” com­presses them into one image: a body. And as soon as a body appears, some­thing else can appear too: the “in­terests of the body”, the “health of the body”, the “danger to the body”. So treat­ment can appear. So a manager can appear. So someone “en­titled” to speak on behalf of the body can appear.

Thus ma­nip­u­la­tion does not begin with a lie. It begins with com­pres­sion. With re­pla­cing mul­ti­pli­city with a name that carries au­thor­ity. Power does not ini­tially need to tell you some­thing untrue. It needs to give you a way of think­ing that makes the outcome in­ev­it­able. To make you see the mul­ti­tude as “society”, dis­cip­line as “order”, ob­jec­tion as “threat”, dis­obedi­ence as “vi­ol­a­tion”, fear as “realism”. Then, once these are es­tab­lished, the rest of the ma­chinery works almost by itself.

This is the point where words acquire an au­thor­it­ari­an char­ac­ter: not because they are harsh, but because they appear neutral. The most power­ful im­pos­i­tion is the one that looks not like a command but like a de­scrip­tion. “Society needs…”, “reality demands…”, “the law stip­u­lates…”, “it is natural…”, “what is normal is…”. All these phrases conceal the same move: they remove re­spons­ib­il­ity from the person im­pos­ing some­thing and trans­fer it to an ab­stract entity — society, reality, nature, nor­mal­ity. This is how power dis­guises itself: it does not command, “I want”. It de­clares, “This is how it is”.

Why did the few his­tor­ic­ally come to dom­in­ate the many? There is no single cause. There is a com­bin­a­tion of con­ver­ging tend­en­cies. First, the need for co­ordin­a­tion: as people grow in number, life re­quires or­gan­isa­tion, and or­gan­isa­tion pro­duces roles. Then, ac­cu­mu­la­tion: whoever con­trols re­sources con­trols time, and whoever con­trols time gains an ad­vant­age over the needs of others. Next, the psy­cho­lo­gic­al pleas­ure of se­cur­ity and su­peri­or­ity: dom­in­a­tion offers a sense of im­mor­tal­ity within time, as though you could sta­bil­ise the world so that it would no longer threaten you. And finally, tech­nique: once it is dis­covered that you can govern not only with weapons but with defin­i­tions, im­pos­i­tion becomes subtler, more lasting, “civ­il­ised”. Power matures when it un­der­stands that the best obed­i­ence is not imposed, but arises within the subject as reason.

This is what is out­rageous: power is not content to re­strict you. It wants to shape you so that the re­stric­tion seems reas­on­able to you. It wants to train you to utter, of your own accord, the words that bind you. To identi­fy with the “we” it fash­ioned in order to govern you, to fear the “de­vi­ation” it named in order to bring you back into line, to accept the “reality” it con­struc­ted in order to close off your horizon.

And yet the crack exists: the moment when you see that “society” is a name, that the “normal” is a measure, that the “natural” is a trans­fer of au­thor­ity, that “must” is a tech­nique for turning choice into ne­ces­sity. At that point you are not tearing down the world; you are tearing down the dis­guise. And strip­ping away the dis­guise is perhaps the first real act of freedom within a world that pre­sup­posed you without asking you.
