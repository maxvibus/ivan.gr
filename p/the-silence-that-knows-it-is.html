The silence that knows it is

Published on May 6, 2026 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

There is a silence that is neither a lack of sound nor an absence of speech, and perhaps, if you pay at­ten­tion to it for a moment, you will see that it is neither some­thing that appears when the noise around you stops nor the result of a still­ness you have managed to attain through effort, but some­thing that exists in­de­pend­ently of what moves within and around you, like a found­a­tion that no one laid and that nev­er­the­less stands.

When you en­counter it, if you en­counter it, you notice some­thing strange, some­thing hard to put into words because words want to make it an object, and it is not an object but a pres­ence that expects nothing of you, does not ask you to address it, prom­ises no answers, does not offer itself as a place where you will hear a voice or receive a rev­el­a­tion, and yet it is there, steady in a way that moving things are not, quiet without seeking its quiet­ness, deep without needing to display its depth.

Our re­la­tion­ship with it is usually awkward, and perhaps this says some­thing im­port­ant about the way we have learned to live, because we pass it by without no­ti­cing when it appears fleet­ingly between two thoughts, we rush to fill the empti­ness we imagine it to be, we equate it with loneli­ness because we know no other way of being present without speak­ing or listen­ing, and so, when it appears within us, we im­me­di­ately look for some­thing to think or some­thing to say so that the awk­ward­ness will go away, as though we were afraid of what would be re­vealed if we let it stay.

But this silence, if you allow your­self to remain with it for a while without im­pos­ing a shape on it or asking it to give you some­thing, begins to reveal a quality that does not fit fa­mil­i­ar cat­egor­ies, because it is not pleas­ant in the way that en­ter­tain­ment or sat­is­fac­tion is pleas­ant, nor un­pleas­ant in the way that depriva­tion or grief is un­pleas­ant, but stands some­where beyond these dis­tinc­tions, with a kind of ser­i­ous­ness that is not heavy and a kind of light­ness that is not frivolity.

When you begin to re­cog­nize its pres­ence, you may notice some­thing even subtler: your whole life is con­stantly moving, thoughts come and go, emo­tions flare up and subside, desires kindle and die out, plans take shape and fall apart, and amid all this move­ment the silence does not move, does not come and go, does not depend on what happens on the surface, but is present as the very con­di­tion in which everything that moves can appear.

It is not divine in the sense that re­li­gion would have you see it, it is not mys­tic­al in the sense that tra­di­tions of spir­itu­al seeking would de­scribe it, it is not a higher state avail­able only to a few, but the simplest of all the things you can en­counter, and perhaps that is also why it is the hardest to re­cog­nize, because we are always looking for some­thing im­port­ant, some­thing great, some­thing that vin­dic­ates the search, and silence has none of these things to offer you.

When you stop in­sist­ing on some­thing, it appears of its own accord, and this is perhaps its strangest feature: it cannot be at­tained through effort; on the con­trary, effort drives it away, and it does not appear to those who seek it in­tensely but to those who have stopped seeking it, as though it were sens­it­ive to the weight of our desire and with­drew when we asked for it, re­turn­ing when the search ceased, not out of some stub­born­ness of its own, but because perhaps the search itself was what con­cealed its pres­ence.

This does not mean that you should stop search­ing, nor that the search was a mistake, but perhaps at some point, in the midst of search­ing, if it happens, you re­cog­nize that what you are looking for was there from the be­gin­ning, that you needed to do nothing for it to appear, that all the work of search­ing was perhaps what pre­ven­ted the en­counter, and when the obstacle ceases, not because you force it to cease but because it has ex­hausted itself, the silence that was always there becomes ap­par­ent as though for the first time.

You do not need to learn any­thing, you do not need to follow anyone, you do not need to prac­tise tech­niques, and this is perhaps the greatest relief this silence can offer you: it demands nothing of you, sets no con­di­tions, expects no achieve­ments, and the only en­counter that can happen with it is when, at some moment, without knowing why or how, you find your­self stand­ing without in­sist­ing on any­thing, and it is there, as it has always been.
