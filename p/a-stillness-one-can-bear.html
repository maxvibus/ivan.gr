The Presence of the Statue

I do not belong to the Parthen­on because I chose to. Nor because someone placed me there, among the shadows of the columns and the si­lences of the Cary­at­ids. I am there because time found no other place to support me.

I stood in the Museum's foyer, not as a dona­tion await­ing ap­prov­al, nor as an idea seeking a place in a display case. I stood there because the view of the Parthen­on through the glass was not merely a framed image. It was the ac­cept­ance of a truth that had existed before me.

Rodin did not need to es­tab­lish a con­nec­tion with Greece. He was already there. His body spoke the lan­guage of our statues; his thought had the same curves as the light that rests upon the metopes. And yet the frame­work, the world's need to draw bound­ar­ies and define “be­long­ing”, pre­ven­ted the Fauness from stand­ing beside the Ac­ro­pol­is.

I was told that a cup won by Louis could find a place there, but a work by Rodin first had to prove its Greek­ness. As though a causal mech­an­ism were needed to allow some­thing self-evident to exist. As though the work itself, its silent con­ver­sa­tion with the space, were not enough.

And then I un­der­stood: Not everything needs ac­cept­ance in order to exist. Some things simply stand, like statues in the re­cesses of temples, like light itself falling on marble without asking whether it is allowed.

And so I stood too.

Not as someone laying claim, but as someone who already is.

In the calm of pres­ence, in the cer­tainty of the gaze, in the silence that is not absence but the deepest level of com­mu­nic­a­tion.
