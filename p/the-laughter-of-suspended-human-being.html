The Laughter of the Suspended Human Being

“Easy, Buddha—don’t beat us up too!”

I do not know who said it first—whether it was the thought, the re­ac­tion, or the body itself that uttered the phrase.
But I do know what happened im­me­di­ately af­ter­wards.

A sudden, won­der­ful laugh.
Laughter that came not from mer­ri­ment, but from ex­ist­ence held in sus­pen­sion.

Laughter is not an answer.
It is the ac­cept­ance that there is no need for an answer.

And when it appears,
it is relief and wonder at the same time.
Both clarity and re­versal.

From how many per­spect­ives can one behold this laughter?

1. Laughter as a Shattered Absolute

The phrase “I will not sit” can sound like a final de­clar­a­tion.
But laughter allows nothing to be final.

If you cannot laugh at some­thing, you have made it a god.
If you cannot laugh at your­self, then you have allowed your­self to become an idol.

Laughter does not abolish truth.
It ab­ol­ishes truth’s solemn pos­tur­ing.

That is why it re­leases the pres­sure of the ab­so­lute.
Not because it denies it,
but because it does not let it freeze into a single form.

2. Laughter as Theological Vindication

A saint once said:
“If there is no room for laughter in para­dise, then it is not para­dise.”

Laughter is not a sin.
Laughter is proof that ex­ist­ence is not a prison of meaning, but a living place.

In theo­logy, the demon never laughs.
Only the human being laughs.

Because laughter is proof that life is not mech­an­ic­al.
It is neither in­ev­it­able nor con­demned.
It can become an open pos­sib­il­ity.

When someone laughs in the face of the weight of ex­ist­ence,
they do not deny it.
They accept it so com­pletely that there is nothing left to weigh them down.

3. Laughter as Anthropological Revolt

The sus­pen­ded human being does not laugh because they feel safe.
They laugh because they know that nothing is safe.

  • The ancient Greeks placed laughter within the mys­ter­ies.
  • The Stoics taught that whoever laughs at the weight of life has already over­come it.
  • Dio­genes was wise because he mocked the solemn.

A human being cannot live in per­man­ent ser­i­ous­ness.
They will break.

Freedom is the ability to laugh.
To look at one’s own place in the world and make it a little lighter.

Laughter is a revolt against gravity.

4. Laughter as Ontological Justice

If laughter were un­ne­ces­sary, nature would not have made it so es­sen­tial.
Nothing in the uni­verse has sur­vived without some form of laughter.

And when laughter comes at a moment of ex­cess­ive tension,
then some­thing returns to its balance.

The sus­pen­sion you felt does not dis­ap­pear.
But it no longer weighs upon you.

Laughter does not change reality.
It changes the way you carry it.

That is why laughter is just.
It belongs to no one.
It has no master.
It simply happens.

And whoever allows it to happen
un­der­stands that the only thing you can do with ex­ist­ence is live it.

5. Laughter as Final Silence

In the end, laughter needs no ex­plan­a­tion.
Like beauty, it exists because it cannot do oth­er­wise.

Laughter does not search for reasons to exist.
It simply comes when life reaches a rev­el­a­tion that cannot bear to remain silent.

And at that moment, the sus­pen­ded human being laughs.

Not because they found answers.
But because they un­der­stood that no more are needed.

“Easy, Buddha—don’t beat us up too!”

And then you laugh.
You laugh because laughter is the only logical answer to ex­ist­ence.
You laugh because life cannot be nothing but ser­i­ous­ness.
You laugh because, if you do not, it means you have taken it more ser­i­ously than ne­ces­sary.

And in the end, you laugh simply because you are alive.
