Divinity without deity

Published on April 19, 2026 · 5 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

There is a ques­tion that anyone who has seen the struc­ture of suf­fer­ing in the world is even­tu­ally com­pelled to ask. Why the world is like this. Why life feeds on life. Why animals tear other animals apart. Why an infant can suffer for no reason. Why there are camps, orphan­ages, fenced-in slaughter­houses where cows stand in line while ma­chines draw their milk.

Tra­di­tion­al theo­logy answers these ques­tions with frame­works that do not with­stand careful scru­tiny. That suf­fer­ing is the price of human freedom. That suf­fer­ing teaches us. That suf­fer­ing is a mystery beyond our un­der­stand­ing. None of these answers ex­plains suf­fer­ing where human freedom is not in­volved — in the animal kingdom, in infants who die before be­com­ing con­scious, in the very struc­ture of evol­u­tion, which op­er­ates through the death of the de­feated.

I would be ashamed of a god who chose to create such a world. This shame is not atheism. It is the right moral re­sponse to an image of god that theo­logy itself has fash­ioned and no one has cor­rec­ted. If god is om­ni­po­tent and chose this world, then the judg­ment against him is jus­ti­fied. If the answer is “we do not un­der­stand his reasons,” then that answer aban­dons the very po­s­i­tion it is de­fend­ing.

There is, however, another pos­sib­il­ity, one that has not been stated clearly enough.

God may not be what we ex­pec­ted. Not because he does not exist. Because he is not a saviour in the sense ima­gined by hu­man­ity’s child­like ima­gin­a­tion. His refusal to act as a saviour is neither aban­don­ment nor weak­ness nor malice. It is an act of trust. He leaves us room. He does not in­ter­vene to force us to become what we ourselves must become. His with­draw­al is the con­di­tion of our coming of age.

From this per­spect­ive, what we would expect of him is not removed from the world — it is trans­ferred to us. Love, justice, care, con­sol­a­tion — everything we would want god to do for us and for others — becomes our own work. Not because god is absent. Because divine nature itself mani­fests as a gradual trans­fer of pos­sib­il­ity to the human being.

A dis­tinc­tion is needed here that is usually not made. Between deity and di­vin­ity. Deity is a status — I am a god, I control, I decide. When human beings thought they could possess deity, the result was Aus­chwitz, the camps, every form of ex­term­in­at­ory ideo­logy. Deity seized as a pos­ses­sion leads to abuse.

Di­vin­ity is some­thing else. It is not a status you acquire. It is a way of being you take on. You act di­vinely not because you are a god, but because you re­cog­nize that your very ex­ist­ence carries a pos­sib­il­ity that exceeds your mere bio­lo­gic­al func­tion­ing. Di­vin­ity is not taken away from god — it passes to you as a pos­sib­il­ity for action.

How do we know what a divine act is. We do not need rev­el­a­tion to know it. Each of us knows what hurts us. We there­fore also know what does not hurt us. And this know­ledge gives us direct access to what hurts and does not hurt another. Our pain is the cri­terion. You do not need a theory to re­cog­nize that an ant being crushed suffers — you know it because you know what it means to be crushed.

The divine act un­der­taken by a human being is not a triumph. It does not fill the person per­form­ing it with a sense of victory or moral su­peri­or­ity. It con­tains a mel­an­choly that does not go away. This mel­an­choly is not per­son­al fatigue. It is the re­cog­ni­tion that the world has been made in such a way that someone needs to act di­vinely, and this need is itself a burden. You act di­vinely because the eco­sys­tem is not divine. If it were, you would not need to do it your­self. Your act pre­sup­poses a lack in the world. And the act does not make good that lack — it reduces it, but does not change the struc­ture that pro­duced it.

Mel­an­choly is the memory of “there should be no need” within “I act.” What I am doing is right, but the world that forced me to do it is not right. This duality — a right act, a wrong con­di­tion — keeps mel­an­choly alive even at the moment of action.

And perhaps this very mel­an­choly is what god would feel if he became ex­pli­citly aware of his own po­s­i­tion. For he too acts — or allows things to be done — within a world whose very nature in­cludes suf­fer­ing. His unease before himself is no dif­fer­ent from the human being’s unease before the need to act di­vinely in a world where di­vine­ness is not guar­an­teed by its struc­ture.

This shared mel­an­choly is the point at which the re­la­tion­ship between human being and god ceases to be hier­arch­ic­al. It is no longer god above and the human being below. Both are in the same dif­fi­culty, on dif­fer­ent scales. God in his own ab­stract po­s­i­tion before himself. The human being in their own con­crete po­s­i­tion before the world. Yet the move­ment is shared. They share a burden that neither chose but that both must bear.

What tra­di­tion­al dis­course would call sal­va­tion takes another form here. You are not saved because someone comes to save you. You become the one who could save, on the small scale that falls to you. You do not believe in an om­ni­po­tent god who will set things right. You become a fellow trav­el­ler with a god who also bears the un­bear­able burden of being or be­com­ing — we do not know which — god.

Faith here is meas­ured not by a de­clar­a­tion but by the act of taking re­spons­ib­il­ity. Whoever takes on the good they would hope for from god puts divine nature into prac­tice, re­gard­less of what they believe about his ex­ist­ence. Whoever invokes god without taking re­spons­ib­il­ity betrays what they invoke.

The freedom born here is neither sev­er­ance nor revolt. It is trav­el­ling to­geth­er. A re­la­tion­ship with some­thing that you do not know exists in the form you once ima­gined, but move­ment towards it makes you what you had hoped it would be. The journey itself becomes the place where god and the human being meet — not as master and slave, but as two beings sharing the same un­bear­able burden.
