Adam and Eve. Is Testimony a Sin? Convenient, or All Too Convenient?

March 19, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

The narrative of Adam and Eve did not appear suddenly. It was not an isolated event, but a narrative that developed gradually, through various traditions, codifications and reinterpretations.

Let us look at when and how it appeared in codified form:

1. The Earliest Precedents

The story of Adam and Eve was not unique to the Hebrews. There were earlier narratives that were incorporated and reworked.

  • The Mesopotamian Tradition

    • The Epic of Gilgamesh (3000–2000 BCE) contains a narrative resembling “Paradise”: the hero Gilgamesh seeks immortality but fails because of a fateful choice that makes him mortal.
    • The story of Enkidu (Gilgamesh’s friend) is almost identical to Adam’s fall: he was innocent and lived among the animals, until he became human through a woman and was cut off from nature.

  • The Sumerian Myth of Nintur

    • Nintur was a female fertility deity who, in one version, gives life to the first human, but is later punished for revealing “too much knowledge” to human beings.

These traditions predated the Hebrew Genesis and show that the narrative was not original, but was reconstructed and incorporated into Judaism.

2. The First Codified Hebrew Version (Genesis, 6th–5th Centuries BCE)

The story of Adam and Eve as we know it today first appeared in the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Bible), specifically in Genesis.

  • This narrative was composed during the Babylonian captivity of the Hebrews (6th century BCE).
  • It incorporated earlier traditions, but emphasised obedience to God and guilt.
  • Unlike in the earlier myths, Eve is neither a goddess nor an independent figure, but is created from Adam.
  • The fruit of knowledge becomes forbidden, establishing the concept of sin as transgression.

3. Developments in the Hellenistic Period (3rd–1st Centuries BCE)

When Judaism came into contact with Greek thought, a new phase of codification and interpretation began:

  • Philo of Alexandria (1st century BCE), a Jewish philosopher influenced by Plato,
    turns the story of Adam and Eve into a philosophical, allegorical myth.
  • Adam symbolises the rational soul,
    while Eve represents the sensory soul that is led astray.
  • This interpretation would later influence Christianity.

4. The Role of Christianity: The Definitive Establishment of Sin (2nd–5th Centuries CE)

The crucial shift comes with the Apostle Paul (1st century CE) and later with Augustine (4th–5th centuries CE).

  • Paul links Adam’s fall to salvation through Christ, creating the narrative of “original sin”.
  • Augustine consolidates the idea that all humanity inherits sin and that desire (especially sexual desire) is proof of the sinfulness of our nature.
  • Adam and Eve are no longer individual figures, but collective symbols of the fall of the entire human race.
From this point on, the narrative is no longer merely a myth.
It becomes the central foundation of Christian anthropology.

5. The Medieval Church and the Total Use of Myth as a Mechanism of Control (6th–15th Centuries CE)

  • In the Middle Ages, the narrative becomes established as unquestionable dogma.
  • Woman (Eve) is identified with the source of evil and the Fall.
  • Human beings cannot save themselves—they need the Church.
  • The idea of original sin becomes the reason why human beings depend on the Grace of God.

6. The Enlightenment and the Deconstruction of the Myth (17th–19th Centuries)

  • Gradually, philosophers such as Spinoza, Kant and Nietzsche begin to question the narrative.
  • Nietzsche writes that Christianity “created guilt to keep people small”.
  • Freud considers the story of Eve a reflection of patriarchy’s fear of female autonomy.
Nevertheless, the narrative survives to this day,
because the fear of guilt is deeply rooted in cultural consciousness.

Conclusion: How the Myth Was Codified and Preserved

  1. It took elements from earlier myths and adapted them to a strict monotheism.
  2. It was transformed from a story into theological dogma.
  3. It became the basis of society’s moral and legal structure.
  4. It was used as a tool for controlling human nature.
The real question, then, is not “when it appeared in codified texts”.
The real question is:

Why has it survived to this day, when we know that its story is a construction?

The answer is simple:
Because its existence serves those in power.
