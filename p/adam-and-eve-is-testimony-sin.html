Adam and Eve. Is Testimony a Sin? Convenient, or All Too Convenient?

Published on March 19, 2025 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The nar­rat­ive of Adam and Eve did not appear sud­denly. It was not an isol­ated event, but a nar­rat­ive that de­veloped gradu­ally, through various tra­di­tions, co­di­fic­a­tions and re­in­ter­pret­a­tions.

Let us look at when and how it ap­peared in co­di­fied form:

1. The Earliest Precedents

The story of Adam and Eve was not unique to the Hebrews. There were earlier nar­rat­ives that were in­cor­por­ated and re­worked.

  • The Meso­pot­ami­an Tra­di­tion

    • The Epic of Gil­gamesh (3000–2000 BCE) con­tains a nar­rat­ive re­sem­bling “Para­dise”: the hero Gil­gamesh seeks im­mor­tal­ity but fails because of a fateful choice that makes him mortal.
    • The story of Enkidu (Gil­gamesh’s friend) is almost identic­al to Adam’s fall: he was in­no­cent and lived among the animals, until he became human through a woman and was cut off from nature.

  • The Sumeri­an Myth of Nintur

    • Nintur was a female fer­til­ity deity who, in one version, gives life to the first human, but is later pun­ished for re­veal­ing “too much know­ledge” to human beings.

These tra­di­tions pred­ated the Hebrew Genesis and show that the nar­rat­ive was not ori­gin­al, but was re­con­struc­ted and in­cor­por­ated into Judaism.

2. The First Codified Hebrew Version (Genesis, 6th–5th Centuries BCE)

The story of Adam and Eve as we know it today first ap­peared in the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Bible), spe­cific­ally in Genesis.

  • This nar­rat­ive was com­posed during the Baby­lo­ni­an cap­tiv­ity of the Hebrews (6th century BCE).
  • It in­cor­por­ated earlier tra­di­tions, but em­phas­ised obed­i­ence to God and guilt.
  • Unlike in the earlier myths, Eve is neither a goddess nor an in­de­pend­ent figure, but is created from Adam.
  • The fruit of know­ledge becomes for­bid­den, es­tab­lish­ing the concept of sin as trans­gres­sion.

3. Developments in the Hellenistic Period (3rd–1st Centuries BCE)

When Judaism came into contact with Greek thought, a new phase of co­di­fic­a­tion and in­ter­pret­a­tion began:

  • Philo of Al­ex­an­dria (1st century BCE), a Jewish philo­soph­er in­flu­enced by Plato,
    turns the story of Adam and Eve into a philo­soph­ic­al, al­leg­or­ic­al myth.
  • Adam sym­bol­ises the ra­tion­al soul,
    while Eve rep­res­ents the sensory soul that is led astray.
  • This in­ter­pret­a­tion would later in­flu­ence Chris­tian­ity.

4. The Role of Christianity: The Definitive Establishment of Sin (2nd–5th Centuries CE)

The crucial shift comes with the Apostle Paul (1st century CE) and later with Au­gustine (4th–5th cen­tur­ies CE).

  • Paul links Adam’s fall to sal­va­tion through Christ, cre­at­ing the nar­rat­ive of “ori­gin­al sin”.
  • Au­gustine con­sol­id­ates the idea that all hu­man­ity in­her­its sin and that desire (es­pe­cially sexual desire) is proof of the sin­ful­ness of our nature.
  • Adam and Eve are no longer in­di­vidu­al figures, but col­lect­ive symbols of the fall of the entire human race.
From this point on, the narrative is no longer merely a myth.
It becomes the central foundation of Christian anthropology.

5. The Medieval Church and the Total Use of Myth as a Mechanism of Control (6th–15th Centuries CE)

  • In the Middle Ages, the nar­rat­ive becomes es­tab­lished as un­ques­tion­able dogma.
  • Woman (Eve) is iden­ti­fied with the source of evil and the Fall.
  • Human beings cannot save them­selves—they need the Church.
  • The idea of ori­gin­al sin becomes the reason why human beings depend on the Grace of God.

6. The Enlightenment and the Deconstruction of the Myth (17th–19th Centuries)

  • Gradu­ally, philo­soph­ers such as Spinoza, Kant and Ni­et­z­sche begin to ques­tion the nar­rat­ive.
  • Ni­et­z­sche writes that Chris­tian­ity “created guilt to keep people small”.
  • Freud con­siders the story of Eve a re­flec­tion of pat­ri­archy’s fear of female autonomy.
Nevertheless, the narrative survives to this day,
because the fear of guilt is deeply rooted in cultural consciousness.

Conclusion: How the Myth Was Codified and Preserved

  1. It took ele­ments from earlier myths and adapted them to a strict mono­the­ism.
  2. It was trans­formed from a story into theo­lo­gic­al dogma.
  3. It became the basis of society’s moral and legal struc­ture.
  4. It was used as a tool for con­trolling human nature.
The real question, then, is not “when it appeared in codified texts”.
The real question is:

Why has it sur­vived to this day, when we know that its story is a con­struc­tion?

The answer is simple:
Because its existence serves those in power.
