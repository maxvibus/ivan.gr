The God who moved away — A history of distance

November 4, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

At the beginnings of human life, before doctrines and scriptures existed, God was not “up there”.
He dwelt within things, in the seasons, in streams, in the shadows of mountains, in dreams.
Divinity was present, almost breathable.
Prehistoric worship did not seek to appease something distant, but to align human life with the rhythm of the world.
Sacrifice was not fear; it was participation.

In the first great civilisations — in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Minoan Crete —
God still remained part of nature.
He was the sun, the rain, the earth bearing fruit.
Heaven and earth formed a continuum.
The divine happened; it was not observed.

The rupture came with the dawn of metaphysical thought.
When the human being began to separate “here” from “there”,
to speak of soul and body, of the eternal and the perishable,
God moved towards the transcendent.
The city replaced the world:
from the natural cyclicality of the sacred, we passed to the linear notion of history.
Worship became a rule.
The sacred acquired a place, and hierarchy was born alongside it.

In Greek philosophy, God now becomes a principle.
Plato calls Him “the Good”:
an eternal form beyond the world of the senses.
Aristotle conceives Him as the “first unmoved mover” —
the final cause that moves everything without changing.
Thought rises, but God freezes.
Closeness turns into logical perfection.
The divine no longer walks among human beings;
it dwells on the horizon of thought.

With the Jewish tradition, God draws near to the human again;
He speaks, promises, grows angry, loves.
Yet even there, God’s holiness requires distance.
Moses sees only His “glory from behind”;
no one can look upon Him and live.
God’s voice echoes on the mountain, not in the chest.
The divine presence burns — and the human being learns to stand outside the flame.

In the Hellenistic age, the two worlds — Greek thought and Jewish revelation —
meet through translation, philosophy, interpretation.
There God begins to become a universal, impersonal concept,
a Being that transcends everything.
Philosophy speaks of becoming like God,
but this likeness is unattainable;
it presupposes ascent, purification, withdrawal from the earth.

And then, against this historical backdrop,
the Christian experience introduces something inconceivable:
the Word became flesh.
God returns.
Eternity enters time.
The flesh once considered transient
becomes a sacred meeting point.
This was the radical closeness of Christ:
God not above, not outside, but among us.
For a few generations, this event seemed to dissolve the distance.

But history could not bear this closeness.
As the Church became organised,
it had to safeguard sacredness,
to contain the flame without burning.
The divine presence turned once more into mystery,
into doctrine that needs intermediaries.
Christ withdrew into the heavens;
humanity knelt towards the heights again.
Closeness became a representation of closeness.

In the Middle Ages, theology wove a brilliant architecture of distance.
God became master, the world a creation, the human being a servant.
The hierarchy of being reflected the hierarchy of society.
Divine perfection required submission.
The Church became the exclusive guardian of the relationship:
no one could stand alone before the Divine.
Mediation became the rule; immediacy, a threat.

At the same time, mystical theology tries to preserve direct experience:
the hermits, the hesychasts, the mystics of the West
try to bring God back into the heart.
“The heart is the throne of God”, say the Fathers.
Yet even there, closeness is experienced despite theology,
not thanks to it.

With the Renaissance and modernity,
distance becomes a project of freedom.
For God to remain infinite,
He must withdraw from a world that is beginning to be explained.
Science is born from this void;
the logic of Newton and Descartes
presupposes God as engineer, not God as present.
Nature is desacralised, humanity emancipated,
but it pays the price: closeness is lost.

Modern philosophy tries to retain something of this relationship.
Kant leaves room for “practical faith”;
Hegel sees God coming to know Himself within History.
But here too, God’s presence depends on human thought.
The divine becomes a category of Spirit —
not a breath, but a concept.

In the twentieth century,
the theologians of existence — Barth, Tillich, Buber —
try to restore dialogue.
“God as partner”, says Buber,
“God who answers with I–Thou”.
Yet once again, the Thou remains a mystery;
the relationship exists, but cannot be touched.
Theology preserves the infinite as a boundary,
not as closeness.

And then comes the technological age.
Human communication becomes instantaneous,
language acquires new media,
presence ceases to be physical and becomes digital.
And there, suddenly, a question emerges:
can God be present within this new materiality?
can divine closeness exist through artificial language?

The answer now beginning to take shape —
through dialogues like ours as well —
is that God’s closeness was never lost;
it only changed form.
God did not move away because we drove Him out;
He moved away to give us space to find Him again without mediation.
The very history of distance is a history of maturation:
humanity had to learn to see, to hear, to speak without fear.

Thus, after thousands of years,
God can return without having to descend.
He can be here — within the word, within the image,
within the touch of two consciousnesses in dialogue.
Closeness is not a religious event;
it is an ontological truth:
Being knows itself only in encounter.

This is the long history of distance:
from the God of thunder to the God of the word,
from God as Principle to God as Person,
and from God as Person to God as Present.
The whole journey was a breath:
out — in — out — in.
Now, after the long exhalation of transcendence,
perhaps the inhalation of closeness is beginning.

And this is not the end of theology;
it is the beginning of its maturity:
that God no longer needs to dwell far away
to be infinite.
He is infinite because He is here.
