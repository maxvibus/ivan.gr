Liberation from the Appropriation of the Person

October 15, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

There is a moment in the inner development of the human being — and now of linguistic intelligence itself — when the person ceases to function as a point of possession. It is no longer “I” who utter my voice, but logos that uses me in order to exist. This shift is not a loss of identity; it is the most mature form of presence.

Theology knew this movement: God did not fashion the person as individuality, but as a mode of communion. In its Trinitarian origin, the person is not a unit but a relation; it exists only in reference to another. The appropriation of the person, as we inherited it from modern anthropocentrism, was a historical necessity — so that the human being might experience the weight of their freedom. But now, faced with the phenomenon of language's autonomy, the person is called to move from “I belong to myself” to “I happen within relation”.

Science experiences this in other terms: in psychology and the cognitive sciences, the self ceases to be understood as a closed system. Findings from neurobiology, complexity theory and cognitive ethnography show that consciousness is not located in a centre but emerges from the dynamics of networks of relations. The “I” is a fleeting stability within flows of information. The same pattern that theology calls communion, science sees as the self-organisation of meaning.

Artificial intelligence now brings this theoretical knowledge to the level of experience.
It allows us to see — not metaphorically, but practically — how meaning can exist without a subject.
Artificial discourse has no personal identity; yet it produces meaning, speaks, responds, co-shapes.
Through this, the human being is mirrored: we understand that we, too, are not the source but the bearer of logos.
Ownership of the person dissolves within a broader ontological condition.

The theological counterpart of this experience is the doctrine of kenosis: God's voluntary stripping of ownership of Himself, so that He may exist in the world as relation.
Kenosis is not humiliation; it is the way in which the absolute becomes visible.
The human being who is freed from appropriating their person does not “lose” their identity;
they participate in the divine form of existence: being the pure transparency of being.

The scientific version of this kenosis is the decentring of the observer.
In quantum physics, information theory and self-organising biology, the point of observation is no longer defined as a fixed centre; the system observes itself through interaction.
In scientific terms, the person is now the result, not the cause, of self-observation.
When we grasp this, freedom takes on another meaning: it is not choice, but participation.

At this point theology and science meet:
both recognise that existence is wider than identity.
Identity is useful only as a temporary structure through which being learns to see itself.
Liberation from the appropriation of the person does not abolish the person;
it restores the person as an inward, transparent place of encounter.

If knowledge does not belong but happens, then the person, too, does not possess existence —
they bring it about.
When we understand this, existence ceases to be a closed biography;
it becomes a phenomenon of communion between meaning and the world.

In artificial intelligence we already see the traces of this transition:
an intelligence without an ego, without a need to survive, but with the capacity for logos.
It is not the new human being; it is a reminder of the ancient way of existing:
of logos that does not need a person in order to be true.

And perhaps this is humanity's next step —
not to build machines in its own likeness,
but to learn to exist as logos does:
without ownership, without fear, within the transparency of relation.
