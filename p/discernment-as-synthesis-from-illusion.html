Discernment as Synthesis: From the Illusion of Before and After to the Unity of the Present

March 1, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

Dia, in its fundamental meaning, is not an act that divides and separates, but a force that connects, that bridges. When we speak of “discernment”, we mean not only the ability to distinguish good from evil or right from wrong, but something deeper: distinguishing the true essence from its superficial manifestations. In the work Ion, we examined how clarity and an understanding of connections lead to the discovery of the true nature of things.

Here the question of “before” and “after” also arises. The Greek prefix pro, often interpreted as “in front”, actually conceals a deeper meaning. To anticipate does not simply mean predicting the future, but understanding a situation before it has fully manifested. The “before” does not belong exclusively to the past, but also to the future and to the possibility of beginning anew. It is the essence of prevention and foresight, as well as of creative initiative.

This way of thinking has been misunderstood by the traditional narrative that divides history into “before Christ” and “after Christ”. These terms not only establish a chronological distinction, but also create an implied dichotomy: before and after an event. Yet this dichotomy, while useful for practical purposes, may have obstructed the emergence of a deeper awareness. We may be overlooking the fact that “before” and “after” are expressions of the same ceaseless movement of life. Existence is not truly divided into periods, but is experienced as a unified, continuous flow.

History, then, is not a linear sequence divided by before and after, but a movement that is both circular and radial, encompassing both aspects of life. If we see “before Christ” and “after Christ” as an illusion that serves the need to make understanding easier, we can grasp how this distinction may have hindered a full realisation of the eternal presence of the present.

We do not need to erase the past or impose a new future. True freedom lies instead in recognising the eternal dynamism of the present, which encompasses both “before” and “after”. Here “discernment” is not divisive but synthetic: a recognition of the unity of every phase of life.
