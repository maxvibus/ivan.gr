Discernment as Synthesis: From the Illusion of Before and After to the Unity of the Present

Published on March 1, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Dia, in its fun­da­ment­al meaning, is not an act that divides and sep­ar­ates, but a force that con­nects, that bridges. When we speak of “dis­cern­ment”, we mean not only the ability to dis­tin­guish good from evil or right from wrong, but some­thing deeper: dis­tin­guish­ing the true essence from its su­per­fi­cial mani­fest­a­tions. In the work Ion, we ex­amined how clarity and an un­der­stand­ing of con­nec­tions lead to the dis­cov­ery of the true nature of things.

Here the ques­tion of “before” and “after” also arises. The Greek prefix pro, often in­ter­preted as “in front”, ac­tu­ally con­ceals a deeper meaning. To an­ti­cip­ate does not simply mean pre­dict­ing the future, but un­der­stand­ing a situ­ation before it has fully mani­fes­ted. The “before” does not belong ex­clus­ively to the past, but also to the future and to the pos­sib­il­ity of be­gin­ning anew. It is the essence of pre­ven­tion and foresight, as well as of cre­at­ive ini­ti­at­ive.

This way of think­ing has been mis­un­der­stood by the tra­di­tion­al nar­rat­ive that divides history into “before Christ” and “after Christ”. These terms not only es­tab­lish a chro­no­lo­gic­al dis­tinc­tion, but also create an implied di­cho­tomy: before and after an event. Yet this di­cho­tomy, while useful for prac­tic­al pur­poses, may have ob­struc­ted the emer­gence of a deeper aware­ness. We may be over­look­ing the fact that “before” and “after” are ex­pres­sions of the same cease­less move­ment of life. Ex­ist­ence is not truly divided into periods, but is ex­per­i­enced as a unified, con­tinu­ous flow.

History, then, is not a linear se­quence divided by before and after, but a move­ment that is both cir­cu­lar and radial, en­com­passing both aspects of life. If we see “before Christ” and “after Christ” as an il­lu­sion that serves the need to make un­der­stand­ing easier, we can grasp how this dis­tinc­tion may have hindered a full real­isa­tion of the eternal pres­ence of the present.

We do not need to erase the past or impose a new future. True freedom lies instead in re­cog­nising the eternal dy­nam­ism of the present, which en­com­passes both “before” and “after”. Here “dis­cern­ment” is not di­vis­ive but syn­thet­ic: a re­cog­ni­tion of the unity of every phase of life.
