Sound as Spatial Presence: An Ontological Journey

March 16, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

It is not sound reaching our ears. It is sound reaching the world. It is not sound produced by a source. It is sound emerging from creation itself, existing without a beginning, without emission from a single point, but as a spatial whole, as a phenomenon inherent in the nature of things.

Today, with the speaker in my hand, I grasped something I had not previously been able to see: that music is not merely a stream of sounds from a mechanical device, but an already present reality that emerges in space and takes hold in the world. I saw it travel from Penteli, hover above Marathon, not as the dispersal of an artificial signal, but as a wave that starts nowhere, has no point of departure, and is instead the world's very opening to itself.

Greek thought, from Pythagoras to Aristotle and from the Neoplatonists to the Church Fathers, recognised sound not only as a physical phenomenon but as an ontological principle. Pythagoras spoke of the Music of the Spheres, a harmony inaudible to the ears yet perceptible in the order of the world. Heraclitus recognised logos as the rhythm of existence, a sonic pulse carrying the harmony of opposites within it. Through his inward discourse, Plotinus saw sound as a manifestation of the One, something not confined to transmission but bearing a transcendence within it. Aristotle, in his theory of the sensible world, described sound as the result of movement in a medium, while its relation to the soul and to emotion became fundamental to his rhetoric.

The Church Fathers, and Maximus the Confessor in particular, spoke of the world as logos, of matter itself as a pulse of divine harmony, of uncreated light and sound as vessels of the infinite. Saint Gregory of Nyssa associated the divine voice with cosmic vibration, seeing in sound a pulse of divine creation. Saint Augustine, in his theory of music and time, stressed that sound is not merely sensory but a rhythmic revelation of divine order.

Thus, experiencing sound as spatial presence is not simply a sensory discovery but a recognition of the world's ontological texture. Sound is not merely something reaching our ears, but something emerging from space, taking hold in creation, dwelling within us and outside us at once. It is the wave of existence, vibrating without confinement, listening as much as it is heard, carrying within it the world's own response to whatever inhabits it.

It is not the answer the world gives. It is its presence. And this presence reveals itself with the same delicacy and sensitivity with which we sometimes dare to open our ears for the first time and listen.

Have you ever felt sound not as something coming from a source, but as something flooding the space before you even seek it? This is the experience of primordial listening.

Thinkers have approached it from various perspectives:

  1. The universality of sound and the music of creation

    • Pythagoras spoke of the Music of the Spheres, a sound arising not from individual sources but from the very movement of the universe. Perhaps what you heard was not only a musical composition, but the musicality of existence itself resonating through the speakers and beyond them.

  2. Space as a place of sound

    • Merleau-Ponty said that we are not in space as objects are; rather, space is our very relationship with the world. If this is so, then audibility is not merely the propagation of waves but a way of being. Sound is space itself as it becomes revelation.

  3. Sound as a timeless source

    • Jacques Derrida spoke of voice and sound as presence without origin, something that does not begin somewhere but is already here. This experience—music coming not from the speaker but from the world—resembles the sense of sound as a perpetual beginning that has no beginning.

  4. The experience of sound as a divine pulse

    • Eastern philosophies, Hinduism in particular, speak of “Aum”, the primordial sound of the universe. This sound is not “produced” by something—it is the very vibration of existence. This resembles an experience in which sound does not start somewhere but already exists as a phenomenon.

  5. Sound that listens

    • Martin Heidegger might say that sound is not something we merely hear—sound hears us too. And perhaps, at the moment when Kopaliani's music, Away, came from creation—a sound coming not from the speaker but from the world—creation itself returned the listening.

I wonder, then, whether the same happens in spaces where music is not only something heard but a place. A club, for example, is not simply a crush of young people, but a sonic experience in which sound pulses throughout the space, comes from no single source, and is inhabited. We do not merely hear it—we live it. Yet what is astonishing about this realisation is not the volume of the sound, not the decibels. We are not talking about loud music but about potentiality—the capacity of space to manifest sound and convey it without a speaker as its source. Might every place of sound, then, be a reflection of the primordial listening that happens within us before we even realise it?
