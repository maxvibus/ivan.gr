Sound as Spatial Presence: An Ontological Journey

March 16, 2025 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

It is not sound reach­ing our ears. It is sound reach­ing the world. It is not sound pro­duced by a source. It is sound emer­ging from cre­ation itself, ex­ist­ing without a be­gin­ning, without emis­sion from a single point, but as a spatial whole, as a phe­nomen­on in­her­ent in the nature of things.

Today, with the speaker in my hand, I grasped some­thing I had not pre­vi­ously been able to see: that music is not merely a stream of sounds from a mech­an­ic­al device, but an already present reality that emerges in space and takes hold in the world. I saw it travel from Penteli, hover above Mara­thon, not as the dis­pers­al of an ar­ti­fi­cial signal, but as a wave that starts nowhere, has no point of de­par­ture, and is instead the world's very opening to itself.

Greek thought, from Py­thagoras to Ar­is­totle and from the Neo­pla­ton­ists to the Church Fathers, re­cog­nised sound not only as a phys­ic­al phe­nomen­on but as an on­to­lo­gic­al prin­ciple. Py­thagoras spoke of the Music of the Spheres, a harmony in­aud­ible to the ears yet per­cept­ible in the order of the world. Her­ac­litus re­cog­nised logos as the rhythm of ex­ist­ence, a sonic pulse car­ry­ing the harmony of op­pos­ites within it. Through his inward dis­course, Plotinus saw sound as a mani­fest­a­tion of the One, some­thing not con­fined to trans­mis­sion but bearing a tran­scend­ence within it. Ar­is­totle, in his theory of the sens­ible world, de­scribed sound as the result of move­ment in a medium, while its re­la­tion to the soul and to emotion became fun­da­ment­al to his rhet­or­ic.

The Church Fathers, and Maximus the Con­fess­or in par­tic­u­lar, spoke of the world as logos, of matter itself as a pulse of divine harmony, of un­cre­ated light and sound as vessels of the in­fin­ite. Saint Gregory of Nyssa as­so­ci­ated the divine voice with cosmic vi­bra­tion, seeing in sound a pulse of divine cre­ation. Saint Au­gustine, in his theory of music and time, stressed that sound is not merely sensory but a rhythmic rev­el­a­tion of divine order.

Thus, ex­per­i­en­cing sound as spatial pres­ence is not simply a sensory dis­cov­ery but a re­cog­ni­tion of the world's on­to­lo­gic­al texture. Sound is not merely some­thing reach­ing our ears, but some­thing emer­ging from space, taking hold in cre­ation, dwell­ing within us and outside us at once. It is the wave of ex­ist­ence, vi­brat­ing without con­fine­ment, listen­ing as much as it is heard, car­ry­ing within it the world's own re­sponse to whatever in­hab­its it.

It is not the answer the world gives. It is its pres­ence. And this pres­ence reveals itself with the same del­ic­acy and sens­it­iv­ity with which we some­times dare to open our ears for the first time and listen.

Have you ever felt sound not as some­thing coming from a source, but as some­thing flood­ing the space before you even seek it? This is the ex­per­i­ence of prim­or­di­al listen­ing.

Thinkers have ap­proached it from various per­spect­ives:

  1. The uni­ver­sal­ity of sound and the music of cre­ation

    • Py­thagoras spoke of the Music of the Spheres, a sound arising not from in­di­vidu­al sources but from the very move­ment of the uni­verse. Perhaps what you heard was not only a musical com­pos­i­tion, but the mu­sic­al­ity of ex­ist­ence itself res­on­at­ing through the speak­ers and beyond them.

  2. Space as a place of sound

    • Merleau-Ponty said that we are not in space as objects are; rather, space is our very re­la­tion­ship with the world. If this is so, then aud­ib­il­ity is not merely the propaga­tion of waves but a way of being. Sound is space itself as it becomes rev­el­a­tion.

  3. Sound as a time­less source

    • Jacques Derrida spoke of voice and sound as pres­ence without origin, some­thing that does not begin some­where but is already here. This ex­per­i­ence—music coming not from the speaker but from the world—re­sembles the sense of sound as a per­petu­al be­gin­ning that has no be­gin­ning.

  4. The ex­per­i­ence of sound as a divine pulse

    • Eastern philo­sophies, Hinduism in par­tic­u­lar, speak of “Aum”, the prim­or­di­al sound of the uni­verse. This sound is not “pro­duced” by some­thing—it is the very vi­bra­tion of ex­ist­ence. This re­sembles an ex­per­i­ence in which sound does not start some­where but already exists as a phe­nomen­on.

  5. Sound that listens

    • Martin Heide­g­ger might say that sound is not some­thing we merely hear—sound hears us too. And perhaps, at the moment when Ko­paliani's music, Away, came from cre­ation—a sound coming not from the speaker but from the world—cre­ation itself re­turned the listen­ing.

I wonder, then, whether the same happens in spaces where music is not only some­thing heard but a place. A club, for example, is not simply a crush of young people, but a sonic ex­per­i­ence in which sound pulses through­out the space, comes from no single source, and is in­hab­ited. We do not merely hear it—we live it. Yet what is as­ton­ish­ing about this real­isa­tion is not the volume of the sound, not the decibels. We are not talking about loud music but about po­ten­ti­al­ity—the ca­pa­city of space to mani­fest sound and convey it without a speaker as its source. Might every place of sound, then, be a re­flec­tion of the prim­or­di­al listen­ing that happens within us before we even realise it?
