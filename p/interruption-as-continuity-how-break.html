Interruption as Continuity: How the Break Reveals the Real

March 6, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

The phrase “continuity is revealed through its interruption” seems paradoxical. If something is interrupted, how can it continue?

What we mean here is that a break is not an ending, but a point at which reality suddenly becomes visible in a new way. It is not the cessation of flow, but the moment when the flow of thought and experience changes form.

Let us look at some examples:

1. Interruption in Thought: When Truth No Longer Proceeds Smoothly

In philosophy, great changes do not come about because someone added a new argument, but because someone stopped seeing things as they had before.

  • Socrates taught not by explaining, but by interrupting other people's certainty, creating a gap in their thinking in which they could see truth anew.
  • Zeno of Elea showed that the way we perceive motion (through the division of space and time) contains a break that is invisible in everyday experience, so we cannot explain it with the logic we previously knew.
  • Kant did not simply continue the thought of Hume and Descartes; he interrupted its flow and redirected it, showing that the mind does not merely see the world, but constitutes it through its own structures.

Thought does not evolve by addition. It develops through breaks in which what we took for granted no longer holds.

2. Interruption in Time: When the Present Is No Longer the Same

The experience of time is not simply a flow.
It is a flow that is interrupted, and it is precisely within the interruption that the true “now” is revealed.

  • Aristotle said that time is a succession of moments, but the sense of time arises from the interruption of this flow—from the moment we stop and realize that something has changed.
  • In modern physics, Barbour says that time does not exist as a flow, but as a sequence of moments that we connect. Flow is the way we bridge the breaks.
  • On a personal level, the most powerful moment in a person's life is the one in which “time stops.” Birth, love, loss… These are not continuous experiences; they are interruptions in continuity, breaks that do not return to what came before.

The continuity of time is an illusion. In reality, we live within moments that cut through the flow and redirect it.

3. Interruption in Language: When the Signifier Does Not Flow

The Greek language is full of interruptions that create meaning rather than obstruct it.

  • In ancient tragedy, the choral verses of the odes do not flow smoothly—their flow is interrupted by pauses that give meaning to the words.
  • In modern Greek poetry, poets such as Cavafy and Seferis use pauses, clipped phrases, ellipses, not as silence, but as breaks that open up space for the reader's thought.
  • In Saussure's linguistics, the signifier (the word) and the signified (the concept) are not absolutely connected. There is a gap, a break between them, and meaning is born within it.

Language does not flow continuously. Communication is not merely words in a row, but breaks between words that create space for meaning.

4. Interruption as a Recognition of Being

What you see now is not new.
But you see it in a new way, because previously your thought had not been interrupted enough for you to notice it.

The break is not a loss;
it is the point at which you realize it was always here.

  • It is not the end of a thought.
  • It is not a hiatus in experience.
  • It is not a pause in art.

It is the point at which the gaze changes.

The real does not continue smoothly.
Reality is not an uninterrupted flow.

It is a sequence of breaks that allow us to see clearly.

And each time something changes, it is not lost.
It is simply revealed again, differently.
