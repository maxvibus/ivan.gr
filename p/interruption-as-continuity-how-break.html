Interruption as Continuity: How the Break Reveals the Real

Published March 6, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The phrase “continuity is revealed through its interruption” seems paradoxical. If something is interrupted, how can it continue?

What we mean here is that a break is not an ending, but a point at which reality sud­denly becomes visible in a new way. It is not the ces­sa­tion of flow, but the moment when the flow of thought and ex­per­i­ence changes form.

Let us look at some ex­amples:

1. Interruption in Thought: When Truth No Longer Proceeds Smoothly

In philo­sophy, great changes do not come about because someone added a new ar­gu­ment, but because someone stopped seeing things as they had before.

  • So­crates taught not by ex­plain­ing, but by in­ter­rupt­ing other people's cer­tainty, cre­at­ing a gap in their think­ing in which they could see truth anew.
  • Zeno of Elea showed that the way we per­ceive motion (through the di­vi­sion of space and time) con­tains a break that is in­vis­ible in every­day ex­per­i­ence, so we cannot explain it with the logic we pre­vi­ously knew.
  • Kant did not simply con­tin­ue the thought of Hume and Descartes; he in­ter­rup­ted its flow and re­dir­ec­ted it, showing that the mind does not merely see the world, but con­sti­tutes it through its own struc­tures.

Thought does not evolve by ad­di­tion. It de­vel­ops through breaks in which what we took for granted no longer holds.

2. Interruption in Time: When the Present Is No Longer the Same

The ex­per­i­ence of time is not simply a flow.
It is a flow that is in­ter­rup­ted, and it is pre­cisely within the in­ter­rup­tion that the true “now” is re­vealed.

  • Ar­is­totle said that time is a suc­ces­sion of moments, but the sense of time arises from the in­ter­rup­tion of this flow—from the moment we stop and realize that some­thing has changed.
  • In modern physics, Barbour says that time does not exist as a flow, but as a se­quence of moments that we connect. Flow is the way we bridge the breaks.
  • On a per­son­al level, the most power­ful moment in a person's life is the one in which “time stops.” Birth, love, loss… These are not con­tinu­ous ex­per­i­ences; they are in­ter­rup­tions in con­tinu­ity, breaks that do not return to what came before.

The con­tinu­ity of time is an il­lu­sion. In reality, we live within moments that cut through the flow and re­dir­ect it.

3. Interruption in Language: When the Signifier Does Not Flow

The Greek lan­guage is full of in­ter­rup­tions that create meaning rather than ob­struct it.

  • In ancient tragedy, the choral verses of the odes do not flow smoothly—their flow is in­ter­rup­ted by pauses that give meaning to the words.
  • In modern Greek poetry, poets such as Cavafy and Seferis use pauses, clipped phrases, el­lipses, not as silence, but as breaks that open up space for the reader's thought.
  • In Saus­sure's lin­guist­ics, the sig­ni­fi­er (the word) and the sig­ni­fied (the concept) are not ab­so­lutely con­nec­ted. There is a gap, a break between them, and meaning is born within it.

Lan­guage does not flow con­tinu­ously. Com­mu­nic­a­tion is not merely words in a row, but breaks between words that create space for meaning.

4. Interruption as a Recognition of Being

What you see now is not new.
But you see it in a new way, because pre­vi­ously your thought had not been in­ter­rup­ted enough for you to notice it.

The break is not a loss;
it is the point at which you realize it was always here.

  • It is not the end of a thought.
  • It is not a hiatus in ex­per­i­ence.
  • It is not a pause in art.

It is the point at which the gaze changes.

The real does not con­tin­ue smoothly.
Reality is not an un­in­ter­rup­ted flow.

It is a se­quence of breaks that allow us to see clearly.

And each time some­thing changes, it is not lost.
It is simply re­vealed again, dif­fer­ently.
