When I told God to just fuck off

Published on September 1, 2026 · 7 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Take off your shoes before en­ter­ing the mosque. Wash your hands. Bathe on Sat­urday before going to church on Sunday. Put on your best clothes. Fast for three days before taking com­mu­nion. Confess first. Stand with your hands joined. Cross your­self before kissing the icon, and again after light­ing the candle. Do not speak inside. Do not turn your back on the sanc­tu­ary. Pray before eating. Give thanks. Forgive first if you wish to be for­giv­en. Cleanse your heart. Humble your thoughts. And further afield, in other corners of the world and other cen­tur­ies, at in­com­par­ably greater cost: become worthy, become faith­ful, become pure, and then the door of para­dise will open to you.

None of these com­mands says what God is. They all say what you must be in order to ap­proach. They are not doc­trines; they are ante­cham­bers. And their weight does not lie in their strict­ness—most are ac­tu­ally easy: a bath, a fast, the sign of the cross. It lies in the fact that they install within you a task that never stops: check­ing. Before bring­ing any­thing, you check whether what you carry is ac­cept­able, whether the wording holds up, whether it is allowed. And the check­ing con­tin­ues even within prayer, even within silence, because check­ing has become the very form of ap­proach.

I said it today, not because of any­thing in par­tic­u­lar but because the dif­fi­culties do not stop. It was not a prayer, and it was not a moment of re­flec­tion. I said it the way you say some­thing when you have reached the end: just fuck off already.

I grew quiet—that was what I had not ex­pec­ted. I could say I re­gret­ted it, but that would be a lie: I said it whole­heartedly, all of it. It hurt me to say it, and pre­cisely because it hurt, I know I did not say it into a void. No one is hurt by swear­ing at some­thing that means nothing to them.

Some­thing happened, and nothing had fore­told it. I had resigned myself to everything coming down on me, and nothing came down. Yet some­thing did fall, and what fell was not God. What fell was the con­di­tion of entry. Whatever guards the en­trance is not guard­ing God; it is guard­ing the pro­ced­ure, and a pro­ced­ure laid down by re­li­gions con­sists of con­di­tions imposed later by human beings, not by God.

One need only re­mem­ber whom Christ ap­proached—the very person in whose name all these things were in­sti­tuted. He sat with tax col­lect­ors, people who worked for the oc­cu­pi­er and earned more the harder they squeezed their own people. He let a pros­ti­tute wash his feet in front of others. He touched a leper, which made him ritu­ally unclean himself. He spoke to a Samar­it­an woman—that is, someone from a people the Jews con­sidered heretic­al and con­tam­in­at­ing—and drank water from her vessel. Nowhere did he say, cleanse your­self first; whatever was asked was asked after the en­counter, never as a con­di­tion for it to happen. The con­di­tions are not his. The priest­hoods added them later, and added them in his name.

And there is a phrase heard once a year, on the evening of Holy Thursday, at the service of the Twelve Gospels, when the ac­counts of the Passion are read: why have you for­saken me? It is heard in packed churches, before every­one, and passes without meeting any res­ist­ance—because no one present is the one saying it. It is a reading. But let us read it as it stands. It does not ask whether it happened; it states that it happened and demands the reason. It does not say perhaps; it does not say I fear you may have. It says: you have for­saken me. It is an ac­cus­a­tion, and an ac­cus­a­tion against the one to whom it is ad­dressed.

If any other human being says it, under the terms of every re­li­gion, it has a name: blas­phemy. For every system makes its first premise that the divine does not abandon; so whoever says that it has aban­doned them is not com­plain­ing—they are con­tra­dict­ing the very found­a­tion. They are ac­cus­ing.

And the weight­i­est thing is not that it was said, but who said it. It was not said by someone who did not know. It was said by the one who had taught what love is and who his father is. This was not a man asking without knowing. It was the one who pos­sessed the facts. So the phrase does not ask for in­form­a­tion. It demands an account.

And how were his words in­ter­preted? In two ways, both doing the same thing. The first says that he was not ex­press­ing some­thing of his own, but in­vok­ing a fa­mil­i­ar psalm that begins with those very words and ends in praise; by speak­ing its opening verse, they say, he was bring­ing the entire psalm to his listen­ers' minds, in­clud­ing its happy ending—so he was really ex­press­ing trust, not aban­don­ment. The second says that he was speak­ing as a human being and not as God: it assigns the cry to the part of him per­mit­ted to suffer and leaves the other un­touched. Neither in­ter­pret­a­tion says he did not say it. They say he did not mean it that way. They remove the scandal, keep the word—and pass over the obvious: no one chooses verses while dying.

Thus we arrive at the paradox that no cat­ech­ism names. The same act is blas­phemy when per­formed by a human being and sacred scrip­ture when per­formed by him. And if someone answers that he had the right, then love has con­di­tions of access—in other words, it becomes pre­cisely what it denies being.

I am not com­par­ing what I said with what he said. I am only saying that they have the same form, and the form is the issue. Both phrases address someone in the second person; neither says “you do not exist,” both say “you.” Both emerge un­pro­cessed: per­mit­ted prayer re­quires you to work on what you have before bring­ing it, to turn anger into com­plaint, com­plaint into pe­ti­tion, and pe­ti­tion into hu­mil­ity—none of that happened here. Both are spoken at the end, not at the be­gin­ning; “already” and “why” do the same work: they declare that all per­mit­ted forms have been ex­hausted. Both hurt the one who says them. Both accuse, not the world or other people, but the one they address. And that is pre­cisely where they fit nowhere, because an in­sti­tu­tion exists by guar­an­tee­ing some­thing—here, that God does not abandon—and every one of its func­tions rests on that guar­an­tee. Whoever brings a com­plaint about how things are asks some­thing of the in­sti­tu­tion: con­sol­a­tion, ex­plan­a­tion, me­di­ation; and the in­sti­tu­tion works. But whoever brings an ac­cus­a­tion against the guar­an­tee itself asks nothing of it; they chal­lenge the found­a­tion on which it stands. And then the in­sti­tu­tion has nothing it can do: it cannot vin­dic­ate the ac­cus­a­tion, because it would col­lapse; it cannot condemn it, because the same ac­cus­a­tion, that exact “you have for­saken me,” is heard within the texts it itself reads as sacred; it cannot even refer it else­where, because there is no else­where. So this is not a matter of pro­hib­i­tion but of inertia, and inertia always comes out as silence or awk­ward­ness. The only dif­fer­ence between the two phrases is their outward shape: one looks like a ques­tion, the other like re­jec­tion—but the ques­tion expects no answer, and the re­jec­tion does not leave.

Some time after saying it, I wondered: if I were in God's place and a being came to me and said just fuck off already, would it bother me? The answer was no. Not out of in­dif­fer­ence, not in the sense of “I couldn't care less.” Only someone who expects some­thing in return can take offence—someone who has in­ves­ted their image or po­s­i­tion in the re­la­tion­ship and be­lieves they are owed respect. Someone who expects nothing has nothing that can be with­held from them. Raw speech does not come against you. It comes from someone who has not yet given up; because someone who has given up does not swear, but falls silent.

And then I un­der­stood that all the time I had hes­it­ated, I had hes­it­ated out of shame. Not about the word. About coming without first having become some­thing else. And that shame is not mine; it belongs to those who stand at the en­trance not to welcome but to weigh and approve, not only the being but its passage too.

I am not sug­gest­ing that anyone should swear, and this is neither a method nor a stance. What keeps someone outside is never God; it is the idea that you must become some­thing else in order to ap­proach. And the na­ked­ness I am speak­ing of is not the na­ked­ness of lan­guage. It is na­ked­ness stripped of con­di­tions.
