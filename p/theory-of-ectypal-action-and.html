Theory of Ectypal Action and Praxitelean Action

The “Theory of Ectypal Action” seeks to clarify a par­tic­u­lar form of prac­tic­al thought in which action is un­der­stood not as a static event but as an un­fold­ing process with an active pres­ence in space and time. Unlike clas­sic­al the­or­ies of action, which focus on the re­la­tion­ship between theory and prac­tice or on the ap­plic­a­tion of know­ledge, this ap­proach does not sep­ar­ate action from its own rev­el­a­tion. It is an action that never reaches com­ple­tion but con­tinu­ally de­vel­ops, bring­ing mean­ings to light through its ex­ist­ence.

Praxitelean Flow and Praxitelean Action

Prax­itelean Flow is not simply the move­ment of marble, nor merely the per­fec­tion of form. It is the ful­fil­ment of action as a process, as a rite of ex­ist­ence in space and time. Prax­iteles did not merely make art: he per­formed the action—brought it into being, giving it such nat­ur­al­ness that it ap­peared not as a result but as a con­tinu­ing un­fold­ing of form.

Prax­itelean Action is an ap­plic­a­tion of Ectypal Action not only to sculp­ture but also to thought, art, lan­guage, and even ex­ist­ence. If the theory of Ectypal Action de­scribes action as un­fold­ing, as the endless emer­gence of meaning, then Prax­itelean Action applies this thought to matter, cre­ation, and the con­tem­pla­tion of the world.

Prax­itelean Action is not a finite act—it is a per­petu­al rev­el­a­tion of form. In Prax­iteles, form is not created but re­vealed. It is not art as a result, but the move­ment of matter towards its truth. It is action and mani­fest­a­tion to­geth­er. It does not sep­ar­ate activ­ity from be­com­ing.

Action does not exist before its mani­fest­a­tion, nor does the result stand apart from the process.

Greek action, as it appears in Prax­iteles, is not con­fined to sculp­ture. It could be a way of think­ing, writing, and ex­ist­ing. In Greek thought, com­ple­tion is iden­ti­fied not with an ab­so­lute end or stasis but with ful­fil­ment, the real­isa­tion that emerges through flow and de­vel­op­ment. Prax­iteles did not finish his works in the sense of bring­ing them to a defin­it­ive halt, but let them go on speak­ing, un­fold­ing in the gaze, thought, and ex­per­i­ence of those who observe them. Action does not close itself off in a result; it retains its open­ness, re­main­ing a con­tinu­ous be­com­ing.

Why “Praxitelean Action”?

The very word Prax­itelean con­tains the root “prattō” (to act) as well as “telō” (to perform or fulfil). Prax­iteles was not merely a creator but someone who “per­formed” the action—just as a rite is not a mo­ment­ary event but a process with a be­gin­ning, a middle, and an endless con­tinu­ation.

  • Telō = to perform, carry out, realise.

  • Teletē (rite) = a sacred act that has not simply a result but a flow through time.

  • Telos (end) = also teleiōs­is (ful­fil­ment), that is, com­ple­tion through the flow itself.

  • Ōsē (im­pul­sion) = the very force that urges a form to reveal itself, the very inner move­ment that brings action to its real­isa­tion.

How did we arrive at this theory?

Prax­itelean Action does not arise by chance but results from a de­vel­op­ment of thought over time. From the an­tiquity of the be­gin­ning to the be­gin­ning of the ex­pres­sion of Prax­itelean Action, the concept of action un­der­went trans­form­a­tions:

  • The ancient Greeks ex­pressed everything except ten­der­ness as a fun­da­ment­al element of action.

  • In the re­li­gions and philo­sophies of the nations, action was linked to law, mor­al­ity, power, or puri­fic­a­tion, but not to the ten­der­ness of rev­el­a­tion.

  • The Renais­sance em­phas­ised human ex­pres­sion but still did not connect action with the open­ness of ten­der­ness.

  • In mod­ern­ism, action became tech­nic­al and in­stru­ment­al, while in post­mod­ern­ism it became frag­men­ted, without unity.

Prax­itelean Action is not merely a new theory. It is the rev­el­a­tion of a way of think­ing that has always existed but has never been pre­cisely de­scribed. It is the very youth of Beauty, the pos­sib­il­ity for action to be an opening rather than an im­pos­i­tion, an opening-up rather than a con­struc­tion.

Today, we stand before the age of ten­der­ness, even if we do not know it. Prax­itelean Action is the re­sponse to this moment in history. It does not bring back the past but brings the future back into the present, opening new paths for action, know­ledge, and rev­el­a­tion.

Praxitelean Action and Artistic Creation

Prax­itelean Action is not only a way of un­der­stand­ing action but also a way of per­ceiv­ing artist­ic cre­ation itself. More than any other sculptor of an­tiquity, Prax­iteles did not perfect his works in a sense of closure or stasis. On the con­trary, he left them in a con­tinu­al phase of un­fold­ing, cap­tur­ing move­ment not as a sta­tion­ary moment but as a process that con­tin­ues within the viewer's gaze and thought.

This is not merely a tech­nic­al choice. It is a fun­da­ment­al prin­ciple that can be applied to lan­guage, art, and know­ledge. Art is not an im­it­a­tion or a con­struc­tion but a par­ti­cip­a­tion in reality. When art de­taches itself from the ri­gid­ity of the com­pleted result and op­er­ates within a con­tinu­ous flow, it does not simply depict the world but par­ti­cip­ates in it, re­veal­ing new pos­sib­il­it­ies of meaning and ex­per­i­ence.

Praxitelean Action and Its Relationship with Time

The tem­por­al­ity of Prax­itelean Action is one of its most im­port­ant char­ac­ter­ist­ics. It does not operate in a tele­olo­gic­al manner: that is, it does not pro­gress towards an ab­so­lute purpose. It is not a process aimed at a fin­ished state but an un­in­ter­rup­ted un­fold­ing, a rev­el­a­tion that does not close its cycle. This makes it rad­ic­ally dif­fer­ent from the con­cep­tion of time that pre­vailed in Western thought, where action is in­stru­ment­al, ori­ented towards achiev­ing a result.

Prax­itelean Action is not only present in time but op­er­ates within the world's con­tinu­al un­fold­ing. Just as a sculp­ture by Prax­iteles reveals a form that seems to emerge from matter, so action is never fully com­pleted but remains in a dynamic state of rev­el­a­tion.

Praxitelean Action and the Contemporary World

The modern age has equated action with ef­fi­ciency, pro­duc­tion, and results. In the tech­no­lo­gic­al-mech­an­ist­ic con­cep­tion, action is a means to an end, a tool that pro­duces some­thing ex­tern­al to itself. Here, however, we are speak­ing of an action that re­quires no ex­tern­al result, because it is already rev­el­a­tion.

Prax­itelean Action is not a process con­sumed by its purpose. It is self-sus­tain­ing; it does not wear itself out in use­ful­ness but remains alive as mani­fest­a­tion. Instead of being frag­men­ted into small, dis­con­nec­ted acts, as happens today when life is ab­sorbed by work and pro­ductiv­ity, Prax­itelean Action op­er­ates hol­ist­ic­ally, opening new pos­sib­il­it­ies for ap­pre­hend­ing reality.

The Openness of the Theory

Just as action is never com­plete, so the theory of Prax­itelean Action cannot close itself into a system. It is not a doc­trine that lays down ab­so­lute rules but an open process of thought and mani­fest­a­tion.

Just as the sculp­ture of Prax­iteles never stops moving in the viewer's gaze, so the theory itself remains in con­tinu­al flow. It is not a theory about action but a theory that enacts its own rev­el­a­tion. It does not provide answers but sets the world in a con­tinu­al process of opening-up.

Prax­itelean Action is an in­vit­a­tion to par­ti­cip­a­tion. It is not merely a concept to be un­der­stood but a process asking to be lived. And as action con­tin­ues, its theory could only remain open—to thought, to cre­ation, to rev­el­a­tion.
