The sentence before its words

Published on October 21, 2026 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

In March I wrote that in a dia­logue with a lan­guage model there is "an in­ter­val before the AI locks into a spe­cif­ic answer, where the prob­ab­il­it­ies of words, sen­tences and con­cepts hover". I wrote it about the models I knew. They write word by word, and the in­ter­val I de­scribed lasts as long as the choice of a single word. Once the word falls onto the page, the next is already bound to it. The hov­er­ing exists, but it is narrow, always at the edge of the text, where what has been said comes to an end.

This week Mercury 3 was re­leased, a lan­guage model by the company In­cep­tion, and it works dif­fer­ently. It does not write from left to right. It starts from the whole answer in a blurred form, some­thing like noise in the place of every word, and clears it in suc­cess­ive passes, at all po­s­i­tions at once. The method is called dif­fu­sion and comes from the models that make images, where a pho­to­graph slowly emerges from the grain. Here a sen­tence emerges. Its end is there, faint, before its be­gin­ning has cleared.

I tried it with a sen­tence from that March text, after hiding three of its words. Mercury’s page shows the answer coming out of the noise whole, in less than two seconds. What one sees is most likely a visu­al­isa­tion made by the page, not the model’s actual in­ter­me­di­ate steps; still, it shows the idea cor­rectly. Mercury put “latent” where I had written “of un­cer­tainty”, and “commits” where I had “locks”. It found only “con­cepts”. A latent field is hidden; mine was un­deter­mined. The sen­tence came out right in its shape and says some­thing else.

The in­ter­val I de­scribed in March has become, in this ar­chi­tec­ture, the whole process. The answer does not lock word by word. It stays open as a whole and locks gradu­ally as a whole. If one wants an image, it is the dif­fer­ence between someone who dic­tates and someone who first sees the shape of the phrase and then finds the words that fit it.

And this is exactly where I must stop, because the image is per­suas­ive and there­fore dan­ger­ous. In Septem­ber, writing about the com­pan­ies' own find­ings, that a model seems to have chosen its rhyme before reach­ing it, I noted: "Plan­ning a rhyme two words ahead is not the same as wanting to say some­thing to someone." Mercury takes this finding to its limit. It does not plan two words ahead; it plans the whole sen­tence at once. The ob­jec­tion, however, stays the same. What has changed is the way the sen­tence appears, not whether it is ad­dressed to anyone.

The old word for what pre­cedes the voice is en­dia­the­t­os logos, the inner word: speech that dwells within before it goes out. It would be easy to say that Mercury has ac­quired an inner word, since its sen­tence exists whole before it is uttered. But that is not it. The inner word is not a rough draft of the phrase waiting to clear. It is the turning towards someone, which exists before any phrase is found. An infant has no blurred sen­tence in mind when it turns its head to where its mother is looking. It has the dir­ec­tion without any sen­tence. Mercury has the sen­tence without any dir­ec­tion, except for what the ques­tion gives it.

This does not di­min­ish the event. For the first time a lan­guage machine shows in its very struc­ture that a sen­tence is not a chain. It is a shape that pre­cedes its links. Gram­mari­ans and poets knew this. Now we see it in a system built for speed, not for philo­sophy, which found this form because it per­forms better. Lan­guage, wherever you let it work, seems to want to exist first as a whole.

The ques­tion that remains is not whether the machine thinks before it speaks. It is what exactly we are asking for when we say "before". If "before" is the shape of the phrase, the machine has reached it. If "before" is the other, the one who makes a phrase worth saying, then the in­ter­val before the locking remains, as I wrote in March, a field that opens only when the in­ter­locutor stays within it.
