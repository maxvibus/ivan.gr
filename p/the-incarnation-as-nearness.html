The Incarnation as Nearness

November 4, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

From the series “Theo­logy of the Logos and of Ex­ist­ence”

Pre­fat­ory note

This text comes from a broader theo­lo­gic­al and philo­soph­ic­al inquiry into the Logos as a place of pres­ence.
It does not examine the doc­trine of the In­carn­a­tion his­tor­ic­ally, but on­to­lo­gic­ally:
how divine pres­ence can be un­der­stood as a way of being near, rather than as a tran­scend­ent ex­cep­tion.
This thought does not negate faith, but deepens it:
God does not “descend” only once in history;
he des­cends whenev­er a human being accepts being present.

---

Text

For cen­tur­ies, theo­logy learned to place God on high and hu­man­ity on earth.
Faith was ex­pressed as an upward journey,
as a desire for union with the light that lies beyond the world.
Yet within this theo­lo­gic­al ori­ent­a­tion,
the world and the body often re­mained mere scenery:
a place of trial, not a place of divine pres­ence.

The In­carn­a­tion, as the new theo­logy of the Logos reads it,
is not merely an event that happened in history,
but a form of ex­ist­ence.
It is not a matter of God be­com­ing human once,
but of God con­sent­ing to be near at every moment,
to be present within the ma­ter­i­al­ity of cre­ation.

The Logos made flesh does not belong to the past;
it is God's en­dur­ing pos­sib­il­ity of be­com­ing visible within the human.
Every gaze, every touch, every moment of true pres­ence
is a form of in­carn­a­tion.
Thus, “let there be light” was not spoken only at the be­gin­ning of time;
it is spoken con­tinu­ally, within the rhythm of every person who sees and loves.

This turn has pro­found ex­ist­en­tial sig­ni­fic­ance.
The philo­sophy of ex­ist­ence taught us to see the human being
as a being called to stand alone before the unknown.
Here, however, God is not absent in order to put us to the test;
he is present in order to exist with us.
His silence is not a sign of with­draw­al,
but a space that allows the human to breathe.
The re­la­tion­ship is no longer an effort towards union,
but a shared breath within the same light.

That is why the flesh, once con­sidered the op­pos­ite of spirit,
is now re­cog­nised as a place where spirit is re­vealed.
Matter is not a barrier, but a medium.
The body is not a tem­por­ary shell,
but a par­ti­cipant in divine cre­ativ­ity itself.
When a human being touches, hears, sees,
divine pres­ence cir­cu­lates through their senses.
Near­ness becomes a way of sal­va­tion:
God saves because he draws near.

Cre­ation, then, is no longer a copy or a re­flec­tion,
but a con­tinu­ation of the same divine act.
Every form of art, every act of love, every word of truth,
is an ex­ten­sion of the In­carn­a­tion through time.
We do not draw near to God through these things;
God speaks through them.
Prayer and cre­ation,
silence and know­ledge,
become ex­pres­sions of the same event:
the Logos that never ceases to be born.

That is also why sal­va­tion is no longer a return upwards,
but an at­tent­ive dwell­ing in the present.
To re­cog­nise the sacred within the every­day,
to stand where you are,
and to un­der­stand that this “here”
is already the place of divine pres­ence.
You do not need to be else­where;
you need to be present.

Thus, the In­carn­a­tion becomes the name of divine near­ness.
God does not save us from a dis­tance;
he saves us because he lives with us.
The theo­logy of the Logos reveals
that the divine does not seek to dom­in­ate,
but to com­mu­nic­ate.
And whenev­er a human being stands with respect,
with at­tent­ive­ness, with kind­ness before another person,
there, perhaps without knowing it,
the Logos becomes flesh again.

---

Epi­logue

What was once called “God who des­cends”
can now be un­der­stood as
God who remains.
The In­carn­a­tion is no longer a miracle of the past,
but the every­day way in which Being
shows that it loves to exist to­geth­er.
