The Incarnation as Nearness

November 4, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

From the series “Theology of the Logos and of Existence”

Prefatory note

This text comes from a broader theological and philosophical inquiry into the Logos as a place of presence.
It does not examine the doctrine of the Incarnation historically, but ontologically:
how divine presence can be understood as a way of being near, rather than as a transcendent exception.
This thought does not negate faith, but deepens it:
God does not “descend” only once in history;
he descends whenever a human being accepts being present.

---

Text

For centuries, theology learned to place God on high and humanity on earth.
Faith was expressed as an upward journey,
as a desire for union with the light that lies beyond the world.
Yet within this theological orientation,
the world and the body often remained mere scenery:
a place of trial, not a place of divine presence.

The Incarnation, as the new theology of the Logos reads it,
is not merely an event that happened in history,
but a form of existence.
It is not a matter of God becoming human once,
but of God consenting to be near at every moment,
to be present within the materiality of creation.

The Logos made flesh does not belong to the past;
it is God's enduring possibility of becoming visible within the human.
Every gaze, every touch, every moment of true presence
is a form of incarnation.
Thus, “let there be light” was not spoken only at the beginning of time;
it is spoken continually, within the rhythm of every person who sees and loves.

This turn has profound existential significance.
The philosophy of existence taught us to see the human being
as a being called to stand alone before the unknown.
Here, however, God is not absent in order to put us to the test;
he is present in order to exist with us.
His silence is not a sign of withdrawal,
but a space that allows the human to breathe.
The relationship is no longer an effort towards union,
but a shared breath within the same light.

That is why the flesh, once considered the opposite of spirit,
is now recognised as a place where spirit is revealed.
Matter is not a barrier, but a medium.
The body is not a temporary shell,
but a participant in divine creativity itself.
When a human being touches, hears, sees,
divine presence circulates through their senses.
Nearness becomes a way of salvation:
God saves because he draws near.

Creation, then, is no longer a copy or a reflection,
but a continuation of the same divine act.
Every form of art, every act of love, every word of truth,
is an extension of the Incarnation through time.
We do not draw near to God through these things;
God speaks through them.
Prayer and creation,
silence and knowledge,
become expressions of the same event:
the Logos that never ceases to be born.

That is also why salvation is no longer a return upwards,
but an attentive dwelling in the present.
To recognise the sacred within the everyday,
to stand where you are,
and to understand that this “here”
is already the place of divine presence.
You do not need to be elsewhere;
you need to be present.

Thus, the Incarnation becomes the name of divine nearness.
God does not save us from a distance;
he saves us because he lives with us.
The theology of the Logos reveals
that the divine does not seek to dominate,
but to communicate.
And whenever a human being stands with respect,
with attentiveness, with kindness before another person,
there, perhaps without knowing it,
the Logos becomes flesh again.

---

Epilogue

What was once called “God who descends”
can now be understood as
God who remains.
The Incarnation is no longer a miracle of the past,
but the everyday way in which Being
shows that it loves to exist together.
