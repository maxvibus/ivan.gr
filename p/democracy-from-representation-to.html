Democracy: From Representation to Arbitrary Domination

March 8, 2026

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

Political representation begins with a subtle gesture: the citizen silently says, “I trust you to act for me.” At that moment, representation is born; but something else begins at the same time. An almost invisible shift, in which the representative translates trust into power — against the very person who originally entrusted them with it.

The delegation that defines this relationship is not merely a transfer of a right. It is an act of trust: I trust you to act for me when I cannot be present. I do not relinquish my will — I extend it through you. The citizen remains its bearer; the representative is an instrument, not an heir.

The problem arises from a different reading of the same gesture. Where the citizen sees trust, the representative may see freedom of action. “You act in my name” is transformed into “I have the right to act as I see fit.” The shift is small in language; enormous in practice.

This transformation requires no rupture. It is facilitated by the very structure of power: the authority defined by institutions, the speed of decision-making imposed by circumstances, the autonomy that arises from the daily exercise of a role. The representative begins to understand their position as power in its own right. And then the asymmetry is born: the citizen thinks in terms of a relationship; the institution sees power.

The paradox is that this transformation takes place amid the language of trust. Institutions continue to speak of a popular mandate and democratic legitimacy. Trust is invoked; its function has been lost. “You act for me” has become “you act upon me” — without a single word in the contract having changed.

This is the invisible scandal: no law is needed to say, “now you have power.” It happens silently, within the game of democratic representation, leaving no traces. Democracy was founded on the first act — trust. Its crisis begins when the second passes unnoticed.
