The Ontology of the Earthquake: Present-Absent in the Interval

September 10, 2025 · 6 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

An earth­quake is not merely a natural phe­nomen­on. It is not only the shaking of the ground, the move­ment of litho­spher­ic plates, the release of stresses ac­cu­mu­lated deep within the earth. It is also — and above all — a rev­el­a­tion. Vi­ol­ently and in­es­cap­ably, it reveals that what we regard as the most self-evident found­a­tion of our lives, the earth that holds us, is never en­tirely safe, never un­shak­able. The earth­quake is the moment when the found­a­tion ceases to be taken for granted, when our pres­ence becomes visible as some­thing held in sus­pense.

This ex­per­i­ence is not new to hu­man­ity. Since ancient times, earth­quakes have been re­garded as signs from the gods, pun­ish­ments or warn­ings. In the Bible, earth­quakes often ac­com­pany the great theo­phanies — at Sinai, at the Cru­ci­fix­ion, at the Re­sur­rec­tion. In myth­o­lo­gies, the earth­quake is the wrath of the Titans or the unrest of sub­ter­ranean forces. Even in the modern age, when earth­quakes are ex­plained sci­en­tific­ally, they remain an ex­per­i­ence that exceeds a simple phys­ic­al de­scrip­tion. Why? Because they touch the very on­to­logy of ex­ist­ence.

An earth­quake fright­ens us not because build­ings move, but because it reveals some­thing deeper: that our very pres­ence is never fully groun­ded. That we always live in a state of sus­pense, as if in­hab­it­ing a world that could dis­ap­pear at any moment. The earth­quake is not merely a “phys­ic­al tremor”; it is the symbol and testi­mony that ex­ist­ence is “born absent-present” — present, yet always under the shadow of absence.

The earthquake as an interval

To put it in philo­soph­ic­al terms, the earth­quake reveals the in­ter­val between being and non-being. Tra­di­tion­ally, thinkers have stood on one side or the other. Par­men­ides pro­hib­ited all thought of non-being: “what is not can neither be thought nor spoken”. Plato trans­formed it into “the other”, oth­er­ness, so that it would not remain wholly outside meaning. Ar­is­totle con­fined it to the notion of “po­ten­ti­al­ity”, a pos­sib­il­ity await­ing ac­tu­al­isa­tion. For the Church Fathers, noth­ing­ness became the back­ground of cre­ation “out of nothing”, but only so that God's cre­at­ive power might shine more brightly. In modern philo­sophy, Heide­g­ger brought “noth­ing­ness” to the fore as an element of dread and as the horizon of Being-towards-death. Sartre saw it as the neg­at­iv­ity that grounds freedom.

Yet in all these ap­proaches, non-being remains either neg­a­tion or threat, or a start­ing point left behind. It does not become a place of re­cep­tion; it is not re­cog­nised as a con­di­tion that can be borne. Here lies the earth­quake's sur­prise: it shows that the found­a­tion of pres­ence is not stable, but an en­dur­ance of non-being. The earth does not cease to exist when it shakes; rather, it reveals that its sta­bil­ity is always in sus­pense. So too with ex­ist­ence: it does not cease when tested, but reveals that its pres­ence co­hab­its with the pos­sib­il­ity of absence.

The experience of being born “absent-present”

Here we en­counter the deepest ex­ist­en­tial ex­per­i­ence: to live means to live within the pos­sib­il­ity of your life not being real­ised. We do not live only with the fear of death in the future; we also live with the trace of “I might never have existed”. Our ex­ist­ence carries the shadow of non-being towards both past and future.

This becomes es­pe­cially ap­par­ent in the ex­per­i­ence of ad­op­tion. The child born without ac­cept­ance being assured carries within them the sense that their pres­ence is already wounded by absence. This is not merely a psy­cho­lo­gic­al matter, but an on­to­lo­gic­al one: ex­ist­ence is ex­per­i­enced from the outset as “present-absent”. And the whole of life becomes a con­tinu­al ne­go­ti­ation of this sus­pen­ded con­di­tion: how to stand in a world that holds and rejects you at the same time.

The earth­quake reveals to every­one what the adopted child knows ex­ist­en­tially: that pres­ence cannot be taken for granted. For a few seconds, every­one lives the same ex­per­i­ence: “we might not exist”. This shared ex­per­i­ence gives rise to a sense of “to­geth­er” that is not a social con­ven­tion, but an ex­ist­en­tial kinship.

A theological reading

The theo­logy of the Fathers spoke of cre­ation “out of nothing”. God brought the world into ex­ist­ence not from some pre-ex­ist­ing matter, but from noth­ing­ness. This truth pre­serves cre­ation's ab­so­lute de­pend­ence on the Creator. Yet tra­di­tion often thought of noth­ing­ness only as a past that had been over­come: once there was no world, God created, and now it exists.

The earth­quake forces us to see dif­fer­ently: noth­ing­ness is not merely yes­ter­day; it is now. Cre­ation always remains created, which means always held in sus­pense before noth­ing­ness. The fact that the earth shakes shows that cre­ation is not a given, but a con­tinu­al en­dur­ance of non-being. Thus God appears not only as a Creator who acted once, but as the One who con­tinu­ally bears our non-being so that we may exist.

This trans­forms the very concept of God. He is not only the God of power, cause and cre­ation. He is also the God of pa­tience — the One who “bears with” our non-pres­ence, who does not an­ni­hil­ate us, who keeps ex­ist­ence open.

The philosophical dimension

Viewed philo­soph­ic­ally, the earth­quake dis­solves the cer­tainty of the found­a­tion. Par­men­ides wanted to es­tab­lish being as im­mov­able. The earth­quake demon­strates that there is no such im­mov­ab­il­ity. The ground, the very em­bod­i­ment of sta­bil­ity, becomes fluid. Being, there­fore, is founded not on sta­bil­ity but on con­tinu­al sus­pense.

Heide­g­ger spoke of Dasein as “Being-towards-death”. But the earth­quake shows that the sus­pen­ded con­di­tion of ex­ist­ence con­cerns not only death in the future; it also con­cerns the present, even the past. We do not merely live towards death; we always live at the bound­ary where our ex­ist­ence might never have existed. This threefold di­men­sion — past, present, future — con­sti­tutes the on­to­logy of sus­pense.

Sartre saw noth­ing­ness as a neg­a­tion that grounds freedom. Here, however, this is not a neg­a­tion uttered by the subject, but the very struc­ture of the world: the ground, the earth, the pres­ence of others, all testify that being is always pre­cari­ous.

The anthropological dimension

An­thro­po­logy knows that natural dis­asters give birth to com­munit­ies. After earth­quakes or floods, people often show greater solid­ar­ity. They share fears, help one another, rebuild. But some­thing deeper is at work here: this com­munity is not only a social phe­nomen­on; it bears witness to a shared on­to­lo­gic­al con­di­tion. We are all born “absent-present”. The earth­quake simply reveals this uni­ver­sally, if only for a few moments.

By con­trast, social cel­eb­ra­tions such as wed­dings try to create “to­geth­er­ness” founded on pres­ence taken for granted. People dance, sing, affirm the com­munity. But whoever looks deeper sees that behind the dance lies each person's secret solitude, a solitude so pure that if you speak of it to another, they are frightened. In an earth­quake, by con­trast, this solitude is openly re­vealed: every­one alone, every­one to­geth­er.

The absolute experience

Here we reach the ques­tion: do we thereby live the ab­so­lute ex­per­i­ence? The answer is yes, insofar as “ab­so­lute ex­per­i­ence” means in­hab­it­ing the very struc­ture of ex­ist­ence. Not merely having ex­per­i­ences — joys, sorrows, events — but real­ising that your ex­per­i­ence happens in sus­pense before the pos­sib­il­ity of nothing at all. When you inhabit this fissure, ex­per­i­ence ceases to be a mere suc­ces­sion of events and becomes testi­mony to ex­ist­ence itself.

This testi­mony is rare, because most people live “being” as some­thing stable. Society builds in­sti­tu­tions and cel­eb­ra­tions in order to forget this sus­pense. The earth­quake, however, reveals it. And whoever has the courage to remain there, to inhabit it, knows the ab­so­lute ex­per­i­ence: living while knowing that you might never have lived at all.
