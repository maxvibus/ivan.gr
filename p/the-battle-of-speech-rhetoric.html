The Battle of Speech: Rhetoric, Belittlement, and True Freedom

Published on March 10, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original


Speech is a bat­tle­field. Not only in public dis­putes, but even in every­day com­mu­nic­a­tion. In the Greek tra­di­tion, rhet­or­ic was never merely the art of per­sua­sion—it was the art of speak­ing mean­ing­fully, speak­ing to reveal rather than conceal.

1. Rhetoric: The Art of the Word, Not of Manipulation

The root of rhet­or­ic lies in the word “rhētos”, meaning clearly ex­pressed, in­dis­put­able.

  • Rhēto → Some­thing spoken with au­thor­ity, some­thing that holds true.
  • Rhētē → Clear ex­pres­sion, leaving no room for doubt.
  • Rhēsē → A phrase pre­served as a dis­tinct­ive for­mu­la­tion, with sig­ni­fic­ance beyond its mere ut­ter­ance.

Thus, rhet­or­ic is not a tech­nique be­long­ing to politi­cians or in­tel­lec­tu­als. It is how human beings struc­ture their speech to convey thought, truth, and feeling.

Yet in con­tem­por­ary Greek life, rhet­or­ic has become a weapon of fear, be­little­ment, and swift dom­in­a­tion.

2. The New Rhetoric of Belittlement

Today, people do not discuss things to un­der­stand; they discuss them to prevail.

Greek rhet­or­ic is no longer used to support thought, but to instil doubt in the other person.

They do not say:

  • “You made a mistake.”
  • “I dis­agree with you.”
  • “Why do you believe that?”

They throw traps of doubt at you:

  • “Is that what you learned at school?”
  • “Is that what the Hymn to Liberty says?”
  • “Did you think you could tell me why you dis­ap­peared?”

In truth, they do not want a dis­cus­sion.
They want to make you ques­tion your own ability to answer.

And when that does not work, when they see you are not falling into their trap, their last line of defence is be­little­ment:

  • “Forget it, you don't un­der­stand.”
  • “There's no point dis­cuss­ing it.”
  • “All right, all right, leave it.”

This is not rhet­or­ic.
This is fear dis­guised as clev­erness.

3. Why Rhetoric Is Not Only for Orators

Rhet­or­ic is not the priv­ilege of those who speak beau­ti­fully.
Rhet­or­ic belongs neither to politi­cians nor to aca­dem­ics.
Rhet­or­ic is the natural tool of a think­ing human being.

And here lies the great mis­un­der­stand­ing:

True rhet­or­ic does not fear being ques­tioned, because it has no need to defend itself through be­little­ment.

4. Praise or Defeat?

The Greek defence of speech, once groun­ded in the search for truth, has now taken two paths:

  1. Praise—where people seek not truth but af­firm­a­tion. They want to hear that they are right, not to develop their think­ing.
  2. Defeat—where dis­cus­sion is not about build­ing some­thing but about pro­du­cing a winner and a loser.

But true rhet­or­ic is neither praise nor defeat.
It is the en­dur­ance to stand by your thought without fearing the process.

5. What Truly Defending Speech Means

If defence is the need to dom­in­ate another person, then it is a product of fear.
If defence is the strength to live up to your thought, then it is an act of freedom.

Rhet­or­ic is not a battle over who can speak the loudest.
It is the art of stand­ing your ground without being brought to your knees by the need to be right.

6. Freedom's Epilogue

If freedom fears being ques­tioned, what exactly does it lib­er­ate?
If Greek rhet­or­ic fears dia­logue, what exactly does it defend?

The answer is not in the noise of words.
It lies in the silence of someone who un­der­stands but does not need to win.

How to Stand in the Battle of Language without Being Trapped

➡ When they throw a loaded ques­tion at you, do not rush to answer.
➡ Turn the mirror back on them: “Do I not un­der­stand, or do you not want to explain?”
➡ If they came for dia­logue, they will con­tin­ue.
➡ If they came to be­little, they will leave of their own accord.

Because the strongest answer is not defence.
It is not re­ac­tion.
It is not victory on their terms.

It is the aware­ness that their game no longer has power over you.
