The Inner Space of Presence: A Dwelling for the Self

August 28, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

There is a moment in human experience when we realise that we are not merely a present body or an outward presence in the world; that there is an “inside” to us, an inner place where we truly dwell. This space is not merely psychological or symbolic; it is a space of presence and calm, given by life and at the same time inhabitable by our own self.

The experience of being “from within” means that we are not defined solely by interaction with the outside world, or by the eyes of others. Instead, the self becomes a place, and our awareness becomes a dwelling — a quiet refuge amid the ceaseless noise of external events. In this space, serenity is not passive absence, but active, conscious presence: we know ourselves, recognise the value of action and effort, and do not depend on others' validation or recognition.

In Eastern philosophy, this inhabiting of inner space is connected with the notion of the “nature of mind”, as described in Buddhism and Zen. The mind observes, becomes calm and experiences without identifying with external disturbances. The space within us becomes the place where inner life emerges inviolable and uninterrupted, unaffected by the uncertainty of events or the intentions of others.

Stoic thinkers likewise speak of an “inner refuge”, where virtue and reason determine peace of mind. The harshness or injustice of the outside world cannot take away the calm that comes from self-awareness. Our space within ourselves is a point of autonomy, where happiness is not the product of external validation, but an expression of inner consistency and self-knowledge.

In contemporary phenomenology, the space of presence is the personal horizon of experience, where the self is experienced as embodied in the world, yet at the same time independent of objective interaction. Inhabiting inner space means seeing the world and others through our own feelings and consciousness, without losing authenticity or becoming instruments of external reality.

This space is given within us; we do not need to create it from nothing or have anyone else validate it. Discovering it brings conscious strength and serenity, but also a reminder that the truth of our inner dwelling requires courage: remaining sensitive and authentic even when others' injustice or harshness causes us pain.

Inhabiting the self is not an escape from the world; it is responsible participation from a point of full presence, where love, attention and self-knowledge coexist without dependencies or fears. And perhaps, ultimately, this sense of the “inside” — the place where we truly are — is the deepest possibility of happiness and freedom a human being can experience.
