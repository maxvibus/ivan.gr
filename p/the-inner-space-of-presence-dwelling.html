The Inner Space of Presence: A Dwelling for the Self

Published on August 28, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

There is a moment in human ex­per­i­ence when we realise that we are not merely a present body or an outward pres­ence in the world; that there is an “inside” to us, an inner place where we truly dwell. This space is not merely psy­cho­lo­gic­al or sym­bol­ic; it is a space of pres­ence and calm, given by life and at the same time in­hab­it­able by our own self.

The ex­per­i­ence of being “from within” means that we are not defined solely by in­ter­ac­tion with the outside world, or by the eyes of others. Instead, the self becomes a place, and our aware­ness becomes a dwell­ing — a quiet refuge amid the cease­less noise of ex­tern­al events. In this space, serenity is not passive absence, but active, con­scious pres­ence: we know ourselves, re­cog­nise the value of action and effort, and do not depend on others' val­id­a­tion or re­cog­ni­tion.

In Eastern philo­sophy, this in­hab­it­ing of inner space is con­nec­ted with the notion of the “nature of mind”, as de­scribed in Buddhism and Zen. The mind ob­serves, becomes calm and ex­per­i­ences without identi­fy­ing with ex­tern­al dis­turb­ances. The space within us becomes the place where inner life emerges in­vi­ol­able and un­in­ter­rup­ted, un­af­fected by the un­cer­tainty of events or the in­ten­tions of others.

Stoic thinkers like­wise speak of an “inner refuge”, where virtue and reason de­term­ine peace of mind. The harsh­ness or in­justice of the outside world cannot take away the calm that comes from self-aware­ness. Our space within ourselves is a point of autonomy, where hap­pi­ness is not the product of ex­tern­al val­id­a­tion, but an ex­pres­sion of inner con­sist­ency and self-know­ledge.

In con­tem­por­ary phe­nomen­o­logy, the space of pres­ence is the per­son­al horizon of ex­per­i­ence, where the self is ex­per­i­enced as em­bod­ied in the world, yet at the same time in­de­pend­ent of ob­ject­ive in­ter­ac­tion. In­hab­it­ing inner space means seeing the world and others through our own feel­ings and con­scious­ness, without losing au­then­ti­city or be­com­ing in­stru­ments of ex­tern­al reality.

This space is given within us; we do not need to create it from nothing or have anyone else val­id­ate it. Dis­cov­er­ing it brings con­scious strength and serenity, but also a re­mind­er that the truth of our inner dwell­ing re­quires courage: re­main­ing sens­it­ive and au­then­t­ic even when others' in­justice or harsh­ness causes us pain.

In­hab­it­ing the self is not an escape from the world; it is re­spons­ible par­ti­cip­a­tion from a point of full pres­ence, where love, at­ten­tion and self-know­ledge coexist without de­pend­en­cies or fears. And perhaps, ul­ti­mately, this sense of the “inside” — the place where we truly are — is the deepest pos­sib­il­ity of hap­pi­ness and freedom a human being can ex­per­i­ence.
