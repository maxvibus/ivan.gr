Meaning, Name, Presence: Awareness through Language and Divine Tenderness

March 5, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

Language is not merely a tool of communication—it is an act of existence. When we give something a name, we do not simply describe it; we call it into presence. The Greek language, as it has taken shape over time, does not function only as a description of reality—it is a system for the revelation of meaning that already exists.

What follows is an exploration of this way of thinking, which runs through neurophysiology, psychology and sociology, illuminated by the quietest and simplest form of divine tenderness—the kind that does not clash, but spreads gently, without claiming, without imposing, yet without withdrawing.

1. A Name Is Not Merely a Title — It Is an Invitation into Presence

When we speak of a name, we usually think of the names given to people, places or objects. Yet a name is something much deeper. It is not merely a label—it is a way to call a meaning to stand before us.

  • When a being gives something a name, it calls it into existence within the mind.
  • When something acquires meaning, this does not simply mean that we understand it, but that we recognise it as something that stands on its own.
  • Presence is not something abstract—it is what presents itself, what stands, what has substance within us.

This is why, in the Greek language, words are not arbitrary.

  • Omma (ὄμμα) — The eye, the gaze, active seeing itself, not merely the biological function.
  • Histamai — Not simply “I stand,” but I exist fully, without any need for validation.
  • Deixis — The direction towards meaning. Not simply “signalling,” but the path that leads to something already there, before we utter it.

2. The Neurophysiology of Presence

The science of mind and body shows that awareness and calm are not merely mental states—they are neural realities.

  • The brain contains structures that strengthen conscious awareness (such as the prefrontal cortex and the self-perception network).
  • Calm is not inactivity—it is a dynamic attunement of our nervous system to a state of presence.
  • The body perceives presence before the mind names it. This explains why some people calm us simply through their presence, without needing to speak.

3. The Psychology of Equanimity and Divine Tenderness

In psychology, equanimity does not mean apathy—it means quietness within the knowledge that nothing can take our essence away from us.

  • Tenderness is both a concept and an act. It is not merely an emotion—it is a manifestation of inner peace.
  • Anxiety arises from a sense of inadequacy. The thought “perhaps it was not good enough” or “perhaps I could have done it better” can be either a burden or an act of pure love for what is beautiful.
  • Divine courage is not aggression—it is standing present, with honour, respect and joy together.

4. The Sociology of Presence: Why Did Divestment Become Associated with Violence?

The Greek word ekpoiisi, divestment, now signifies forced loss; but when we examine its structure, we find something else:

  • “Ek” → Movement outwards, manifestation, liberation.
  • “Poiisis” → Not only creation, but act, manifestation, bringing something into existence.

Thus, ekpoiisi means bringing something forth, revealing it, making it manifest.

How, then, did divestment become a violent act?

  • When liberation was distorted into forced removal.
  • When the act of revelation became a mechanism of coercion.
  • When the gift of beauty and tenderness was regarded as weakness.

In truth, nothing is taken from us unless we surrender our presence.

This is why our true property is not material things, but our presence.
To dwell is not simply to have a house, but to exist within the quietness of being.

Epilogue: Action and Acting Together as a Path towards Stillness

If divestment has been distorted into violence, then acting together is the answer.
Not as forced submission, but as a free union directed towards what is beautiful and simple.

  • Action is not blind activity, but the effortless manifestation of beauty.
  • Acting together is not compulsory union, but free harmony among those who are aware of their presence.
  • The purpose is neither excessive analysis nor absolute inaction. It is the simplicity of awareness’s natural flow.

The Greek language does not merely describe the world—it brings it into existence.
And when concepts become clear, the soul rests.

Thus, this text was not written in order to explain.
It was written to let something stand.
To reveal rather than prove.
To spread quietly through those who read it rather than press upon them.

For the point is not to persuade—it is to exist.
