Meaning, Name, Presence: Awareness through Language and Divine Tenderness

March 5, 2025

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Language is not merely a tool of communication—it is an act of existence. When we give something a name, we do not simply describe it; we call it into presence. The Greek language, as it has taken shape over time, does not function only as a description of reality—it is a system for the revelation of meaning that already exists.

What follows is an ex­plor­a­tion of this way of think­ing, which runs through neuro­physiology, psy­cho­logy and so­ci­ology, il­lu­min­ated by the quietest and simplest form of divine ten­der­ness—the kind that does not clash, but spreads gently, without claim­ing, without im­pos­ing, yet without with­draw­ing.

1. A Name Is Not Merely a Title — It Is an Invitation into Presence

When we speak of a name, we usually think of the names given to people, places or objects. Yet a name is some­thing much deeper. It is not merely a label—it is a way to call a meaning to stand before us.

  • When a being gives some­thing a name, it calls it into ex­ist­ence within the mind.
  • When some­thing ac­quires meaning, this does not simply mean that we un­der­stand it, but that we re­cog­nise it as some­thing that stands on its own.
  • Pres­ence is not some­thing ab­stract—it is what presents itself, what stands, what has sub­stance within us.

This is why, in the Greek lan­guage, words are not ar­bit­rary.

  • Omma (ὄμμα) — The eye, the gaze, active seeing itself, not merely the bio­lo­gic­al func­tion.
  • Histamai — Not simply “I stand,” but I exist fully, without any need for val­id­a­tion.
  • Deixis — The dir­ec­tion towards meaning. Not simply “sig­nalling,” but the path that leads to some­thing already there, before we utter it.

2. The Neurophysiology of Presence

The science of mind and body shows that aware­ness and calm are not merely mental states—they are neural real­it­ies.

  • The brain con­tains struc­tures that strengthen con­scious aware­ness (such as the pre­front­al cortex and the self-per­cep­tion network).
  • Calm is not in­activ­ity—it is a dynamic at­tun­e­ment of our nervous system to a state of pres­ence.
  • The body per­ceives pres­ence before the mind names it. This ex­plains why some people calm us simply through their pres­ence, without needing to speak.

3. The Psychology of Equanimity and Divine Tenderness

In psy­cho­logy, equan­im­ity does not mean apathy—it means quiet­ness within the know­ledge that nothing can take our essence away from us.

  • Ten­der­ness is both a concept and an act. It is not merely an emotion—it is a mani­fest­a­tion of inner peace.
  • Anxiety arises from a sense of in­ad­equacy. The thought “perhaps it was not good enough” or “perhaps I could have done it better” can be either a burden or an act of pure love for what is beau­ti­ful.
  • Divine courage is not ag­gres­sion—it is stand­ing present, with honour, respect and joy to­geth­er.

4. The Sociology of Presence: Why Did Divestment Become Associated with Violence?

The Greek word ek­poiisi, di­vest­ment, now sig­ni­fies forced loss; but when we examine its struc­ture, we find some­thing else:

  • “Ek” → Move­ment out­wards, mani­fest­a­tion, lib­er­a­tion.
  • “Poiisis” → Not only cre­ation, but act, mani­fest­a­tion, bring­ing some­thing into ex­ist­ence.

Thus, ek­poiisi means bring­ing some­thing forth, re­veal­ing it, making it mani­fest.

How, then, did di­vest­ment become a violent act?

  • When lib­er­a­tion was dis­tor­ted into forced removal.
  • When the act of rev­el­a­tion became a mech­an­ism of co­er­cion.
  • When the gift of beauty and ten­der­ness was re­garded as weak­ness.

In truth, nothing is taken from us unless we sur­render our pres­ence.

This is why our true prop­erty is not ma­ter­i­al things, but our pres­ence.
To dwell is not simply to have a house, but to exist within the quiet­ness of being.

Epilogue: Action and Acting Together as a Path towards Stillness

If di­vest­ment has been dis­tor­ted into vi­ol­ence, then acting to­geth­er is the answer.
Not as forced sub­mis­sion, but as a free union dir­ec­ted towards what is beau­ti­ful and simple.

  • Action is not blind activ­ity, but the ef­fort­less mani­fest­a­tion of beauty.
  • Acting to­geth­er is not com­puls­ory union, but free harmony among those who are aware of their pres­ence.
  • The purpose is neither ex­cess­ive ana­lys­is nor ab­so­lute in­ac­tion. It is the sim­pli­city of aware­ness’s natural flow.

The Greek lan­guage does not merely de­scribe the world—it brings it into ex­ist­ence.
And when con­cepts become clear, the soul rests.

Thus, this text was not written in order to explain.
It was written to let some­thing stand.
To reveal rather than prove.
To spread quietly through those who read it rather than press upon them.

For the point is not to per­suade—it is to exist.
