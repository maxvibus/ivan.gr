The Cornerstone: Kapodistrias’s Overlooked Proposal for a National Bank

July 7, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

A bank, as we understand it today, appears to be a commercial instrument: money management, lending, interest rates, investment products. Yet in its original conception — and particularly in the words of Ioannis Kapodistrias — the idea of a bank was not synonymous with that of the market. It was something much deeper: an institution of trust.

In 1828, a few months after arriving in Greece as Governor, Ioannis Kapodistrias wrote to political and financial allies in Geneva. In that letter, he explicitly referred to the creation of a “new National Bank”, which he called:

“the cornerstone of our social edifice”.

This phrase is not rhetorical. It is foundational. Kapodistrias was not proposing a banking arrangement to facilitate transactions; he was proposing an institution that would establish collective trust in a devastated economy, a society torn apart by war, and a state that was only just coming into being.

Kapodistrias’s proposal came at a time when there were no stable public revenues, no domestic capital, and no institutional banking culture. Yet he envisioned a bank that would belong to the nation, not to the few; that would support society, not vested interests.

His idea was not technical: it was political. The Bank was not a business, but a foundation of the State. It would operate in terms of shared faith, not profit. That faith was not financial; it was institutional, ethical, historical.

The later National Bank, founded in 1841, followed a different course: shareholders, commercial logic, private capital. The bank gradually became what we know today: an instrument of the market. But Kapodistrias’s phrase remains there, in an almost forgotten letter, like a prelude to an institution that could have been different.

The cornerstone Kapodistrias imagined was not the accounting basis of commerce. It was the basis of social cohesion. And this is the forgotten vision behind the word “National”. Perhaps the time has come to remember it again.
