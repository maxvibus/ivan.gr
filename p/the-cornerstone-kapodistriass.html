The Cornerstone: Kapodistrias’s Overlooked Proposal for a National Bank

Published on July 7, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

A bank, as we un­der­stand it today, appears to be a com­mer­cial in­stru­ment: money man­age­ment, lending, in­terest rates, in­vest­ment products. Yet in its ori­gin­al con­cep­tion — and par­tic­u­larly in the words of Ioannis Ka­pod­istri­as — the idea of a bank was not syn­onym­ous with that of the market. It was some­thing much deeper: an in­sti­tu­tion of trust.

In 1828, a few months after ar­riv­ing in Greece as Gov­ernor, Ioannis Ka­pod­istri­as wrote to polit­ic­al and fin­an­cial allies in Geneva. In that letter, he ex­pli­citly re­ferred to the cre­ation of a “new Na­tion­al Bank”, which he called:

“the corner­stone of our social edifice”.

This phrase is not rhet­or­ic­al. It is found­a­tion­al. Ka­pod­istri­as was not pro­pos­ing a banking ar­range­ment to fa­cil­it­ate trans­ac­tions; he was pro­pos­ing an in­sti­tu­tion that would es­tab­lish col­lect­ive trust in a dev­ast­ated economy, a society torn apart by war, and a state that was only just coming into being.

Ka­pod­istri­as’s pro­pos­al came at a time when there were no stable public rev­en­ues, no do­mest­ic capital, and no in­sti­tu­tion­al banking culture. Yet he en­vi­sioned a bank that would belong to the nation, not to the few; that would support society, not vested in­terests.

His idea was not tech­nic­al: it was polit­ic­al. The Bank was not a busi­ness, but a found­a­tion of the State. It would operate in terms of shared faith, not profit. That faith was not fin­an­cial; it was in­sti­tu­tion­al, ethical, his­tor­ic­al.

The later Na­tion­al Bank, founded in 1841, fol­lowed a dif­fer­ent course: share­hold­ers, com­mer­cial logic, private capital. The bank gradu­ally became what we know today: an in­stru­ment of the market. But Ka­pod­istri­as’s phrase remains there, in an almost for­got­ten letter, like a prelude to an in­sti­tu­tion that could have been dif­fer­ent.

The corner­stone Ka­pod­istri­as ima­gined was not the ac­count­ing basis of com­merce. It was the basis of social co­he­sion. And this is the for­got­ten vision behind the word “Na­tion­al”. Perhaps the time has come to re­mem­ber it again.
