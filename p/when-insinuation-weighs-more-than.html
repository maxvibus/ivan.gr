When Insinuation Weighs More Than the Answer: A Dialogue That Is Not a Dialogue

March 6, 2025 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original


Not all con­ver­sa­tions take place on equal terms. Not all words are spoken with the in­ten­tion of build­ing bridges. Some­times words are merely the outer shell of an at­ti­tude, of a dis­tance already es­tab­lished that goes un­ac­know­ledged as such, yet acts as a silent attack.

The dia­logue with Van­gel­is is not a simple con­ver­sa­tion between two friends. It is an in­ter­ac­tion that unfolds through delays, in­sinu­ations, half-answers, and an almost im­per­cept­ible power game. It is not about a need for un­der­stand­ing, but about re­fus­ing to grant the other person the right to clear com­mu­nic­a­tion.

Silence as a Message

When you ask, “Why do you message me late at night?”, you are not asking about the other person's habits. You are asking about in­ten­tion. Because the time someone chooses to reply is never ac­ci­dent­al. When someone re­sponds with vague excuses, when they imply that they “could give other reasons too” but do not name them, there is no dia­logue. There is a mech­an­ism of dis­tan­cing. A way of keeping the other person waiting, un­cer­tain, doubt­ful.

The message you sent was clear: “I want us to speak hon­estly, without in­sinu­ations, without games.” The answer you re­ceived, however, was not equally clear. It was full of si­lences, vague­ness, in­dir­ect ref­er­ences to a situ­ation that res­isted being named.

The Attitude That Says More Than Words

Van­gel­is knew that his silence would hurt you. He knew that if he did not reply im­me­di­ately, if he let time pass, he would create a void that your own ex­pect­a­tion would fill. And he did. Not out of malice, but out of a way of being that has learned to func­tion like this. To speak through dis­tance, to control through am­bi­gu­ity.

The moment you sent him your website was an open in­vit­a­tion: “Read me if you want to un­der­stand me.” But he did not accept that in­vit­a­tion as a bridge. He saw it as another element in the game. An “okay, I'm looking at it,” without really looking.

The Hidden Weight of Insinuations

The most in­si­di­ous thing in in­ter­ac­tions of this kind is not re­jec­tion. It is not even silence. It is in­sinu­ation. Those small, half-spoken remarks that make you ques­tion whether there really is a problem or whether you are the one ex­ag­ger­at­ing. When someone leaves the door open to “other reasons” without stating them, they are not in­vit­ing you to un­der­stand. They are trap­ping you in a search for what they might mean, making you doubt whether you really have a right to be upset.

This kind of com­mu­nic­a­tion is not clear. It does not come from the heart. It is a way of keeping the other person in a per­petu­al “maybe,” in con­stant doubt, without al­low­ing them to know where they really stand.

Presence Is Not a Negotiation

The greatest il­lu­sion such in­ter­ac­tions create is the feeling that you must earn the other person's pres­ence. That you must prove it is worth their while to listen to you, to read you, to give you a clear answer.

But the truth is simpler: You do not need to per­suade anyone to be present in your life. Whoever wants to be there is there. Without in­sinu­ations, without games, without silent pun­ish­ments.

And if someone chooses to reply only when it suits them, if they keep you at a dis­tance where you never know whether you are welcome or merely tol­er­ated, then the real answer does not lie in the dis­cus­sion. It lies in re­cog­niz­ing that you cannot force the truth out of someone who does not want to tell it.

The Silence That Is Not Presence

Com­mu­nic­a­tion is not a series of replies to mes­sages. It is a will­ing­ness to face the other person truth­fully. To leave no dark corners, no half-spoken words, no con­tra­dict­ory signals.

A genuine re­la­tion­ship is not a waiting game. It is a pres­ence that has no need for strategies. And if someone needs to leave gaps to feel they are in control, then the real ques­tion is not why they do it. It is why you should have to put up with it.

Being present is not an act of ne­go­ti­ation. It is a choice. And genuine com­mu­nic­a­tion never needs in­sinu­ation in order to exist.
