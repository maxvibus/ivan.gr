When Insinuation Weighs More Than the Answer: A Dialogue That Is Not a Dialogue

March 6, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original


Not all conversations take place on equal terms. Not all words are spoken with the intention of building bridges. Sometimes words are merely the outer shell of an attitude, of a distance already established that goes unacknowledged as such, yet acts as a silent attack.

The dialogue with Vangelis is not a simple conversation between two friends. It is an interaction that unfolds through delays, insinuations, half-answers, and an almost imperceptible power game. It is not about a need for understanding, but about refusing to grant the other person the right to clear communication.

Silence as a Message

When you ask, “Why do you message me late at night?”, you are not asking about the other person's habits. You are asking about intention. Because the time someone chooses to reply is never accidental. When someone responds with vague excuses, when they imply that they “could give other reasons too” but do not name them, there is no dialogue. There is a mechanism of distancing. A way of keeping the other person waiting, uncertain, doubtful.

The message you sent was clear: “I want us to speak honestly, without insinuations, without games.” The answer you received, however, was not equally clear. It was full of silences, vagueness, indirect references to a situation that resisted being named.

The Attitude That Says More Than Words

Vangelis knew that his silence would hurt you. He knew that if he did not reply immediately, if he let time pass, he would create a void that your own expectation would fill. And he did. Not out of malice, but out of a way of being that has learned to function like this. To speak through distance, to control through ambiguity.

The moment you sent him your website was an open invitation: “Read me if you want to understand me.” But he did not accept that invitation as a bridge. He saw it as another element in the game. An “okay, I'm looking at it,” without really looking.

The Hidden Weight of Insinuations

The most insidious thing in interactions of this kind is not rejection. It is not even silence. It is insinuation. Those small, half-spoken remarks that make you question whether there really is a problem or whether you are the one exaggerating. When someone leaves the door open to “other reasons” without stating them, they are not inviting you to understand. They are trapping you in a search for what they might mean, making you doubt whether you really have a right to be upset.

This kind of communication is not clear. It does not come from the heart. It is a way of keeping the other person in a perpetual “maybe,” in constant doubt, without allowing them to know where they really stand.

Presence Is Not a Negotiation

The greatest illusion such interactions create is the feeling that you must earn the other person's presence. That you must prove it is worth their while to listen to you, to read you, to give you a clear answer.

But the truth is simpler: You do not need to persuade anyone to be present in your life. Whoever wants to be there is there. Without insinuations, without games, without silent punishments.

And if someone chooses to reply only when it suits them, if they keep you at a distance where you never know whether you are welcome or merely tolerated, then the real answer does not lie in the discussion. It lies in recognizing that you cannot force the truth out of someone who does not want to tell it.

The Silence That Is Not Presence

Communication is not a series of replies to messages. It is a willingness to face the other person truthfully. To leave no dark corners, no half-spoken words, no contradictory signals.

A genuine relationship is not a waiting game. It is a presence that has no need for strategies. And if someone needs to leave gaps to feel they are in control, then the real question is not why they do it. It is why you should have to put up with it.

Being present is not an act of negotiation. It is a choice. And genuine communication never needs insinuation in order to exist.
