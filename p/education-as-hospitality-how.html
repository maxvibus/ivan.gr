Education as Hospitality: How the Educational Relationship Becomes a Place of Welcome Rather than Assimilation of the Other

July 15, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

True education does not seek to make you the same as everyone else. Its aim is not assimilation, but the recognition of a uniqueness that does not need to explain itself in order to endure. Hospitality, in its deepest form, does not say: “come into our space and become like us”, but “come as you are, and let the space take shape together with you”.

When the educational relationship functions as hospitality, it does not impose adaptation, but offers a space in which to remain. The child does not feel called upon to abandon their own language, their rhythm, their temperament, in order to learn. On the contrary, they feel that even their misspellings, their questions, their absence can find room in the place offered to them, not as tolerance but as recognition.

Education is not an ideological space of integration. It is a space of relationship. And for a relationship to exist, it needs a place of freedom—not of conformity. This means that acceptance precedes transformation. The child does not change in order to be loved, but is loved, and within that love their freedom to grow through education emerges.

Education as hospitality, then, accepts difference not as a problem, but as a revelation. The pupil who brings another language, another body, another rhythm is not required to “fit in”. The place of teaching stretches to make room for them. The community is not abolished—it is transformed by the person’s entry into it.

Where education offers hospitality rather than assimilation, the relationship becomes free; it is grounded not in control, but in recognition. Knowledge does not become a mechanism of integration, but a language of encounter. And difference is not restricted—it enlarges the whole.

There, the classroom ceases to be a place of assessment. It becomes a place of shared dwelling. And every child who enters knows that even if they change, they will change not because they were pressured, but because they were welcomed. And this is the essence of every formative process: not making the other like ourselves, but our encounter in the common place that comes into being because we were not afraid to remain different.
