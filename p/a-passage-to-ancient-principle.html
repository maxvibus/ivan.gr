The Journey of Thought: From Chaos to Grace — A Passage to the Ancient Principle

The word Chaos, though Greek, has acquired connotations of threat, instability, and uncertainty through modern usage. Yet in ancient Greek thought, Chaos was not necessarily something terrible. It meant the boundless, the open, the unborn field from which order arises. It was the absence of structure, but also the possibility for every form to exist.

If we wish to retain this sense without the frightening dimension it has acquired, we can use a gentler and more encompassing term, such as:

  • The Unmanifest → emphasizes the unexplored dimension before clarity.
  • The Formless → denotes form that has not yet manifested but holds potential.
  • The Indeterminate Beginning → suggests not something negative, but an initial state before expression.
  • The Undefined → relates to thought’s open horizon before it takes a definite shape.
  • The Infinite → a word pointing to what has not yet been confined to forms.

Thought does not begin with absolute knowledge, but with an initial state of indeterminacy, in which concepts have not yet taken shape. What we often call chaos is not chaotic disorder in the negative sense, but the open space of possibility, the womb of every new form of thought. In the ancient Greek worldview, chaos was not a threat, but the origin of all coming-into-being, the point before the world’s differentiation into light and darkness, order and harmony.

Thus, the journey of thought is not an escape from chaos as something negative, but a passage from the undefined to the clear, from the formless to grace. It is not a battle against chaos, but a natural movement toward the revelation of what is true.

This passage is the very process of seeking: from initial uncertainty toward awareness, from unseen possibility toward action.

Thought is not a static process, but a continuous inner movement that generates, overturns, and reshapes our perception of the world. The Greeks expressed it not as a mere intellectual function, but as an inner geometry of the mind—a movement possessing rhythm, proportion, conflict, and catharsis.

What Does a Geometry of Thought Mean?

Geometry is order within movement, form emerging from conflict. In thought, geometry is not merely an analogy, but a sense of inner order that lends rhythm to intellectual experience. The Greeks saw thought as a structure of analogy, even when it seemed chaotic.

When we think, we do not generate meanings at random, but connect points, form curves, and join opposites, as in Plato’s dialectic or Aristotle’s analogy. And when thought reaches transcendence, it is no longer linear—it is vision, a direct perception of truth, as in Byzantine theology.

The Neurophysiology of Thought and Psychoanalysis

Modern science is beginning to map how the principles of Greek philosophy are reflected in the workings of the brain.

Dialectic and Neural Plasticity

  • The brain is not a static organ, but a network of dynamic interactions.
  • Just as Platonic dialectic dismantles old ideas and creates new ones, the brain changes its connections with every new experience.
  • Neuroscientists speak of synaptic plasticity—the ability of neurons to form new pathways when we learn something new.

Heraclitean Fire and the Activity of Neural Circuits

  • Heraclitus said “everything flows”, meaning that nothing remains fixed.
  • The human mind never stops creating new synapses and adapting to new information.

Aristotelian Logos and the Brain’s Executive Function

  • The prefrontal cortex is responsible for comparative thinking and decision-making.
  • Aristotelian logic can be seen as the way the brain organizes information and finds relationships between its elements.

Psychoanalysis and the Clinical Psychology of Thought

Thought is not merely a computational mechanism. According to psychoanalysis, it is a structure of desires, conflicts, and memories.

  • Freud spoke of the unconscious, which processes reality in a nonlinear way, just as Greek dialectic reveals that truth is not a simple assumption, but the outcome of an inner struggle.
  • Lacan pointed out that language is not simply a tool of expression, but a structure that determines our thinking. In Greek philosophy, this is reflected in the mind’s structure of analogy, in which every thought has its own “geometric” system.

The Sociology of Thought — How Society Shapes Mental Geometry

Social structure determines how we perceive and how we think.

  • Michel Foucault described how power shapes thought through discourses that determine what is acceptable and what is not.
  • Pierre Bourdieu analyzed the relationship between cultural capital and thought—that is, knowledge is not neutral, but shaped by its social context.
  • Society functions as a geometry of the mind, giving us mental schemas that determine how we see reality.

Homer: Guarantor or Prisoner of History?

It is not the Odyssey I went through, but the fact that it was written by Homer. And here a question arises: what does “homēros” mean in Greek? The word means “guarantor,” “bound,” but also “one given as a pledge for reconciliation.” In ancient Greece, hostages were not necessarily prisoners, but people given as a guarantee between two cities or states to ensure that an agreement was honored.

If we consider the name Homer through this meaning, we might ask:

  • Was there really a person named “Homer”? Or does the name signify something deeper?

  • Is the Odyssey merely a narrative of wanderings, or is the text itself a “hostage” of history, bound to carry something beyond time?

  • Might the Iliad and the Odyssey be works that bear within them a truth that had to be preserved—like a hostage waiting to be released from oblivion?

A Dangerous Distortion?

If the return to truth was indeed presented as dangerous or violent, this may not be due to Homer’s work itself, but to how human history used and distorted its symbols.

  • The Odyssey is not a mere adventure; it is the ordeal of returning to one’s true homeland. A return that is not only geographical, but spiritual.

  • If this return was misinterpreted as a dangerous adventure full of traps, perhaps it was because those who did not want a return to truth clothed it in fear.

  • Just as the word Revelation became identified with fear and destruction, the Return to the Ancient Principle was presented as dangerous, difficult, almost forbidden.

Homer and the Odyssey: Return or Captivity?

  • Homer did not write an Odyssey that is a threat. He did not create a work presenting adventure as an endless struggle full of fear and danger. He composed an epic about return. Not simply someone’s return to their homeland, but a return to something that was never lost, yet had to be found again.

    But might the Odyssey itself, as it has come down to us, be a hostage in the hands of history?
    Might its true message have been obscured by interpretations that made it seem an adventure full of dangers, rather than a path toward beauty?

    If someone is afraid to return to the Ancient Principle, perhaps they fear not truth itself, but the image they were taught to have of it.

    And there lies the great question:

    Is the Odyssey a narrative to be feared, or guidance for living it?

Homer: The Composer of a Living Memory

  • Homer did not write in the modern sense of writing. He composed.

    His epic compositions, the Iliad and the Odyssey, were not merely products of individual creation, but the crystallization of a long oral tradition. They were the voice of a collective memory, passed from generation to generation until it took shape through his own art.

    Composition presupposes something living:

    • Not a static record, but continuous reinterpretation.
    • Not a closed text, but a work reshaped in the flow of speech.

    Thus Homer did not write as we write today. He composed, that is, wove together words, rhythms, images, and meaning in a way that could endure through time. That is why, even now, his words are not merely “textual”; they are pulsing with life.

    And perhaps this is the true Odyssey—not a story of battles and obstacles, but a journey back to something imperishable.
    Not an exile into uncertainty, but a journey into revelation.

What changes is not truth, but the human capacity to receive it. Truth is not an external dogma, a set of predetermined rules demanding submission. It is a quality of consciousness, revealed only to those willing to live it and allow it to change them from within. Truth does not punish, but calls. It does not demand obeisance, but understanding and lived experience.

Nikolai Berdyaev’s Christian Philosophy of Revelation

Nikolai Berdyaev (Nikolai Berdyaev, 1874–1948) was one of Russia’s most important thinkers in Christian philosophy of revelation. In his work, he analyzed the conflict between the spiritual human being and dogmatic forms of power, focusing on the creative freedom of human existence and on revelation as the disclosure of life’s innermost meaning.

Some of his most important works:

  • The Destiny of Man (The Destiny of Man)
    → A philosophical exploration of human nature, freedom, and humanity’s relationship with the divine.

  • The Realm of Spirit and the Realm of Caesar (The Kingdom of Spirit and the Kingdom of Caesar)
    → Examines how the world of powers clashes with the world of spirit, analyzing the difference between divine law and human legislation.

  • The Philosophy of the Free Spirit (Philosophy of the Free Spirit)
    → A profound work on spiritual freedom and the human need to overcome every form of coercion, whether political or religious.

  • The Vocation of Man (The Meaning of the Creative Act)
    → Focuses on creativity as a fundamental element of human existence, seeing the human being as a co-creator of the world, rather than a mere servant of the divine plan.

Berdyaev showed that the abuse of judgment is not merely a political or theological distortion, but an existential fall—when judgment is used not to illuminate, but to dominate. He believed that God does not impose His truth, but reveals it, and that the human soul must be ready to receive it through freedom rather than fear.

The Distinction Between Divine Wisdom and Cunning

Wisdom, when severed from Divine Compassion—meaning the Holy Wisdom of meanings, not the harmful “wisdom” called cunning—becomes rigid law that does not guide, but imprisons. In the Greek tradition, wisdom is not merely knowledge, but the harmonization of thought with beauty and truth.

Cunning, by contrast, is a perverted form of knowledge that leads not to illumination, but to control. When wisdom is not accompanied by love, it becomes merely a means of power—a tool that can manipulate, distort, and sever people from their true nature. In patristic thought, wisdom without love leads to arrogance, while wisdom springing from humility leads to the vision of truth.

Judgment as Deeper Vision—Not as a Tool of Control

Judgment is not a mechanism of control, a way of imposing fear of a supposed Antikristos or Antichrist, but a method of deeper vision. Revelation concerns not “salvation” as an external reward, but the restoration of true knowledge, the return of dead meanings to life.

The virgin mind—one untainted by fear and an authoritarian conception of knowledge—does not “save,” but preserves. The word “dia-sōzei” means “carries beyond chaos”, “distinguishes truth from darkness”. The Mind That Remains Virgin (one that retains clarity and is not tainted by the impositions of dogmas) promises not salvation as a future reward, but restores the heart of universal consciousness—that is, true love rather than promised, conditional love.

Divine Love as Ultimate Clarity

Love, in its universal dimension, is neither an emotional term nor a transaction between the human and the divine. It is the very meaning of existence—the very clarity of thought. It is not merely a moral imperative, but the natural state of a mind that sees clearly.

That is why judgment should not be a means of control, but a way for truth to emerge. Revelation is not terror, but tenderness. Truth is not imposition, but a call. And thought that sees does not condemn, but preserves the very possibility of vision.

Divine beauty reveals itself not to those who fear, but to those who love clarity. The search for truth is not a moral obligation, but a natural movement of the mind longing to see beauty. There, wisdom ceases to be cunning and becomes joy. Judgment ceases to be fear and becomes freedom. And salvation ceases to be a promise and becomes now.

This statement holds a fundamental difference between two ways of being: imposition and revelation.

When we speak of societies imposing order without Action, we speak of structures organized not around living experience, but around rules, laws, and disciplines operating independently of human truth. From this arises the crucial point of discernment:

👉 Power that reveals has no need to control. Action, when true, functions not as a tool of management, but as an inner revelation of truth. It needs no orders; it simply happens.

Why Do They Fear the Word “Revelation”?

The word “Apokalypsis” (a- + kalypsis) means removing the covering, revealing truth. It is not an act of violence, but a natural disclosure of what already exists, yet was not previously visible.

Yet powers—whether religious, political, scientific, or economic—fear the word “Revelation,” because they fear truth they do not control.

So What Did They Do?

  1. They channeled the concept of Revelation into fear of the future. They turned it into an image of terrifying events, so that people would not dare seek the real revelation of existence in everyday life.

  2. They linked Revelation to external powers. Instead of recognizing that the revelation of truth is a personal, inner process, they identified it with an event dependent on higher, external forces. Thus revelation became not an act of free realization, but a process requiring intermediaries: Churches, States, Organizations, Ideologies.

  3. They made Revelation the privilege of the few. Although the word means something that uncovers truth for everyone, in practice it became the property of particular structures of power.

The sculpture by Auguste Rodin, “The Walking Man” (“L’Homme qui marche”).

What Is This Sculpture?

  • It is an unfinished sculpture depicting a man in motion, but without a head or arms.
  • The figure is cut off, almost incomplete, yet despite the absence of specific features, his body is full of dynamism and tension.
  • It is as if he walks beyond the limits of his own form, as if the act of movement matters more than bodily completeness itself.

What Does It Symbolize?

  • The Walking Man needs no head to possess thought, nor hands to possess power. His movement is his very strength.
  • He is not a static statue standing complete and perfect—he is in a process of becoming.
  • The absence of hands removes the notion of possession. He holds nothing, imposes nothing, consumes nothing.
  • The absence of a head removes identity as something given—the human being is more an act, a process, than a static being.
