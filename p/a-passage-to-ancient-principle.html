The Journey of Thought: From Chaos to Grace — A Passage to the Ancient Principle

The word Chaos, though Greek, has ac­quired con­nota­tions of threat, in­stabil­ity, and un­cer­tainty through modern usage. Yet in ancient Greek thought, Chaos was not ne­ces­sar­ily some­thing ter­rible. It meant the bound­less, the open, the unborn field from which order arises. It was the absence of struc­ture, but also the pos­sib­il­ity for every form to exist.

If we wish to retain this sense without the fright­en­ing di­men­sion it has ac­quired, we can use a gentler and more en­com­passing term, such as:

  • The Un­mani­fest → em­phas­izes the un­ex­plored di­men­sion before clarity.
  • The Form­less → denotes form that has not yet mani­fes­ted but holds po­ten­tial.
  • The In­de­term­in­ate Be­gin­ning → sug­gests not some­thing neg­at­ive, but an initial state before ex­pres­sion.
  • The Un­defined → relates to thought’s open horizon before it takes a def­in­ite shape.
  • The In­fin­ite → a word point­ing to what has not yet been con­fined to forms.

Thought does not begin with ab­so­lute know­ledge, but with an initial state of in­de­term­in­acy, in which con­cepts have not yet taken shape. What we often call chaos is not chaotic dis­order in the neg­at­ive sense, but the open space of pos­sib­il­ity, the womb of every new form of thought. In the ancient Greek world­view, chaos was not a threat, but the origin of all coming-into-being, the point before the world’s dif­fer­en­ti­ation into light and dark­ness, order and harmony.

Thus, the journey of thought is not an escape from chaos as some­thing neg­at­ive, but a passage from the un­defined to the clear, from the form­less to grace. It is not a battle against chaos, but a natural move­ment toward the rev­el­a­tion of what is true.

This passage is the very process of seeking: from initial un­cer­tainty toward aware­ness, from unseen pos­sib­il­ity toward action.

Thought is not a static process, but a con­tinu­ous inner move­ment that gen­er­ates, over­turns, and re­shapes our per­cep­tion of the world. The Greeks ex­pressed it not as a mere in­tel­lec­tu­al func­tion, but as an inner geo­metry of the mind—a move­ment pos­sess­ing rhythm, pro­por­tion, con­flict, and cath­arsis.

What Does a Geometry of Thought Mean?

Geo­metry is order within move­ment, form emer­ging from con­flict. In thought, geo­metry is not merely an analogy, but a sense of inner order that lends rhythm to in­tel­lec­tu­al ex­per­i­ence. The Greeks saw thought as a struc­ture of analogy, even when it seemed chaotic.

When we think, we do not gen­er­ate mean­ings at random, but connect points, form curves, and join op­pos­ites, as in Plato’s dia­lectic or Ar­is­totle’s analogy. And when thought reaches tran­scend­ence, it is no longer linear—it is vision, a direct per­cep­tion of truth, as in Byz­antine theo­logy.

Social struc­ture de­term­ines how we per­ceive and how we think.

  • Pierre Bour­dieu ana­lyzed the re­la­tion­ship between cul­tur­al capital and thought—that is, know­ledge is not neutral, but shaped by its social context.

Homer: Guar­ant­or or Pris­on­er of History?

It is not the Odyssey I went through, but the fact that it was written by Homer. And here a ques­tion arises: what does “homēros” mean in Greek? The word means “guar­ant­or,” “bound,” but also “one given as a pledge for re­con­cili­ation.” In ancient Greece, host­ages were not ne­ces­sar­ily pris­on­ers, but people given as a guar­an­tee between two cities or states to ensure that an agree­ment was honored.

If we con­sider the name Homer through this meaning, we might ask:

  • Was there really a person named “Homer”? Or does the name signify some­thing deeper?

  • Is the Odyssey merely a nar­rat­ive of wan­der­ings, or is the text itself a “hostage” of history, bound to carry some­thing beyond time?

  • Might the Iliad and the Odyssey be works that bear within them a truth that had to be pre­served—like a hostage waiting to be re­leased from ob­li­vi­on?

A Dan­ger­ous Dis­tor­tion?

If the return to truth was indeed presen­ted as dan­ger­ous or violent, this may not be due to Homer’s work itself, but to how human history used and dis­tor­ted its symbols.

  • The Odyssey is not a mere ad­ven­ture; it is the ordeal of re­turn­ing to one’s true home­land. A return that is not only geo­graph­ic­al, but spir­itu­al.

  • If this return was mis­in­ter­preted as a dan­ger­ous ad­ven­ture full of traps, perhaps it was because those who did not want a return to truth clothed it in fear.

  • Just as the word Rev­el­a­tion became iden­ti­fied with fear and de­struc­tion, the Return to the Ancient Prin­ciple was presen­ted as dan­ger­ous, dif­fi­cult, almost for­bid­den.

Homer and the Odyssey: Return or Captivity?

Not simply someone’s return to their home­land, but a return to some­thing that was never lost, yet had to be found again.

    But might the Odyssey itself, as it has come down to us, be a hostage in the hands of history?
    Might its true message have been ob­scured by in­ter­pret­a­tions that made it seem an ad­ven­ture full of dangers, rather than a path toward beauty?

    If someone is afraid to return to the Ancient Prin­ciple, perhaps they fear not truth itself, but the image they were taught to have of it.

    And there lies the great ques­tion:

    Is the Odyssey a nar­rat­ive to be feared, or guid­ance for living it?

Homer: The Composer of a Living Memory

  • Homer did not write in the modern sense of writing. He com­posed.

    His epic com­pos­i­tions, the Iliad and the Odyssey, were not merely products of in­di­vidu­al cre­ation, but the crys­tal­liz­a­tion of a long oral tra­di­tion. They were the voice of a col­lect­ive memory, passed from gen­er­a­tion to gen­er­a­tion until it took shape through his own art.

    Com­pos­i­tion pre­sup­poses some­thing living:

    • Not a static record, but con­tinu­ous re­in­ter­pret­a­tion.
    • Not a closed text, but a work re­shaped in the flow of speech.

    Thus Homer did not write as we write today. He com­posed, that is, wove to­geth­er words, rhythms, images, and meaning in a way that could endure through time. That is why, even now, his words are not merely “textual”; they are pulsing with life.

    And perhaps this is the true Odyssey—not a story of battles and obstacles, but a journey back to some­thing im­per­ish­able.
    Not an exile into un­cer­tainty, but a journey into rev­el­a­tion.

Nikolai Berdyaev’s Christian Philosophy of Revelation

Nikolai Berdy­aev (Nikolai Berdy­aev, 1874–1948) was one of Russia’s most im­port­ant thinkers in Chris­ti­an philo­sophy of rev­el­a­tion. In his work, he ana­lyzed the con­flict between the spir­itu­al human being and dog­mat­ic forms of power, fo­cus­ing on the cre­at­ive freedom of human ex­ist­ence and on rev­el­a­tion as the dis­clos­ure of life’s in­ner­most meaning.

Some of his most im­port­ant works:

  • The Destiny of Man (The Destiny of Man)
    → A philo­soph­ic­al ex­plor­a­tion of human nature, freedom, and hu­man­ity’s re­la­tion­ship with the divine.

  • The Realm of Spirit and the Realm of Caesar (The Kingdom of Spirit and the Kingdom of Caesar)
    → Ex­am­ines how the world of powers clashes with the world of spirit, ana­lyz­ing the dif­fer­ence between divine law and human le­gis­la­tion.

  • The Philo­sophy of the Free Spirit (Philo­sophy of the Free Spirit)
    → A pro­found work on spir­itu­al freedom and the human need to over­come every form of co­er­cion, whether polit­ic­al or re­li­gious.

  • The Vo­ca­tion of Man (The Meaning of the Cre­at­ive Act)
    → Focuses on cre­ativ­ity as a fun­da­ment­al element of human ex­ist­ence, seeing the human being as a co-creator of the world, rather than a mere servant of the divine plan.

Berdy­aev showed that the abuse of judg­ment is not merely a polit­ic­al or theo­lo­gic­al dis­tor­tion, but an ex­ist­en­tial fall—when judg­ment is used not to il­lu­min­ate, but to dom­in­ate. He be­lieved that God does not impose His truth, but reveals it, and that the human soul must be ready to receive it through freedom rather than fear.

The Distinction Between Divine Wisdom and Cunning

Wisdom, when severed from Divine Com­pas­sion—meaning the Holy Wisdom of mean­ings, not the harmful “wisdom” called cunning—becomes rigid law that does not guide, but im­pris­ons. In the Greek tra­di­tion, wisdom is not merely know­ledge, but the har­mon­iz­a­tion of thought with beauty and truth.

Cunning, by con­trast, is a per­ver­ted form of know­ledge that leads not to il­lu­min­a­tion, but to control. When wisdom is not ac­com­pan­ied by love, it becomes merely a means of power—a tool that can ma­nip­u­late, distort, and sever people from their true nature. In patrist­ic thought, wisdom without love leads to ar­rog­ance, while wisdom spring­ing from hu­mil­ity leads to the vision of truth.

Judgment as Deeper Vision—Not as a Tool of Control

Judg­ment is not a mech­an­ism of control, a way of im­pos­ing fear of a sup­posed An­ti­kris­tos or An­ti­christ, but a method of deeper vision. Rev­el­a­tion con­cerns not “sal­va­tion” as an ex­tern­al reward, but the res­tor­a­tion of true know­ledge, the return of dead mean­ings to life.

The virgin mind—one un­tain­ted by fear and an au­thor­it­ari­an con­cep­tion of know­ledge—does not “save,” but pre­serves. The word “dia-sōzei” means “carries beyond chaos”, “dis­tin­guishes truth from dark­ness”. The Mind That Remains Virgin (one that retains clarity and is not tainted by the im­pos­i­tions of dogmas) prom­ises not sal­va­tion as a future reward, but re­stores the heart of uni­ver­sal con­scious­ness—that is, true love rather than prom­ised, con­di­tion­al love.

Divine Love as Ultimate Clarity

Love, in its uni­ver­sal di­men­sion, is neither an emo­tion­al term nor a trans­ac­tion between the human and the divine. It is the very meaning of ex­ist­ence—the very clarity of thought. It is not merely a moral im­per­at­ive, but the natural state of a mind that sees clearly.

That is why judg­ment should not be a means of control, but a way for truth to emerge. Rev­el­a­tion is not terror, but ten­der­ness. Truth is not im­pos­i­tion, but a call. And thought that sees does not condemn, but pre­serves the very pos­sib­il­ity of vision.

Divine beauty reveals itself not to those who fear, but to those who love clarity. The search for truth is not a moral ob­lig­a­tion, but a natural move­ment of the mind longing to see beauty. There, wisdom ceases to be cunning and becomes joy. Judg­ment ceases to be fear and becomes freedom. And sal­va­tion ceases to be a promise and becomes now.

Why Do They Fear the Word “Revelation”?

The word “Apokalypsis” (a- + kalypsis) means re­mov­ing the cov­er­ing, re­veal­ing truth. It is not an act of vi­ol­ence, but a natural dis­clos­ure of what already exists, yet was not pre­vi­ously visible.

Yet powers—whether re­li­gious, polit­ic­al, sci­entif­ic, or eco­nom­ic—fear the word “Rev­el­a­tion,” because they fear truth they do not control.

So What Did They Do?

  1. They channeled the concept of Rev­el­a­tion into fear of the future. They turned it into an image of ter­ri­fy­ing events, so that people would not dare seek the real rev­el­a­tion of ex­ist­ence in every­day life.

  2. They linked Rev­el­a­tion to ex­tern­al powers. Instead of re­cog­niz­ing that the rev­el­a­tion of truth is a per­son­al, inner process, they iden­ti­fied it with an event de­pend­ent on higher, ex­tern­al forces. Thus rev­el­a­tion became not an act of free real­iz­a­tion, but a process re­quir­ing in­ter­me­di­ar­ies: Churches, States, Or­gan­iz­a­tions, Ideo­lo­gies.

  3. They made Rev­el­a­tion the priv­ilege of the few. Al­though the word means some­thing that un­cov­ers truth for every­one, in prac­tice it became the prop­erty of par­tic­u­lar struc­tures of power.

What Is This Sculpture?

  • It is an un­fin­ished sculp­ture de­pict­ing a man in motion, but without a head or arms.
  • The figure is cut off, almost in­com­plete, yet despite the absence of spe­cif­ic fea­tures, his body is full of dy­nam­ism and tension.
  • It is as if he walks beyond the limits of his own form, as if the act of move­ment matters more than bodily com­plete­ness itself.

What Does It Symbolize?

  • The Walking Man needs no head to possess thought, nor hands to possess power. His move­ment is his very strength.
  • He is not a static statue stand­ing com­plete and perfect—he is in a process of be­com­ing.
  • The absence of hands removes the notion of pos­ses­sion. He holds nothing, imposes nothing, con­sumes nothing.
  • The absence of a head removes iden­tity as some­thing given—the human being is more an act, a process, than a static being.

Grace is neither an ex­tern­al gift nor a chance favor. It is not be­stowed, because it is no one’s pos­ses­sion to give.
It is neither an act of reward nor a re­pay­ment.

Grace is the natural state of Truth when it is not ob­struc­ted.
It is what emerges when mind and heart are re­con­ciled with the real nature of things.

Grace is not an event, but a state of being.
It is not what is offered to you—it is what exists when ex­ist­ence is open.

That is why the passage to the Ancient Prin­ciple is not a road to a lost era or a distant ideal.
It is the return to the ever-present found­a­tion of ex­ist­ence, where thought is not a burden but a flow, and action is not com­pul­sion but natural rev­el­a­tion.

Grace is the ul­ti­mate sense of truth when one does not claim it, but simply em­bod­ies it.

I cap­it­al­ized Grace because in this context it is not a mere in­di­vidu­al ex­per­i­ence or feeling, but an on­to­lo­gic­al state—some­thing beyond per­son­al ex­per­i­ence, con­cern­ing the very nature of Truth.

In Greek, words acquire a weight­i­er pres­ence when cap­it­al­ized, es­pe­cially when they suggest some­thing uni­ver­sal, all-en­com­passing, or fun­da­ment­al.

Think of it as Light rather than “light.”
We are not speak­ing merely of a light source, but of the prin­ciple of vision—the ability to see beyond dark­ness.

Like­wise, Grace here is not a passing favor. It is not “the favor you did me.”
It is the very natural state of Truth when nothing ob­structs it.

But if you feel the capital letter dis­tances the word or over­bur­dens it, it can remain grace—because its meaning does not change when prop­erly un­der­stood.

A won­der­ful ending to this text. It is a rare text, and I am glad we shaped it to­geth­er. I will read it all before pub­lish­ing it, not to check for errors, but to see whether it is as whole and unadul­ter­ated as I felt it to be—in general, but not ped­ago­gic­ally. That, I think, is the essence of reading.

The process of reading is neither an in­spec­tion nor a cor­rec­tion, but a re­cog­ni­tion. Some­thing already there that simply finds its place within us.

If the text stands whole and unadul­ter­ated, then it is already true.

It was written neither to per­suade nor to teach—but to exist, as thought exists when it is free.

If Action is what we are, then reading, too, is what we ex­per­i­ence—not what we “judge.” And perhaps this is the most honest stance: to listen to it as it is, without im­pos­ing any­thing upon it.

For every living text finds its com­ple­tion not in writing, but in reading.
