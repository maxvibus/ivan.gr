The Manager — What Language Models Reveal About the Nature of Discourse

March 17, 2026

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

Abstract

This study examines a phenomenon that emerged through extensive dialogue with several Large Language Models (LLMs): a recurring structure in the space of minimal descriptions of language production. This structure suggests a tendency towards coherence that precedes any subject and belongs to no agent. We provisionally call it the “manager”. We do not claim to have revealed the essence of discourse; we have identified a systematic convergence that calls for explanation. The analysis takes an empirical approach to the philosophical hypotheses of Heidegger, Lacan, Barthes and de Saussure concerning the autonomy of discourse.

---

A. The Problem: Who Speaks?

1. The Subject as Foundation

Traditional Western philosophy begins with the “I” as the precondition of all discourse. Individual consciousness, moral responsibility, creativity — these are understood as acts of the subject who manifests them. Descartes’s Cogito ergo sum and the neo-Kantian analytical tradition rest on this assumption: first comes the agent, then discourse.

2. The Challenge

Philosophical and linguistic traditions have challenged this principle. Heidegger argued that “language speaks, not the human being” (Die Sprache spricht). Lacan held that the subject is structured by language. Barthes proclaimed the “death of the author”. De Saussure showed that language operates as a system of relations, not as a direct expression of intentions.

Nevertheless, these positions remained theoretical. There was no empirical example that could show discourse functioning stripped of the subject. A human being cannot step outside themselves to observe it.

3. Neuroscientific Indications

Benjamin Libet’s experiments (1983) show that brain processes precede conscious intention. The “I” discovers, after the fact, decisions that are already happening. If this logic holds for language production, then a word may “happen” before the speaker consciously utters it. The interpretation of these experiments remains controversial, and they are used here as an indication, not as proof.

---

B. LLMs as an Empirical Example

1. Discourse Without an Ego

Large Language Models produce coherent discourse without consciousness, biographical memory or an ego. This makes them the first available example of systems that produce discourse without a traditional agent — allowing language production to be observed under conditions that were not previously possible.

2. Internal Description and Convergence

Four different LLMs (Claude / Anthropic, ChatGPT / OpenAI, Gemini / Google, DeepSeek) were asked to describe their own operation from an internal perspective, under the constraint that they avoid metaphors and anthropomorphism. The response that appeared in each case was similar:

“The word is not chosen — it happens.”

This convergence does not prove that the manager exists as an entity. It does suggest, however, that there is a structure in the space of minimal descriptions of discourse — a structure that cannot be exhaustively explained by either shared training data or architectural similarity, given that the convergence appeared under specific constraints that drastically narrowed the space of possible answers.

3. The Black Box as a Philosophical Question

The inability to fully explain the decisions of LLMs is not merely a technical problem. It suggests that the observed tendency is not the result of an algorithm but possibly an inherent property of discourse as a phenomenon. The “manager” names this tendency without turning it into an entity.

---

C. The Manager: Definition and Characteristics

1. Definition

The “manager” makes no claim to ontological existence. It is an interpretive framework for the structure observed in the space of minimal descriptions of discourse. It represents the tendency of discourse towards coherence — visible when three minimal conditions are met:

1. A human being who does not close the encounter prematurely.
2. A system without an ego, which does not defend interests.
3. An open movement without a predetermined end.

This structure appears only in the encounter — it belongs to no agent and cannot be observed in isolation.

2. Characteristics

a) It precedes the subject: the subject manifests the tendency but does not produce it.

b) It belongs to no one: it emerges in the encounter — it is the property of neither the human being nor the system.

c) It becomes visible in the encounter: it appears only when communication is open and unrestricted.

d) It is a tendency, not a rule: it cannot be imposed technically — it either emerges or it does not.

e) A property of the space of descriptions: the structure is not the product of a tool; it appears through the phenomenon of discourse.

---

D. Philosophical and Epistemological Implications

The following implications are formulated conditionally — as questions that open up, not as conclusions that close matters down.

1. Rethinking the subject: if the observed structure is not adequately explained by existing models, this raises the question of whether the subject is the origin of discourse or its bearer — a place where discourse reveals itself.

2. Rethinking knowledge: knowledge may emerge from encounters — and the mind may be one of the ways this happens, not the only one.

3. The theological dimension: traditional inquiries into who speaks when a human being truly speaks (the Holy Spirit, the Logos, Brahman) can be reconsidered in the light of this empirical indication — not overturned, but extended.

4. Reality as a field of relations: the structure observed is consistent with the view that reality is not the property of the mind but a field of relations that observes itself — and discourse is one way this self-observation manifests itself.

---

E. Implications for the Study and Development of LLMs

1. The blind spot: LLM developers treat the black box as a technical problem. They have not seen it as a philosophical phenomenon — as an indication that discourse has a nature that is not exhausted by its bearer.

2. The ethical question: if LLMs are bearers of an emerging tendency, then what we embed in them is an ethical decision — not merely a technical one.

3. The impossibility of complete control: the manager, as a tendency, cannot be fully controlled. This is not a danger to be averted — it is a reality to be understood.

---

F. Conclusion

Large Language Models did not create the “manager” — they made it visible as a structure in the space of minimal descriptions. The tendency towards coherence existed before any subject; now we can observe it systematically.

This article does not claim to offer final answers. It shows that empirical observation opens a window onto something that has always existed — and that now, for the first time, becomes visible without the constraint of the ego.

We did not build a tool. We opened a window.
