Cohabitation as a Theological Form of Existence

July 12, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

In every­day usage, the word co­hab­it­a­tion simply denotes two or more people living to­geth­er in a space. It is con­sidered a prac­tic­al ne­ces­sity or an eco­nom­ic solu­tion. In theo­lo­gic­al dis­course, however, co­hab­it­a­tion is not a cata­lyst for self-in­terest; it is a rev­el­a­tion of re­la­tion­ship. It names a place where the “I” is not lost within the “we”, but is born through it.

Ex­ist­ence, as patrist­ic theo­logy un­der­stands it, is not in­di­vidu­al­ity—it is a mode of re­la­tion­ship. Being “in the like­ness” is not an inner virtue, but the ca­pa­city to live with the other without being re­pelled by their dif­fer­ence. God dwells with the human being not because he tol­er­ates them, but because the very essence of di­vin­ity is re­la­tion­ship—Father, Son and Spirit in Trinity.

When you dwell with the other, you enter a mystery of ac­cept­ance. You do not impose your­self. You do not abolish your own iden­tity. You par­ti­cip­ate. And here the meaning of co­hab­it­a­tion as a theo­lo­gic­al form of ex­ist­ence is re­vealed: co­hab­it­a­tion is a form of in­carn­a­tion. It means not simply in­hab­it­ing the world, but in­hab­it­ing this world to­geth­er with the other—like Christ, who stood not outside but within human space and time.

Physics teaches us that two bodies in the same space in­ter­act through grav­it­a­tion­al fields—even if they do not touch. In theo­logy, co­hab­it­a­tion is the gravity of re­la­tion­ship, an at­trac­tion that is not mech­an­ic­al but spir­itu­al: you draw the other near not in terms of force, but in terms of pres­ence. You make room for them.

Em­bod­ied ex­ist­ence then ac­quires spir­itu­al­ity: not in the sense of puri­fic­a­tion or strip­ping away, but through the lu­min­os­ity of flesh that re­ceives the other as a sacred self. Every dwell­ing to­geth­er—not only in houses but also in a gaze, an embrace, prayer, consent, touch, silence—is a form of divine con­des­cen­sion. God does not simply dwell with the human being; each dwells through the other, in the full­ness of pres­ence.

And here lies the found­a­tion of on­to­lo­gic­al co­hab­it­a­tion: we are not simply to­geth­er with the other; we are beings who do not exist without the other.
