Cohabitation as a Theological Form of Existence

July 12, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

In everyday usage, the word cohabitation simply denotes two or more people living together in a space. It is considered a practical necessity or an economic solution. In theological discourse, however, cohabitation is not a catalyst for self-interest; it is a revelation of relationship. It names a place where the “I” is not lost within the “we”, but is born through it.

Existence, as patristic theology understands it, is not individuality—it is a mode of relationship. Being “in the likeness” is not an inner virtue, but the capacity to live with the other without being repelled by their difference. God dwells with the human being not because he tolerates them, but because the very essence of divinity is relationship—Father, Son and Spirit in Trinity.

When you dwell with the other, you enter a mystery of acceptance. You do not impose yourself. You do not abolish your own identity. You participate. And here the meaning of cohabitation as a theological form of existence is revealed: cohabitation is a form of incarnation. It means not simply inhabiting the world, but inhabiting this world together with the other—like Christ, who stood not outside but within human space and time.

Physics teaches us that two bodies in the same space interact through gravitational fields—even if they do not touch. In theology, cohabitation is the gravity of relationship, an attraction that is not mechanical but spiritual: you draw the other near not in terms of force, but in terms of presence. You make room for them.

Embodied existence then acquires spirituality: not in the sense of purification or stripping away, but through the luminosity of flesh that receives the other as a sacred self. Every dwelling together—not only in houses but also in a gaze, an embrace, prayer, consent, touch, silence—is a form of divine condescension. God does not simply dwell with the human being; each dwells through the other, in the fullness of presence.

And here lies the foundation of ontological cohabitation: we are not simply together with the other; we are beings who do not exist without the other.
