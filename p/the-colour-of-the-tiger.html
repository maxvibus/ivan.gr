The colour of the tiger

Published on November 15, 2026 · 4 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

My parents told me to beware of strangers. They did not tell me to beware of my re­l­at­ives as well.

Two days after the me­mori­al service for my last aunt, I came upon a letter from my father, written in 2010 to his family about me. I was not looking for it. I found it during a broader search through his old cor­res­pond­ence. Sixteen years had passed since it was written, and four­teen since his death.

Through it I learned that my pres­ence in the family had not been only a joy. For some it was also a loss: they would not be the ones to inherit their child­less uncle. My father did not turn to his sib­lings but to one of their daugh­ters, asking her to speak to her mother and her aunt. He wanted them not to stand in the way of his effort to make me feel a member of the family, even when he would no longer be there.

What he asked for was the dona­tion, during his life­time, of a small ground-floor flat from my other uncle, so that no dis­putes would arise later. My father wanted Ivan to receive not only from his parents, but from his re­l­at­ives as well.

You cannot force a mar­riage, Ath­anasopoulos. That is what I would have told him, had I known the truth then.

My ad­op­tion had been com­pleted prop­erly. It had taken place, however, one year before the law changed, the law by which adopted chil­dren came to be related to the rest of the family as well. So, al­though I was my parents’ child, I did not have the same legal kinship with their sib­lings and their fam­il­ies. That is what I learned from the letter. It was not only that my po­s­i­tion was dif­fer­ent from what I had be­lieved. It was that some people knew of this dif­fer­ence, while I did not.

For years I lived with the silence and the harsh­ness of one of my uncle’s nieces without un­der­stand­ing where they came from. I was told to be grate­ful, though I had asked for nothing. They cul­tiv­ated in me the idea that I was an heir and that I had ob­lig­a­tions towards them, while they knew my dif­fer­ent legal po­s­i­tion. They were cal­cu­lat­ing what would be left of my uncle’s money. I stayed near him and looked after him, so that he would have someone beside him and would not be left alone.

The last sister died as well. Only then did I learn the truth about all of them. There was no one left from that gen­er­a­tion whom I could ask what they knew, what had happened, what had been said and what had been decided before me.

The tiger has the colour of the nature around it. Its stripes make it hard to make out among the ve­get­a­tion. Its prey may be close to it and not re­cog­nise it.

Nor did I re­cog­nise what was hap­pen­ing, because they looked like family. Their colour was the words of kinship: “we are all the uncle’s nephews and nieces”, “be grate­ful”, “you have an ob­lig­a­tion too, since you are an heir”. I did not know that behind these words lay a dif­fer­ence the others knew. I could not see it, because no one had shown it to me.

The tiger did not choose its stripes, nor does it put on make-up to leave its lair. They are its body. It is hungry, it hides, it eats, and it is not to blame. Man chooses his colour. He can speak, and he keeps silent. He can tell the truth about kinship, and he says only its words. That is why malice does not exist in nature. It exists only where there is choice.

The subject line of my father’s letter was “For help”. It was not an ac­cus­a­tion. It was an appeal to par­tic­u­lar people to help his child find his place in the family. And he knew. The other aunt had a daugh­ter too, with the same rights. He did not write to her. He does not even mention her. He did not write the colour down. He pointed to it.

Sixteen years later, the help reached me from the letter itself. Not from those to whom it had been ad­dressed. My father was no longer here to explain it to me. And the people who could have answered were gone.

Words may be enough. The in­ten­tion, though, always belongs to the human being.

The tiger need not apo­lo­gise for its colour. I had to wait for all of them to die to learn what the colour of those I called my re­l­at­ives was hiding.
