Diaulos

October 20, 2026 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The country's new su­per­com­puter, in­aug­ur­ated at Lavrio, has been named "Daedalus". It is the name of a crafts­man. The myth­ic­al Daedalus was the Atheni­an who knew how to work matter until it became some­thing else, and the word has kept this from the be­gin­ning: in Greek, daidallō means to work some­thing with art. Everything he made was a passage. The labyrinth was cor­ridors that held inside whoever entered. The wings were a way out, over the sea. The same hand made both, and what tells them apart does not lie in the making. It lies in the re­la­tion between the one who crosses them and the place he wants to reach.

The maker's name says what was made. It does not say how one is to stand within what was made. That second thing has another word, very close to the first: diaulos.

The place helps the dif­fer­ence show. In an­tiquity, Lavrio gave silver. The build­ing that now houses the machine once gave elec­tric power. First metal, then energy, now com­pu­ta­tion, and through com­pu­ta­tion, lan­guage. Each time the place pro­duces some­thing that does not stay there. The silver became coin and trav­elled into hands that never saw the mines. The current became light in houses that never knew the power station. Lan­guage, however, when it comes out of such a machine, does some­thing the others did not: it returns to the one who spoke.

This is the diaulos in its ancient sense: the race of two stades, in which the runner reaches the far mark, turns, and comes back to the start­ing point. He fin­ishes where he began, but he is no longer the same. Written dia­logue with a lan­guage model moves in the same way. The sen­tence leaves, passes through the mass of re­cor­ded lan­guage, through what was written by people we will never know, and comes back. The machine at Lavrio is the phys­ic­al body of such a course, made of silicon, cooling water and mega­watts. The name "Daedalus" looks at this body and honours its con­struc­tion. The name "Diaulos" looks at what passes through it.

The word has a second root, the aulos: the pipe through which breath passes and becomes sound. A diaulos is a double pipe. Here some­thing appears that the crafts­man need not say: the in­stru­ment has no breath of its own. It sounds when someone blows into it. The lan­guage the machine gives out is the breath of the people who wrote it, kept, to­geth­er with the breath of the one who now asks and sets it moving. An aulos without a mouth is a tube. It becomes an in­stru­ment the moment someone plays.

Every diaulos has two ends, and the course exists to be run. Great works open slowly. First the con­struc­tion is fin­ished, then it is settled who may enter and how, and only with time does the course fill with runners. The name given to a work shows where on this path it stands. "Daedalus" is the name of the moment the work is com­pleted. "Diaulos" is the name of the moment it begins to be used.

Perhaps, then, every con­struc­tion of lan­guage has two names. One is given by its maker. The other is earned by use, and no one writes it on it. Daedalus made the wings. The flight becomes a diaulos when someone flies and returns.
