A Review Does Not Live in the Present

How Will Artificial Intelligence Read Reviews of a Changing World?

A review is usually written shortly after an event. Someone has stayed in a home, eaten at a restaurant, or taken a journey. They return and write about what they encountered. The event is over, but their account remains available to people who were not there.

That seems simple until we ask when, exactly, what they read is true. “The door did not close properly” may be entirely true of one night. Then the door is repaired. The statement has not become false; it has become old. The person who wrote it has no reason to retract it, since that really was the state of the door when they found it. But someone reading it today may make a mistaken decision if they hear its past tense as a description of the present.

The world changes while reviews remain. That coexistence is both useful and difficult. Reviews tell us things a listing photograph would not. Yet because they endure, something that has been fixed may continue to appear as an active problem. The reverse is equally true: an excellent old review does not guarantee that conditions are still excellent today.

It is not enough, then, to divide reviews into positive and negative. We need to remember that each answers a question rarely written down: What was it like for this person, under these conditions, at that moment? The reader has a different question: What will it be like for me, under my conditions, when I go? The distance between those two questions is the real work of reading.

Five stars do not tell us why someone travelled. A late arrival can make entry instructions crucial, while someone arriving at midday may barely notice them. Proximity to a particular address may matter more than an amenity everyone praises. One person needs quiet for sleep, another accessibility, another the freedom to change plans without a major loss. The same stay does not answer all those needs in the same way.

Today a platform asks visitors to turn their experience into a rating. Tomorrow a language system may read hundreds of ratings and written reviews on behalf of the next visitor. It would be tempting to ask it for a clear answer: “Is this place worth booking?” That is where the danger begins. An answer can be convenient precisely because it has erased the differences we needed in order to decide.

If a system reads ten old reports of a noise and says “the place is noisy,” it may carry into the present something it does not know to be true now. If it reads ten recent reviews that do not mention noise and says “the problem has been solved,” it may again claim more than it knows. The silence of later guests is not proof of a repair. Perhaps they heard nothing; perhaps they heard it but did not think it worth mentioning; perhaps the issue really was fixed. The system must keep those possibilities open.

The same applies to attributing responsibility. “An object was found broken” is an observation. “No one cared enough to fix it” is a conclusion about a chain of people the guest usually cannot see: the person who cleans, the person who receives a report, the person who decides, the person who makes the repair. A review can offer valuable information without knowing exactly where that chain broke down. If artificial intelligence turns the observation into a certain explanation, it will not have read better than a human being. It will merely have spoken more confidently.

I would therefore want a language system that does not immediately manufacture a verdict. It should be able to say: “This issue was reported in this particular month. It appears again in later reviews” — or “It does not appear again, but there is not enough evidence to know why.” It should distinguish what the writer experienced from their interpretation, and a manager’s response from independent confirmation that something changed. It should let the reader return to the accounts themselves instead of asking for trust in an invisible synthesis.

Such a system would not need to pretend to be neutral. It would need to know whom it is reading for. If I tell it I will arrive late and need clear instructions for entry, a recent report of difficulty on arrival becomes especially relevant. If I arrive in daylight and a friend is waiting for me nearby, perhaps it matters less. The system does not change the fact recorded in the review. It changes the question that fact can help us answer.

This is more than a technical improvement in purchase recommendations. It is a way of not placing on one account a weight it cannot bear. The person writing knows their own experience. The person reading knows their own need. The person managing the place knows, or can check, what has changed since then. None of these positions alone contains the whole picture.

Perhaps this is artificial intelligence’s most useful task when faced with reviews: not to decide more quickly who was right, but to prevent time, circumstance, and uncertainty from disappearing in the speed of a decision. To preserve the truth of “that is how it was then” without arbitrarily translating it into “that is how it always is.”
