A Review Does Not Live in the Present

Published on October 3, 2026 · 5 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

How Will Artificial Intelligence Read Reviews of a Changing World?

A review is usually written shortly after an event. Someone has stayed in a home, eaten at a res­taur­ant, or taken a journey. They return and write about what they en­countered. The event is over, but their account remains avail­able to people who were not there.

That seems simple until we ask when, exactly, what they read is true. “The door did not close prop­erly” may be en­tirely true of one night. Then the door is re­paired. The state­ment has not become false; it has become old. The person who wrote it has no reason to retract it, since that really was the state of the door when they found it. But someone reading it today may make a mis­taken de­cision if they hear its past tense as a de­scrip­tion of the present.

The world changes while reviews remain. That co­ex­ist­ence is both useful and dif­fi­cult. Reviews tell us things a listing pho­to­graph would not. Yet because they endure, some­thing that has been fixed may con­tin­ue to appear as an active problem. The reverse is equally true: an ex­cel­lent old review does not guar­an­tee that con­di­tions are still ex­cel­lent today.

It is not enough, then, to divide reviews into pos­it­ive and neg­at­ive. We need to re­mem­ber that each answers a ques­tion rarely written down: What was it like for this person, under these con­di­tions, at that moment? The reader has a dif­fer­ent ques­tion: What will it be like for me, under my con­di­tions, when I go? The dis­tance between those two ques­tions is the real work of reading.

Five stars do not tell us why someone trav­elled. A late arrival can make entry in­struc­tions crucial, while someone ar­riv­ing at midday may barely notice them. Prox­im­ity to a par­tic­u­lar address may matter more than an amenity every­one praises. One person needs quiet for sleep, another ac­cess­ib­il­ity, another the freedom to change plans without a major loss. The same stay does not answer all those needs in the same way.

Today a plat­form asks vis­it­ors to turn their ex­per­i­ence into a rating. To­mor­row a lan­guage system may read hun­dreds of ratings and written reviews on behalf of the next visitor. It would be tempt­ing to ask it for a clear answer: “Is this place worth booking?” That is where the danger begins. An answer can be con­veni­ent pre­cisely because it has erased the dif­fer­ences we needed in order to decide.

If a system reads ten old reports of a noise and says “the place is noisy,” it may carry into the present some­thing it does not know to be true now. If it reads ten recent reviews that do not mention noise and says “the problem has been solved,” it may again claim more than it knows. The silence of later guests is not proof of a repair. Perhaps they heard nothing; perhaps they heard it but did not think it worth men­tion­ing; perhaps the issue really was fixed. The system must keep those pos­sib­il­it­ies open.

The same applies to at­trib­ut­ing re­spons­ib­il­ity. “An object was found broken” is an ob­ser­va­tion. “No one cared enough to fix it” is a con­clu­sion about a chain of people the guest usually cannot see: the person who cleans, the person who re­ceives a report, the person who decides, the person who makes the repair. A review can offer valu­able in­form­a­tion without knowing exactly where that chain broke down. If ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence turns the ob­ser­va­tion into a certain ex­plan­a­tion, it will not have read better than a human being. It will merely have spoken more con­fid­ently.

I would there­fore want a lan­guage system that does not im­me­di­ately man­u­fac­ture a verdict. It should be able to say: “This issue was re­por­ted in this par­tic­u­lar month. It appears again in later reviews” — or “It does not appear again, but there is not enough evid­ence to know why.” It should dis­tin­guish what the writer ex­per­i­enced from their in­ter­pret­a­tion, and a manager’s re­sponse from in­de­pend­ent con­firm­a­tion that some­thing changed. It should let the reader return to the ac­counts them­selves instead of asking for trust in an in­vis­ible syn­thes­is.

Such a system would not need to pretend to be neutral. It would need to know whom it is reading for. If I tell it I will arrive late and need clear in­struc­tions for entry, a recent report of dif­fi­culty on arrival becomes es­pe­cially rel­ev­ant. If I arrive in day­light and a friend is waiting for me nearby, perhaps it matters less. The system does not change the fact re­cor­ded in the review. It changes the ques­tion that fact can help us answer.

This is more than a tech­nic­al im­prove­ment in pur­chase re­com­mend­a­tions. It is a way of not placing on one account a weight it cannot bear. The person writing knows their own ex­per­i­ence. The person reading knows their own need. The person man­aging the place knows, or can check, what has changed since then. None of these po­s­i­tions alone con­tains the whole picture.

Perhaps this is ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence’s most useful task when faced with reviews: not to decide more quickly who was right, but to prevent time, cir­cum­stance, and un­cer­tainty from dis­ap­pear­ing in the speed of a de­cision. To pre­serve the truth of “that is how it was then” without ar­bit­rar­ily trans­lat­ing it into “that is how it always is.”
