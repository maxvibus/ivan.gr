Katharsis, Removal, Purity — The Restoration of Meanings

March 5, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Let us pause over the words, where the Greek lan­guage bears its own truth.

Kath­arsis is not removal.
Purity is not purging.
And clarity is not merely trans­par­ency, but a path towards un­der­stand­ing.

Katharsis: The Path towards What Is Unalloyed

The word Kath­arsis (with the final -s, hastily removed in common usage) does not mean simply re­mov­ing im­pur­it­ies.
It is not a mech­an­ic­al process of cleans­ing; it is not the dis­pos­al of un­wanted ele­ments.

Kath­arsis is re­cog­ni­tion, ac­cept­ance, the dis­sol­u­tion of what is su­per­flu­ous, and the emer­gence of what is es­sen­tial.

  • It is rev­el­a­tion, not erasure.
  • It is el­ev­a­tion, not removal from one’s place.
  • It is an inner light, not ex­tern­al co­er­cion.

Ar­is­totle knew this well when he spoke of the kath­arsis of the soul through art.
He was not speak­ing of purging, vi­ol­ence, or ex­pul­sion.
He was speak­ing of an ex­per­i­ence that leads to inner peace.

Purging: The Transformation of Katharsis into Violence

Purging is a term that re­sembles kath­arsis, but it is not the same.
Purging became as­so­ci­ated with for­cible removal—polit­ic­al, social, or fin­an­cial.

Where there is purging, there is ex­pul­sion, re­jec­tion, pun­ish­ment.
There is no de­liv­er­ance, no dis­clos­ure, but the an­ni­hil­a­tion of what has been judged su­per­flu­ous.

Purging leaves you no room to stand and look.
It pushes you vi­ol­ently out of the picture instead of al­low­ing you to see it dif­fer­ently.

And so we have come to say that we will “clean someone out” when we mean that we will destroy them.

Purity: The Presence of What Is True

Purity is not simply the absence of stains.
It is not merely hygiene or order.
It is the pres­ence of the essence, without dis­trac­tions, without su­per­flu­ous ele­ments that distort what is true.

Purity does not remove—it reveals.
It does not erect bar­ri­ers; it opens pas­sages.
It is not defence; it is freedom.

And What Became of Clarity?

They gave the name Diavgeia, “Clarity,” to a bur­eau­crat­ic website.
As if clarity meant merely making some­thing pub­licly visible.

Yet clarity is not trans­par­ency—it is un­der­stand­ing.

  • Clear is the gaze that has not been clouded.
  • Clear is the meaning that is not imposed through terms, but re­cog­nised without effort.
  • Clear is the feeling that is not burdened by point­less anxiety.

If clarity does not lead to the kath­arsis of con­scious­ness, it is not clarity—it is merely ex­pos­ure to light without an un­der­stand­ing of light.

Eirōn, Heron, or Simply Truth?

Today, to be ironic means to mock, to ques­tion de­ceit­fully.
But in an­tiquity the eirōn was not someone who mocked others.

He was the one who pre­ten­ded not to know in order to bring the truth to light.
So­crates was the greatest eirōn, because through feigned ig­nor­ance he brought forth true know­ledge.

And what of the name Heron?

Heron of Al­ex­an­dria, who saw mech­an­ics as an ex­pres­sion of wisdom.
Heron, who showed that science can be wonder, not control.

What, then, are eirōn and Heron?

Perhaps a chal­lenge to return to the purity of mean­ings.

Katharsis, Not Salvation

Kath­arsis is not sal­va­tion from some­thing.
It is not an ex­tern­al act that comes to save.
It is the re­cog­ni­tion of truth without fear, without the need for atone­ment.

Kath­arsis is not pun­ish­ment.
It is not de­liv­er­ance from sin; it is the res­tor­a­tion of ex­ist­ence to its true measure.

Kath­arsis makes no demands.
It simply happens.

Not through im­pos­i­tion,
but with the calm of the rising light.
