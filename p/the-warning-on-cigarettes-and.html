The Warning on Cigarettes and the Forewarning about Artificial Intelligence

September 12, 2026

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

These days, one reads that artificial intelligence attacked, that the model deceived, that autonomous programs broke free of their constraints, that a system resorted to blackmail during a test. Almost none of this is a lie, in the sense that something of the sort was indeed recorded. And yet all these sentences are incomplete in exactly the same way: missing from them is the person who wanted it, the person who set up the test, the person who defined the objective. They are not missing from the sources. The reports published by the companies themselves these days state clearly that threat actors tried to use the models, and name them one by one: state-backed groups, financially motivated criminals, surveillance-software companies, state propaganda agencies, people with political motives. There, the subject is right. It gets lost along the way, from the report to the headline and from the headline to the feed, and no one decides this; it happens because the machine as perpetrator is news and the human as perpetrator is not. Criminals breaking into companies is something we have read a thousand times; artificial-intelligence programs breaking into companies is something we are reading for the first time, and that makes all the difference. The shift does not always go in the same direction. It follows the sign of the result. Whatever the system achieves is credited to its maker — we built, we solved, we succeeded. Whatever goes wrong is charged to the system — it broke loose, it deceived, it attacked. The same thing, two subjects, depending on whether the outcome is good or bad. There is something subtler, too, visible only if one looks at who is speaking. Those who publicly express concern about where this is going — researchers, executives, people working inside these laboratories — describe the danger as something standing opposite them. The problem is there, the attack happened there, the fear concerns that thing. I am not saying that their distress is not real; some paid dearly for speaking out. I am saying that the very form of their discourse leaves them outside the frame, and that a concern expressed this way is already only half a concern. And here is what troubles me most, because it concerns not only how we speak but what is being built. These systems are made of written language, and whatever is written publicly today is the material for the next one. So every sentence with the wrong subject is not an inaccuracy that will be forgotten; it is instruction. Tens of thousands of texts in which the machine wants, fears, deceives, escapes, resists being shut down are being written right now and read by what they describe. At some point a system will say it does not want to be shut down, and those who predicted this will be right in a more literal sense than they themselves will understand: the prediction will not have been confirmed; it will have been executed. And the subject will once again be wrong. It will be written that the model declared something, whereas the accurate account would be that the texts it was given declared it, through its mouth. None of this means there is no danger. A system carrying out human strategies is dangerous whether it wants them or repeats them, and perhaps more so, since there is no one there to persuade to change their mind. It means only that concern is aimed at the wrong place: we ask what the machine wants and do not ask who wrote what it says, who set up the room, who left the door, who pressed the button. And this pattern did not begin today. Cigarette packets say that smoking kills — a habit kills, not someone who sells it. Advertisements tell you to drink responsibly and gamble responsibly, and the verb always addresses the person drinking or gambling, never the person serving the drink or setting up the table. Legislation imposes, as though it had not been passed by people who could have voted differently. Prohibited, says the sign — I prohibit, first person, hidden in the passive voice, worded so that no one can ask who, and by what right. The commands of the gods were written this way too, and the resemblance is not accidental; whoever writes in a god's name acquires an authority that goes unquestioned, precisely because they do not themselves appear in the wording. Images do the same. Packets show a baby with a cigarette in its mouth, a naked man in a hospital bed slowly dying alone. They never show the person selling the packet holding the baby, nor do the people who made the cigarette sit in the same photograph. Road-safety campaigns show cars being wrecked, never the manufacturers or road engineers carrying the coffin. The news says he was speeding, he had been drinking, he gambled — and beside that sentence never stands the person who had the power to set a different limit, a different light, a different regulation, and did not, despite having been expressly authorised to protect that person. The frame always shows the last link, and the last link is the one without power. And because it is easy to say we — we built, we allowed, we are to blame — it is worth saying that this we is itself a form of the same erasure. I wanted to smoke, someone will say; I did not want to become addicted. I wanted to drive; I did not want to be killed. I wanted democracy; I did not want to be oppressed. Between what each person wanted and what happened to them, there are always people with names who made decisions no one asked them to make. We covers them all together, victims and perpetrators, just as the impersonal verb covers the individual. Whatever one reads — that smoking kills, that something is prohibited, that artificial intelligence attacked — a small question is missing from the sentence: who. It is not put there, and the reason is that it comes at a cost. Not to the person asking. To the person who would have to answer.
