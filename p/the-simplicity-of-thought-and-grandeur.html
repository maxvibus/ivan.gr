The Simplicity of Thought and the Grandeur of Experience

Published on May 30, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Deeper aware­ness is born not from simply adding know­ledge, but from re­mov­ing un­ne­ces­sary con­cepts, from re­mov­ing whatever ob­structs clarity of vision. The essence of un­der­stand­ing often lies in the dis­tance we take from the obvious, in the move­ment of step­ping back that allows us to see the true face of things, hidden beneath their fa­mil­i­ar surface. Every densely layered meaning invites an inner shift, a silent ad­mis­sion that the most im­port­ant truths are not those we can readily express, but those that dwell at the edge of silence, where lan­guage yields before the grandeur of ex­per­i­ence. Thought thus becomes an act of sim­pli­city, an act of re­duc­tion that leads not to poverty, but to the rich freedom of clear per­cep­tion. What remains, then, is not merely what we know, but what we are truly able to see and ex­per­i­ence dir­ectly, with nothing in­ter­ven­ing except the very clarity of pres­ence.

One example of an un­ne­ces­sary concept is the con­stant search for con­firm­a­tion of our worth through other people’s judg­ments. This concept is un­ne­ces­sary because it adds no real un­der­stand­ing of who we are. Instead, it dis­tracts us and clouds our clear image of ourselves, ob­struct­ing the au­then­t­ic ex­per­i­ence of ex­ist­ence and of our actual pres­ence in the world. Identi­fy­ing such un­ne­ces­sary con­cepts is the first act of step­ping back that pre­pares us to grasp true un­der­stand­ing beyond the obvious.

True un­der­stand­ing often does not come from an im­me­di­ate, self-evident per­cep­tion of a situ­ation. Instead, it re­quires a con­scious move­ment away from what ini­tially seems obvious or given, so that we can gain a broader per­spect­ive.

This step­ping back is not avoid­ance, but an active move­ment that enables us to see the deeper and often unseen aspects of things. Beneath the surface — behind what appears at first glance — lie the essence, the truth, the deeper reality. When we take such a dis­tance, per­cep­tion expands, al­low­ing us to discern the true face, the actual nature of situ­ations, free from pre­ju­dice, hasty con­clu­sions or su­per­fi­cial judg­ments.

In other words, to see clearly, we must move beyond the im­me­di­ate and enter a space where our gaze is free from the limits of a first im­pres­sion. From there, reality appears truer and more sub­stan­tial.

The “grandeur of ex­per­i­ence” refers to the dis­tinct­ive quality of moments that exceed lan­guage’s ability to express them fully. These are ex­per­i­ences in which we feel we are touch­ing some­thing deeper, some­thing almost in­ex­press­ible and yet pro­foundly real. They may be moments of pure joy, a sudden aware­ness, a deep con­nec­tion with someone or some­thing, or even our simple yet com­plete pres­ence in the here and now.

This grandeur lies pre­cisely in the fact that the ex­per­i­ence is so im­me­di­ate and com­plete that it does away with the need for de­scrip­tion or ex­plan­a­tion — it is ab­so­lute and self-suf­fi­cient in itself. It is like a small rev­el­a­tion, in which life dis­closes its true depth, tran­scend­ing the su­per­fi­ci­al­ity of every­day life and re­mind­ing us that there is a level of ex­ist­ence always there, ready to sur­prise and pro­foundly move us.

Thought as an act of sim­pli­city means that sub­stan­tial, deep think­ing does not arise from ac­cu­mu­lat­ing ever more in­form­a­tion or ideas, but from se­lect­ively re­mov­ing what is un­ne­ces­sary, su­per­flu­ous or ob­structs clear per­cep­tion. This sim­pli­city is a process of clear­ing the mind: instead of con­tinu­ally adding new layers of know­ledge, thought removes whatever does not lead to clarity and lu­cid­ity. Space is thus freed within us to per­ceive truths that are simple but not simplist­ic. This sim­pli­city helps us ex­per­i­ence thought as pure pres­ence, as lu­cid­ity, as im­me­di­ate ex­per­i­ence, rather than merely as in­tel­lec­tu­al over­load.

In this way, thought as an act of sim­pli­city leads not to in­tel­lec­tu­al poverty, but to a wealth of clear and sub­stan­tial aware­ness. It is a prac­tice that leads to calm, to au­then­ti­city and, ul­ti­mately, to a deeper and more au­then­t­ic re­la­tion­ship with ourselves and the world around us.
