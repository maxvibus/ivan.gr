The image of mother and child — a stolen language

March 18, 2026 · 8 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Power is not a thing. You cannot point to it or touch it. It is a re­la­tion­ship — and it exists only when it is re­cog­nized.

And yet, for this thing without a body, people have knelt, obeyed, died.

How?

---

Power in­ven­ted nothing. It found some­thing ready-made.

It saw the mother holding her child — the first ex­per­i­ence of safety — and copied her. Not the act. The lan­guage of the act.

It said: “I will protect you. I will care for you.”

But it did not care as the mother did. It kept the form. Emptied out the sub­stance.

There is some­thing within us that does not think — it simply re­mem­bers. It re­mem­bers the warmth, being held, the first voice that said “you are safe.” That part of us — the infant who never en­tirely left — heard power speak­ing in the mother’s lan­guage. And obeyed. Not because it was per­suaded. Because it re­cog­nized.

Reason was not de­ceived. Memory was ac­tiv­ated.

---

Some­where in history — I do not know exactly when — someone hung the method on the wall.

The Virgin Mary with the child. The truest re­la­tion­ship — becomes an image, becomes an icon, becomes an object of ven­er­a­tion. And then they say: “The Church is Mother. God is Father. You are the child. Come to us.”

Why do I mention the church? Because it is the clearest example of a tech­nique we might call *pre-emptive dis­clos­ure*: you show your method openly, before anyone has time to expose it. You put it on display. And call it sacred.

So if someone comes along later and says “look, they are using the mother–child re­la­tion­ship to make you need them — to come between you and God, to control access to the sacred, to decide for them­selves what is sin and what is sal­va­tion, to make them­selves in­dis­pens­able,” the answer is ready: “Yes, we know. That is what we worship. That is the mystery.”

I am not saying this was a plan. Perhaps it was. Perhaps not. Perhaps it happened without them knowing. But the result is the same: you cannot expose some­thing that is already ven­er­ated. The dis­clos­ure has already happened — and has been clothed in holi­ness. And if you try to point it out — you do not become the one who exposes it. You become the one who pro­fanes it.

---

The most stable power does not need vi­ol­ence.

Vi­ol­ence is often its failure. When it needs to strike, some­thing has already cracked. Silent consent — when you obey without needing to be told, without a threat being needed, without force being ex­pen­ded — is the tool that costs power the least. It needs no army, no prisons, no sur­veil­lance. It only needs you to believe that this is how things should be.

The power that works best is the one you cannot see. You do not obey because you are forced. You obey because you no longer know whether what holds you is pro­tect­ing you or re­strict­ing you.

---

And when the mask falls? When not only the struc­ture but the in­ten­tions too — hidden or open — are re­vealed?

It rarely speaks without dis­guise. Even then.

The tor­turer does not say to himself: “I torture because I enjoy hurting people.” He says: “I do it for the nation. This man is a traitor. I am pro­tect­ing the in­no­cent. It is a ne­ces­sary evil.”

That is the story he needs in order to sleep at night. To look in the mirror and see some­thing other than what he is.

A human being cannot bear to do harm without telling them­selves they are doing good.

That is why power rarely speaks without dis­guise — even during torture.

---

This did not happen yes­ter­day. It has been hap­pen­ing for mil­len­nia.

Priests, kings, states, cor­por­a­tions — many speak the same lan­guage. “We care for you.” And behind the care, the chain.

Not always. Not everything is like this. There is genuine care some­where too. The problem is how to tell it apart.

---

And now it is ex­tend­ing beyond the human being.

Re­cently they decided: declare your olive trees. Not the olives you harvest — the trees that exist. Even if you do not take their fruit. Even if they simply stand there, on your land, un­touched.

The tree signed nothing. It did not agree. It did not vote. It simply exists.

But you are called upon to declare it. To account for its ex­ist­ence.

And if you do not? Power does not begin with vi­ol­ence. It begins with a fine — some­thing that seems reas­on­able, ad­min­is­trat­ive, almost mild. Most people pay at this point, and that is the end of it. But if you do not pay, seizure follows — they take some­thing that is yours. And if you resist the seizure, vi­ol­ence follows — the police, de­ten­tion, blows. Vi­ol­ence is always there, at the end of the chain. You do not see it because most people stop much earlier. They obey at the first step. But the last step waits — for anyone who dares to go that far.

---

Some people saw. From time to time, some people see.

And what happened to some of them?

They were hon­oured. They became philo­soph­ers, clas­sics, chapters in books. They were ab­sorbed.

Fou­cault de­scribed how in­sti­tu­tions produce sub­mis­sion — how the uni­ver­sity, the prison, the hos­pit­al and the army operate ac­cord­ing to the same logic: clas­si­fic­a­tion, ex­am­in­a­tion, dis­cip­line. And now he is taught within those very uni­ver­sit­ies — which examine, grade, dis­cip­line and award degrees. His cri­tique became a course. Stu­dents are ex­amined on it. They receive a grade. And the system carries on.

Marx ana­lysed how capital ex­ploits labour. And now his ana­lys­is is funded by in­sti­tu­tions that rely on capital. Con­fer­ences are held. Books are pub­lished. Sold. The cri­tique of the system becomes a product of the system.

Christ entered the temple and over­turned the mer­chants’ tables. He said they had made the house of God a den of thieves. And now? He is ven­er­ated in churches. Churches with hier­archy, prop­erty, power. The over­turn­ing became an icon. The icon was hung on the wall. And the mer­chants re­turned — in cas­socks.

Uni­ver­sit­ies are not neutral. They are in­sti­tu­tions that decide what counts as know­ledge, who has the right to speak, who re­ceives a degree and who does not. They give you per­mis­sion to think — and take it back if you do not follow the rules. The cri­tique of power becomes a spe­cial­ism. And the spe­cial­ists draw their salar­ies from the system they cri­ti­cize.

Dis­clos­ure can be ab­sorbed. Become a spec­tacle. Become one more chapter.

I am not saying it always happens. But it happens often enough that you should not trust that seeing is enough.

---

And the promise that one day everything will be re­vealed? That one day the truth will come?

That too may be part of the trap. The hope someone else gives you — rather than your own sight — can be a way of keeping you waiting. And while you wait — you obey.

I am not saying every hope is a chain. I am saying: pay at­ten­tion to who gives it to you.

---

And the person who sees the struc­ture of the powers that govern?

They lose some­thing that does not easily return: trust in the in­ten­tions of those who say they want what is good for them. What is good for the world. For the trees. For nature. For chil­dren. For the nation. For your soul.

It is not that you trust no one. It is that every “I want what is best for you” now sounds dif­fer­ent. Every “I am pro­tect­ing you” con­tains a shadow. Every “I am doing it for you” may be true — or it may be the same lan­guage every­one uses. You no longer know auto­mat­ic­ally.

That is the cost. Perhaps the gift too. I am not sure.

---

What remains?

Not a new theory — it risks being ab­sorbed.
Not a re­volu­tion — it may replace one father with another.
Not a de­nun­ci­ation — it may become a spec­tacle.

What remains is a ques­tion. Not a the­or­et­ic­al one — a bodily one:

Does anyone have jur­is­dic­tion over a tree? The tree was there before him. It will be there after him. It asked him for nothing. It owes him nothing.

And if he has no jur­is­dic­tion over the tree — why should he have it over me?

When did I agree that someone else would decide how much of my labour I get to keep? I do not re­mem­ber signing. I do not re­mem­ber being asked. I was simply born, and it was already like this.

When they tell me “we do it for the good of society” — which society? The one that did not ask whether I wanted to join it? The one that lets me live within it only if I pay the price of ex­ist­ing — on the terms of the many or the very few?

---

This ques­tion does not abolish power.

But it strips away its ap­par­ent nat­ur­al­ness.

What does that mean? Not that power will fall. Not that it will be lev­elled. Perhaps it will never fall.

But the way you see it changes.

It is one thing to see a king in his robe — and another to see him naked. The king is still there. Perhaps he still has power. Perhaps he can still give orders. But you now see him as he is — without the cloak. Without the magic. Without the aura that made sub­mis­sion seem an honour.

Dis­clos­ure does not auto­mat­ic­ally bring freedom. But it brings some­thing else: the pos­sib­il­ity of knowing what it is that holds you. Of not mis­tak­ing it for some­thing natural, in­ev­it­able, eternal.

And the moment some­thing stops looking natural — it starts to become visible.
