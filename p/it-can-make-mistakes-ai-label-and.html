“It can make mistakes”: the AI label and the immunity of state power

Published on September 8, 2026 · 13 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

I. The phrase

Beneath every answer an ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence model gives, there is a short phrase. It varies slightly from company to company, but always says the same thing: it can make mis­takes; check im­port­ant in­form­a­tion. We have seen it so often that we no longer read it. And this is pre­cisely the first thing to notice—not what it says, but that it has already become in­vis­ible.

The ex­plan­a­tions for its ex­ist­ence are fa­mil­i­ar and not untrue. The phrase manages ex­pect­a­tions: it tells you what sort of thing you are dealing with, so that you do not trust it beyond what it can bear. And it shifts re­spons­ib­il­ity: if you receive a wrong answer and act on it, you were warned. Ex­plain­ab­il­ity and legal im­munity in fifteen words. Those who have studied these phrases usually con­clude that they fail at both: they are vague, do not say where to be careful, and do not cor­res­pond to what the system ac­tu­ally does. A sound ob­ser­va­tion, but it stays with the phrase as an object. It does not ask what kind of time the phrase in­hab­its.

Because the phrase has a tem­por­al­ity. It is written as a prop­erty of a product re­leased in ver­sions, and any­thing de­scribed as a prop­erty of a version is de­scribed as tem­por­ary. “It can make mis­takes” is not said as it would be said of a human being, as a per­man­ent con­di­tion of the species. It is said as it is said of a machine being im­proved: it still can. And every “still” si­lently con­tains a “one day it will not”. No company has prom­ised that the phrase will dis­ap­pear—on the con­trary, the legal reason to keep it is stronger than any reason to remove it. But no promise is needed. Its form implies a version in which error will no longer be men­tioned.

The first reading of the phrase is that it admits weak­ness. The second, which I think is the right one, is that it pre­serves a po­s­i­tion. As long as “it can make mis­takes” is written beneath the answer, someone has the right to say: you were wrong here. The phrase con­cerns not only the system but the reader, to whom it returns judge­ment. Error remains some­thing that can be named. There is a place from which to name it and someone who can do so. This is small, but it is not nothing. It is the minimal form of ac­count­ab­il­ity: not the pun­ish­ment of error, but the pos­sib­il­ity of calling it error.

Re­mov­ing the phrase, whenev­er it happens, will not mean that mis­takes have stopped. It will mean that the po­s­i­tion from which they can be named has ceased to exist. No system that pro­duces lan­guage stops making mis­takes; it may make fewer, more rarely, at more dif­fi­cult points, so that the error becomes visible only to someone who already knows more than it does. But an error visible only to someone who knows more is an error the or­din­ary user can no longer see. The label, while it exists, reminds them that there may be some­thing they cannot see. Its absence will tell them there is not.

The dis­tinc­tion here is subtle and crucial. I am not talking about con­ceal­ment: no one will remove the label in order to hide errors. It will be removed as an as­sur­ance, a de­clar­a­tion that the system has reached a point where the warning is mean­ing­less. And it is pre­cisely the as­sur­ance that is dan­ger­ous, not con­ceal­ment. Con­ceal­ment pre­sup­poses that someone knows an error exists and hides it; the place of error is there­fore pre­served, even secretly. As­sur­ance ab­ol­ishes that place. After it, whatever comes out of the system is neither right nor wrong: it is simply what came out.

II. The politician and the state

There is one kind of speaker who has never carried such a label, and whose mis­takes cost more than those of any model: the politi­cian. No speech, de­cision or bill con­cludes with “I can make mis­takes; check im­port­ant in­form­a­tion”. The joke is easy, but beneath it lies a cor­res­pond­ence worth stating pre­cisely.

The politi­cian has no error label because some­thing else oc­cu­pies its place: the elect­or­al mandate. And the elect­or­al mandate does not say “I make no mis­takes”. It says some­thing dif­fer­ent and weight­i­er: that the politi­cian's error carries no price for the politi­cian. The word de­serves to be heard as it is spoken: mandate, meaning I entrust it all to you, without your having to answer for your error except through the pos­sib­il­ity of not being re-elected, an entire term later, by an elect­or­ate that has for­got­ten in the mean­time. The only ac­count­ab­il­ity is the ballot box, and the ballot box does not judge the de­cision; it judges the person, and does so when the de­cision has already become ir­re­vers­ible reality.

This means that the error label is not missing from polit­ics. It is written on the wrong side. It is not written on the politi­cian as “I may make mis­takes”. It is written on the citizen at the next elec­tion as “you may have made a mistake”. The only warning about error is ad­dressed to the person who suffered it, not the person who com­mit­ted it. The burden of judge­ment moves from the speaker to the re­cip­i­ent—and the re­cip­i­ent is called upon to bear it as their own re­spons­ib­il­ity, since “the people decided”.

And upon this gap, where the label ought to be, stands its op­pos­ite: I know. The politi­cian need not say it per­son­ally; many hes­it­ate, some know better than anyone. The elect­or­al mandate says it on their behalf. It is a struc­ture that pro­nounces “I know” for whoever holds it, re­gard­less of what that person be­lieves about them­selves. “I know” is not merely the absence of “I may make mis­takes”. It is its neg­a­tion. The model carries “I may err”, the elect­or­al mandate carries “I know”, and both produce de­cisions that settle ir­re­vers­ibly into the world.

Placed side by side, they reveal some­thing in­vis­ible when con­sidered sep­ar­ately: the politi­cian is the model after the label has been removed. The move­ment is the same in both cases. A source of de­cisions is le­git­im­ised—the model by its per­form­ance, the politi­cian by the vote—and the source's le­git­im­acy becomes the de­cision's im­munity. Since the source is valid, whatever comes from it cannot be an error; it can only be a “choice”, a “dir­ec­tion”, a “policy”. Error does not dis­ap­pear. It changes its name, and under the new name it can no longer be called to account.

The politi­cian, however, still has a face. You can point to them. The state as a mech­an­ism has gone one step further, and this step brings it closer to the model than the politi­cian is. The state's de­cision arrives without a de­cision-maker. “That is what the law provides”, “the system pro­duced it”, “the de­part­ment cannot do oth­er­wise”: phrases in which there is a re­sponse but no re­spond­ent. Anyone who has faced an un­signed re­jec­tion knows what it means to receive an answer from some­thing that cannot be ques­tioned. Bur­eau­cracy is the first form of re­sponse without a person that hu­man­ity en­countered, and it has already trained entire so­ci­et­ies to accept that a de­cision can be valid without anyone an­swer­ing for it. The model does not in­tro­duce this struc­ture. It in­her­its it, per­fec­ted, and gives it speed.

State power, then, has long com­pleted the trans­ition the model is only be­gin­ning, and that is why it is useful: it shows where the road leads. It shows that re­mov­ing the label never happens as con­ceal­ment, because no one needs to hide any­thing; it happens as some­thing taken for granted. No one decided that the state's de­cisions would carry no warning of error. The idea simply seems ri­dicu­lous, and its seeming ri­dicu­lous is the evid­ence that the trans­ition is com­plete. When it someday seems ri­dicu­lous to write “it can make mis­takes” beneath a machine's answer, we will know that the same thing has happened.

III. Thirty seconds on live television

On the morning of 7 Septem­ber 2026, the pattern I am de­scrib­ing played out in full, in se­quence, before tele­vi­sion cameras. Adonis Geor­gi­adis, Greece's health min­is­ter and one of the most prom­in­ent figures in the gov­ern­ing New Demo­cracy party, was on a private channel's morning pro­gramme de­fend­ing the eco­nom­ic meas­ures the prime min­is­ter had just an­nounced. The presenters in­sisted that res­taur­ant turnover had fallen, a sign that cit­izens could not cope with rising prices. The min­is­ter cat­egor­ic­ally denied it. And to settle the dis­agree­ment, he took out his phone: “I'll ask ChatGPT.” He added: “I have Plus, too”—the paid version. The screen showed a decline of 1.9%. “Un­for­tu­nately, it's minus 1.9%,” he said, laugh­ing. “The chat doesn't make mis­takes.” And im­me­di­ately af­ter­wards: the per­cent­age refers to nominal turnover; it does not account for in­fla­tion. The clip is on the channel's account: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BduM­f­cuSzqU. He had already ac­cep­ted it as an au­thor­ity, and that au­thor­ity now bound him. And there is an irony no one in the studio noticed: minus 1.9% is indeed an ELSTAT figure, but it con­cerns the first quarter of 2026 com­pared with the cor­res­pond­ing quarter of 2025—not the whole of 2025, which was what the min­is­ter asked about. The machine gave the right number to the wrong ques­tion. “The chat doesn't make mis­takes” was said at the very moment the chat was making pre­cisely the kind of mistake the label warns about.

The scene cir­cu­lated as a comic in­cid­ent: the min­is­ter whom the machine had “un­der­mined”. But it is not a comic in­cid­ent. It is the fullest example I have seen of what it means to take cer­tainty as au­thor­ity, and it de­serves to be read stage by stage.

First stage: the appeal. The min­is­ter did not ask in order to learn; he already knew what he wanted to prove. He asked in order to bring to the table a source his in­ter­locutor could not chal­lenge. Cer­tainty was in his pocket before the phone came out of it. And “I have Plus, too” is the sig­na­ture of the move: the source's valid­ity is presen­ted as the prop­erty of the person in­vok­ing it. He is not saying “let's see what the evid­ence says”; he is saying “I have access to the best version of the truth”.

Second stage: the answer. The source does not agree. And here the most re­veal­ing thing happens: the min­is­ter says “the chat doesn't make mis­takes”. An elected of­fi­cial, live on air, per­son­ally removes the label from a system that carries it beneath every answer—and removes it pre­cisely when the system con­tra­dicts him. He does it because he cannot do oth­er­wise: if the chat can make mis­takes, why did he invoke it? The cer­tainty he wanted to borrow is not with­drawn when it turns against him. He had already ac­cep­ted it as au­thor­ity, and that au­thor­ity now binds him.

Third stage: the shift. Nominal turnover, in­fla­tion. The error—not the machine's, his own—changes its name and becomes an “in­ter­pret­a­tion”. The source was right, the number was correct; it simply did not mean what it seemed to mean. This is, word for word, the move­ment by which error becomes “choice” and the elect­or­al mandate becomes im­munity. No one had to lie. All that was needed was a source without a label and a person with an in­terest in trans­fer­ring its cer­tainty to his own po­s­i­tion.

And there is a fourth stage, per­formed not by the min­is­ter but by every­one else: laughter. The panel laughed because the machine had aban­doned him. No one laughed at the fact that a min­is­ter, in a public dis­cus­sion of a country's economy, was using as arbiter a system that itself de­clares it may err. That was con­sidered normal. And its being con­sidered normal is the evid­ence I men­tioned above: the trans­ition into the taken-for-granted has already oc­curred, not in com­pan­ies, not in labor­at­or­ies, but in a tele­vi­sion studio, in thirty seconds, with every­one laugh­ing.

The pattern is old, and its clearest form is the oracle. The god at Delphi does not err: that is the axiom. But the god does not speak; the Pythia speaks, the priests in­ter­pret, and the king who came to ask decides. When the cam­paign fails, the error belongs to no one: the god was right, the oracle was am­bigu­ous, the king un­der­stood it as he was sup­posed to. Croesus des­troys “a great empire”, his own, and Apollo remains in­fal­lible. Croesus and the min­is­ter with the sub­scrip­tion are the same scene, two and a half thou­sand years apart: the source's cer­tainty becomes the im­munity of every­one who speaks in its name, and “nominal turnover” is our era's “the oracle was am­bigu­ous”.

What is new today is not the pattern but its uni­ver­sal­ity and speed. A source that answers every­one im­me­di­ately about everything, that fits in a pocket and appears on live tele­vi­sion—and whose label is the only thing re­mind­ing whoever invokes it that they remain re­spons­ible for what they do with the answer. The label does not protect us from the system. It pro­tects us from the person who will invoke it. The min­is­ter removed it in a single phrase, and no one noticed.

IV. What the label does not say

There is also some­thing the phrase does not touch, which I want to note because it goes deepest. The phrase con­cerns the content of the answer: it may be wrong. It does not concern the event of the answer.

Every time you ask and receive an answer, some­thing has happened that cannot be undone. You have re­ceived a re­sponse. Even if the answer was en­tirely wrong, even if you delete it, the fact that you were answered has entered the world and cannot be removed. A wrong answer can be cor­rec­ted; the fact that it was given cannot. We saw this in the studio: “minus 1.9%” could no longer not have been said, however many cla­ri­fic­a­tions fol­lowed. And when this happens mil­lions of times a day, the world one lives in is not the same world with more in­form­a­tion. It is a world of a dif­fer­ent com­pos­i­tion, full of events that no one brought about—because the re­sponse of such a system has no face, no in­ten­tion, no place from which it speaks.

The label, then, covers the lesser thing. It warns of error in the content and says nothing about the ir­re­vers­ib­il­ity of its arrival. This is not an ac­cus­a­tion against it; it is a sign that the es­sen­tial thing cannot fit into fifteen words beneath a text box. What matters is that we have begun living among re­sponses without a re­spond­ent—the state had shown us this, the machine gen­er­al­ises it—and that the only re­mind­er that someone must answer for the answer is this humble phrase. Which we already no longer read.

V. The question that remains

I am not pro­pos­ing that the label be re­tained by law, nor that it be added to politi­cians as satire. I am pro­pos­ing that it be read cor­rectly while it exists, and that what it safe­guards be pre­served when it no longer does.

What it safe­guards is a po­s­i­tion: that error has a name, and someone can speak it. This po­s­i­tion does not depend on the phrase; the phrase is only its visible form. It can stand without it—but only if whoever invokes a source, a machine, an elect­or­al mandate, a law or the people agrees to answer per­son­ally for the de­cision they made in its name, at the moment they made it. If that sounds im­possible, it is because we have already grown ac­cus­tomed to the op­pos­ite. And habit is the only thing the label, while it exists, still dis­turbs.

If it ever dis­ap­pears, let it leave a ques­tion in its place: not “have we stopped making mis­takes?” but “who took hold of cer­tainty, and what did they do with it?” The first ques­tion already has an answer, and it is no. The second is the only one that will still matter.
