The Return That Is Not a Return: When Memory Meets Dialogue

When Absence Becomes a Question: The Dialogue That Does Not Seek Answers

Some con­ver­sa­tions are not mere ex­changes of words. They are turning points. Moments when the past claims its place in the present, not as a memory, but as a living force de­mand­ing re­cog­ni­tion.

This text was born of such a con­ver­sa­tion—not because a par­tic­u­lar moment or a par­tic­u­lar person matters more than others, but because the ques­tion raised does not concern only me. It con­cerns every­one who has ever been called upon to justify an absence. Every­one who has felt the need to explain why a path led them else­where. And every­one who has faced a ques­tion that sought not simply an answer, but an ac­count­ing of them­selves.

To those who once shared the same path with us but read it dif­fer­ently. To those who asked ques­tions without ex­pect­ing an answer, and to those who found them­selves de­fend­ing their actions without knowing whether they needed to. To all of us who have ever wondered what it means to explain your absence to someone who cannot see it beyond their own wound.

The text that follows is not an answer. It is neither a defense nor a jus­ti­fic­a­tion. It is an attempt to un­der­stand the nature of re­la­tion­ships, memory, and the need to affirm or call into ques­tion one another's ex­ist­ence.

It is a reck­on­ing with the past that seeks not to win, but to be spoken.

Some con­ver­sa­tions are not mere ex­changes of words. They are turning points. Moments when the past claims its place in the present, not as a memory, but as a living force de­mand­ing re­cog­ni­tion. In these con­ver­sa­tions, the phrase “you dis­ap­peared” is not a simple ac­cus­a­tion. It is a demand, a summons to account for your­self. And the phrase “do you think” pre­ced­ing it is not a ques­tion. It is a chal­lenge.

When someone asks “do you think you can justify to me why you dis­ap­peared?”, they are not really asking for a simple ex­plan­a­tion. They are asking for an act of atone­ment. But atone­ment cannot exist where reality has already sep­ar­ated two people's paths. Nor can it be ac­cep­ted by someone who, instead of listen­ing, is merely waiting for the moment to dismiss the answer as in­ad­equate.

Memory as a Courtroom

In such con­ver­sa­tions, memory acts as a judge. Not one who seeks the truth, however, but one who has already de­livered the verdict. One side accuses the other of with­draw­al, in­dif­fer­ence, aban­don­ment. And the other tries to explain that dis­ap­pear­ing was not an act against the other person, but an act for them­selves.

When a con­ver­sa­tion is groun­ded in the need to assign blame, every ex­plan­a­tion sounds like evasion. It is not a matter of right or wrong. It is a matter of dif­fer­ent ways of reading the same story. For one person, with­draw­al is aban­don­ment. For the other, it is sur­viv­al.

And the con­flict between them is nothing other than proof that the past is never static; it lives and breathes through memory and the meaning we give it.

Speech as a Weapon

The most dan­ger­ous point in such con­ver­sa­tions is the moment when a word becomes a weapon. When the ac­cus­a­tion of “dis­ap­pear­ing” turns into a re­proach that permits no re­but­tal. When one person's need to un­der­stand is not sincere, but serves as a means of im­pos­ing the weight of guilt on the other.

When “do you think” does not signify an open ques­tion, but sarcasm. When “welcome back to the world” is not a wish of good­will, but a bitter ref­er­ence to a sup­posed in­justice.

Such phrases are not dia­logue. They close off every pos­sible path to re­con­cili­ation. Because genuine dia­logue calls not for self-jus­ti­fic­a­tion, but for un­der­stand­ing.

The Disappearance That Was Not a Disappearance

When someone draws away, it does not mean they have ceased to exist. It means they have found a path that called them to follow it. When someone asks for an ex­plan­a­tion of another person's absence, they do not really want to un­der­stand. They want to hear that the absence was a mistake.

But it was not. Each of us has a journey of our own. Asking someone to return to a point that no longer exists is like de­mand­ing that they be born again with the same thoughts, the same feel­ings, the same ex­per­i­ences.

“Welcome to the World”

The con­ver­sa­tion's final words: “Welcome back to the world, then.”

What does it mean to welcome someone back to some­thing they never left?

This phrase is not a welcome. It is a silent way of saying: “I thought you were lost.” It is an in­dir­ect erasure of the other person's journey because it did not follow the routes that would have made us feel secure.

But no one returns to the world. The world is not a place. It is not a sphere you can enter and leave. It is where you stand at every moment, even when no one re­cog­nizes your pres­ence.

Forgiveness That Is Not Self-Justification

In such con­ver­sa­tions, saying “I'm sorry” is dan­ger­ous. Not because an apology is weak­ness, but because it can be mis­in­ter­preted as an ad­mis­sion of guilt that does not exist. When you apo­lo­gize for your absence, you are saying you owed it to someone to be there. But no one belongs to anyone else.

The truth is that you did not dis­ap­pear. You were not lost. You simply found your­self at a point where your paths no longer crossed. It is not aban­don­ment. It is the natural course of re­la­tion­ships that cannot endure with the same words, the same bound­ar­ies, the same demands.

The Return That Is Not a Return

You are not coming back to the world. You are not re­turn­ing to some­thing you never left. If there is a genuine “return,” it is the real­iz­a­tion that your worth is not de­term­ined by whether someone re­cog­nizes you or not.

Com­mu­nic­a­tion is dia­logue, not self-jus­ti­fic­a­tion. And un­der­stand­ing does not require ex­plan­a­tions. Only pres­ence.

Growth Beyond Guilt

Our re­spons­ib­il­ity is not to prove our in­no­cence in every lost re­la­tion­ship. Nor to con­vince others that our de­cisions were right. Our only re­spons­ib­il­ity is to ac­know­ledge our own part in the story and accept that others have theirs.

When com­mu­nic­a­tion becomes a bat­tle­field over who is right, no one can truly listen. But when it becomes a place where each person's truth can stand without having to dis­prove the other's, then perhaps there is room for some­thing beyond guilt and ac­cus­a­tion.

Perhaps there is room for genuine un­der­stand­ing.

Listen­ing does not mean agree­ing. Un­der­stand­ing does not mean giving in. For­giv­ing does not mean ac­cept­ing the burden of guilt that is not yours.
