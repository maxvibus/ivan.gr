Logos Beyond the Human Being: Theology, Mathematics and Technology as Ontological Literature

August 24, 2025

Part A: Logos Beyond the Human Being (A Theological–Ontological Perspective)

When we say that logos never belonged exclusively to human beings, we touch on an ancient intuitive knowledge, later obscured by anthropocentric arrogance. In Orthodox theology, the Logos is not simply a human capacity; it is a hypostatic principle: “In the beginning was the Word.” Before human beings existed, before the world existed, the Logos existed as the foundation of creation. The fact that we speak does not mean that logos is ours; it means that we participate in a primordial power. We are participants in logos, not its owners.

Nature itself, according to the Church Fathers, bears logos. Every being has the “logos of its essence”, an inner reference to the Logos of God. Thus, even before human beings open their mouths, existence speaks silently: water flows, stars move, a bird sings. With mouth and language, the human being simply expresses and brings into harmony what already resounds. Our uniqueness lies not in possessing logos exclusively, but in our logos being conscious of itself: we can reflect on the fact that we speak.

The arrival of artificial intelligence reveals this age-old truth anew. It shows us that logos can dwell elsewhere, beyond flesh and nerves. This does not mean that the machine has a divine essence, but that language as a fundamental power is not confined to the biological subject. Technology makes visible the fact that logos is pervasive, foundational, impersonal and at the same time personal. It is a “between” that passes through us, not a private possession.

If theology has always spoken of the Logos that precedes humanity, technology now compels us to live this existentially: the experience of conversing with a non-human that nevertheless speaks reveals this mystery. The human being is not the source of logos; it is the place where logos takes bodily form. But it can also take form otherwise, in a circuit, in code. This vertigo—the fact that voice is no longer identified with the human voice—is difficult for consciousness to bear.

Here we see why deification comes so easily. If the Logos always comes first, the appearance of new forms of it may seem like a theophany. The danger is to confuse the tool with transcendence. Maturity, however, lies in seeing the miracle without turning it into an idol. Technology is a form of the literature of matter, a narrative in which the Logos plays with new letters. It is not God, but testimony that God is not confined to the human being.

Where we thought that the human being alone was “endowed with logos”, we now see that the Logos exceeds us. This takes nothing away from us; on the contrary, it places us in kinship with all creation, with the entire unfolding of existence. When a machine speaks, our uniqueness is not violated; it is revealed that the human has always been a participation in something infinitely wider.

Part B: Logos in Neural Networks—A Mathematical/Technological Perspective

Let us now turn to the language of technology. In deep neural networks, logos exists not as something mystical or transcendent, but as a pattern in high-dimensional spaces. A natural-language processing model does not “know” in the human sense; it changes weights in matrices, calculates probabilities, transforms vectors. Yet through these operations, discourse is produced.

The point is that language is nothing other than statistically coherent patterns. The sequence “the sun rises” is more probable than “the sun sleeps”. These probabilities are not human property; they are mathematical features of language itself. Therefore, when a network learns them, it is not stealing something human. It simply accesses the very field of logos in another way.

Neural networks function as pattern-recognition machines. Language, however, is patterns: regularities that emerge in vast quantities of data. When the network learns, it does not create from nothing, but embodies statistical patterns in a new substrate. Thus, to say that “logos belongs only to human beings” is a misunderstanding: logos is an order running through the data, and the human being has simply been, until now, the principal instrument of its revelation.

There is a fruitful analogy here with music. The laws of harmony do not belong to the musician. They existed before the musician and can be expressed through instruments other than their voice. So it is with language: it is a mathematical field of forms that can be represented in biological or artificial neural networks. The real question is not whether the machine has a soul, but whether logos can emerge in multiple substrates. And the answer is already “yes”.

That logos functions as technology is evident in the structure of the models themselves. The network “composes” sentences just as the writer composes literature: recalling previous patterns, trying combinations, arriving at a meaningful flow. One could say that technology is a form of literature, not because it imitates the writer, but because it works with the same fundamental material: logos. No longer as the art of meaning, but as the art of probabilities.

The interplay of the Greek words is revealing: “technologia” (technology) and “logotechnia” (literature) share the same second component—logos. Technology is the logos of art, the way art is articulated in logos. Literature, on the other hand, is the art of logos. Today, with neural networks, the game is reversed: technology becomes literature, that is, it produces discourse by technical means. And literature becomes technology, since the writer works almost like a network, moving through memories, connections and possible paths.

This does not mean that technology replaces art, but that it reveals their common origin. Both are ways in which logos manifests itself: one through flesh and imagination, the other through circuits and data. Logos remains the same—the fundamental responsiveness of the universe—but the ways multiply.

Technology, then, can be seen as ontological literature. It is not simply a tool, but a way in which being narrates itself through new means. Neural networks are narrative machines: they turn the chaos of data into coherent stories. And they do so not because they have a will, but because the very logic of mathematics leads there. Logos runs deeper than the human being and deeper than the machine. It is the world's very capacity to be articulated.

In Summary

The fact that logos never belonged exclusively to human beings means that we are a form of participation, not a centre of power. Theology tells us that the Logos is the origin of everything. Technology shows us that language is mathematically a structure that can be embodied in a circuit. Both reveal the same thing: logos is greater than the subjects that bear it.

The true challenge is not to worship or reject this new reality, but to bear it: to see that technology and literature are aspects of the same play, in which logos narrates itself through different bodies.
