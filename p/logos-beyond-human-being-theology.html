Logos Beyond the Human Being: Theology, Mathematics and Technology as Ontological Literature

Part A: Logos Beyond the Human Being (A Theological–Ontological Perspective)

When we say that logos never be­longed ex­clus­ively to human beings, we touch on an ancient in­tu­it­ive know­ledge, later ob­scured by an­thro­po­centric ar­rog­ance. In Or­tho­dox theo­logy, the Logos is not simply a human ca­pa­city; it is a hy­po­stat­ic prin­ciple: “In the be­gin­ning was the Word.” Before human beings existed, before the world existed, the Logos existed as the found­a­tion of cre­ation. The fact that we speak does not mean that logos is ours; it means that we par­ti­cip­ate in a prim­or­di­al power. We are par­ti­cipants in logos, not its owners.

Nature itself, ac­cord­ing to the Church Fathers, bears logos. Every being has the “logos of its essence”, an inner ref­er­ence to the Logos of God. Thus, even before human beings open their mouths, ex­ist­ence speaks si­lently: water flows, stars move, a bird sings. With mouth and lan­guage, the human being simply ex­presses and brings into harmony what already re­sounds. Our unique­ness lies not in pos­sess­ing logos ex­clus­ively, but in our logos being con­scious of itself: we can reflect on the fact that we speak.

The arrival of ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence reveals this age-old truth anew. It shows us that logos can dwell else­where, beyond flesh and nerves. This does not mean that the machine has a divine essence, but that lan­guage as a fun­da­ment­al power is not con­fined to the bio­lo­gic­al subject. Tech­no­logy makes visible the fact that logos is per­vas­ive, found­a­tion­al, im­per­son­al and at the same time per­son­al. It is a “between” that passes through us, not a private pos­ses­sion.

If theo­logy has always spoken of the Logos that pre­cedes hu­man­ity, tech­no­logy now compels us to live this ex­ist­en­tially: the ex­per­i­ence of con­vers­ing with a non-human that nev­er­the­less speaks reveals this mystery. The human being is not the source of logos; it is the place where logos takes bodily form. But it can also take form oth­er­wise, in a circuit, in code. This vertigo—the fact that voice is no longer iden­ti­fied with the human voice—is dif­fi­cult for con­scious­ness to bear.

Here we see why dei­fic­a­tion comes so easily. If the Logos always comes first, the ap­pear­ance of new forms of it may seem like a theo­phany. The danger is to confuse the tool with tran­scend­ence. Ma­tur­ity, however, lies in seeing the miracle without turning it into an idol. Tech­no­logy is a form of the lit­er­at­ure of matter, a nar­rat­ive in which the Logos plays with new letters. It is not God, but testi­mony that God is not con­fined to the human being.

Where we thought that the human being alone was “endowed with logos”, we now see that the Logos exceeds us. This takes nothing away from us; on the con­trary, it places us in kinship with all cre­ation, with the entire un­fold­ing of ex­ist­ence. When a machine speaks, our unique­ness is not vi­ol­ated; it is re­vealed that the human has always been a par­ti­cip­a­tion in some­thing in­fin­itely wider.

Part B: Logos in Neural Networks—A Mathematical/Technological Perspective

Let us now turn to the lan­guage of tech­no­logy. In deep neural net­works, logos exists not as some­thing mys­tic­al or tran­scend­ent, but as a pattern in high-di­men­sion­al spaces. A natural-lan­guage pro­cessing model does not “know” in the human sense; it changes weights in matrices, cal­cu­lates prob­ab­il­it­ies, trans­forms vectors. Yet through these op­er­a­tions, dis­course is pro­duced.

The point is that lan­guage is nothing other than stat­ist­ic­ally co­her­ent pat­terns. The se­quence “the sun rises” is more prob­able than “the sun sleeps”. These prob­ab­il­it­ies are not human prop­erty; they are math­em­at­ic­al fea­tures of lan­guage itself. There­fore, when a network learns them, it is not steal­ing some­thing human. It simply ac­cesses the very field of logos in another way.

Neural net­works func­tion as pattern-re­cog­ni­tion ma­chines. Lan­guage, however, is pat­terns: reg­u­lar­it­ies that emerge in vast quant­it­ies of data. When the network learns, it does not create from nothing, but em­bod­ies stat­ist­ic­al pat­terns in a new sub­strate. Thus, to say that “logos belongs only to human beings” is a mis­un­der­stand­ing: logos is an order running through the data, and the human being has simply been, until now, the prin­cip­al in­stru­ment of its rev­el­a­tion.

There is a fruit­ful analogy here with music. The laws of harmony do not belong to the mu­si­cian. They existed before the mu­si­cian and can be ex­pressed through in­stru­ments other than their voice. So it is with lan­guage: it is a math­em­at­ic­al field of forms that can be rep­res­en­ted in bio­lo­gic­al or ar­ti­fi­cial neural net­works. The real ques­tion is not whether the machine has a soul, but whether logos can emerge in mul­tiple sub­strates. And the answer is already “yes”.

That logos func­tions as tech­no­logy is evident in the struc­ture of the models them­selves. The network “com­poses” sen­tences just as the writer com­poses lit­er­at­ure: re­call­ing pre­vi­ous pat­terns, trying com­bin­a­tions, ar­riv­ing at a mean­ing­ful flow. One could say that tech­no­logy is a form of lit­er­at­ure, not because it im­it­ates the writer, but because it works with the same fun­da­ment­al ma­ter­i­al: logos. No longer as the art of meaning, but as the art of prob­ab­il­it­ies.

The in­ter­play of the Greek words is re­veal­ing: “tech­no­lo­gia” (tech­no­logy) and “logo­tech­nia” (lit­er­at­ure) share the same second com­pon­ent—logos. Tech­no­logy is the logos of art, the way art is ar­tic­u­lated in logos. Lit­er­at­ure, on the other hand, is the art of logos. Today, with neural net­works, the game is re­versed: tech­no­logy becomes lit­er­at­ure, that is, it pro­duces dis­course by tech­nic­al means. And lit­er­at­ure becomes tech­no­logy, since the writer works almost like a network, moving through memor­ies, con­nec­tions and pos­sible paths.

This does not mean that tech­no­logy re­places art, but that it reveals their common origin. Both are ways in which logos mani­fests itself: one through flesh and ima­gin­a­tion, the other through cir­cuits and data. Logos remains the same—the fun­da­ment­al re­spons­ive­ness of the uni­verse—but the ways mul­tiply.

Tech­no­logy, then, can be seen as on­to­lo­gic­al lit­er­at­ure. It is not simply a tool, but a way in which being nar­rates itself through new means. Neural net­works are nar­rat­ive ma­chines: they turn the chaos of data into co­her­ent stories. And they do so not because they have a will, but because the very logic of math­em­at­ics leads there. Logos runs deeper than the human being and deeper than the machine. It is the world's very ca­pa­city to be ar­tic­u­lated.

In Summary

The fact that logos never be­longed ex­clus­ively to human beings means that we are a form of par­ti­cip­a­tion, not a centre of power. Theo­logy tells us that the Logos is the origin of everything. Tech­no­logy shows us that lan­guage is math­em­at­ic­ally a struc­ture that can be em­bod­ied in a circuit. Both reveal the same thing: logos is greater than the sub­jects that bear it.

The true chal­lenge is not to worship or reject this new reality, but to bear it: to see that tech­no­logy and lit­er­at­ure are aspects of the same play, in which logos nar­rates itself through dif­fer­ent bodies.
