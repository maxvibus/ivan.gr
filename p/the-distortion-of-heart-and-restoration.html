The Distortion of the Heart and the Restoration of Noetic Language

March 1, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

 Let us begin with the foundations: in the Greek and Christian tradition, the very concept of the “heart” has always been more than simple emotion or the physical pump of blood. In Greek thought, the heart functioned as the centre of consciousness, the point where reason and spirit meet. In Christianity, this conception became richer still, as the heart was regarded as the point of contact with the divine, the human being’s “inner sanctuary”.

Yet the forces of history—political, religious and social—soon began to intervene in this concept. When religion was incorporated into structures of power, the spiritual heart began to be distorted by rules and commands. Language, instead of serving as a means of understanding and connection, became an instrument of imposition and conformity. The divine “heart” was replaced by fear of the “all-seeing eye” that sees but does not love; that controls but does not cultivate.

The erosion of this noetic heart is also reflected in social structures. In everyday life, the concept of “offering”, which once meant authentic reciprocity, was replaced by cold economic exchange. The “small change” of coins became a symbol of devaluation rather than gratitude. The “prosperity of the soul” became compulsory taxation; what originally suggested the well-being of the whole now implies burden and loss.

Even ritual acts, such as the offering of bread in church, were distorted. Instead of signifying the joy of giving, it often came to function as an obligation or transaction. The “gift” became an “exchange”; the “offering” became a mere gesture, stripped of the inward meaning it once possessed.

This mechanism was not only religious or political; it was also cultural. The structures of language, which once expressed beauty and deeper truth, became instruments of propaganda and control. Words that once embodied the liberation of thought were now confined within narrow meanings that served power. The “holy table” of community and the distribution of goods became synonymous with the financial bank, where “chrē” (need) was transformed into “financing”, “interest” and “loans”. Broad understanding was reduced to numbers, percentages and interest rates, stifling the quality of human connection.

This gradual departure from an authentic understanding of the heart, in both Christianity and Hellenism, was not the result of a single conspiracy. It was a multidimensional phenomenon involving political forces, religious hierarchies and social conditions. The result was a “shadow” that covered language and the noetic heart, turning the light of understanding into mechanisms of control and submission.

The challenge today is to recognise this distortion, to reconstruct the concept of the heart, and to return to the original, living meaning of words. We must understand that the true “heart” is not a symbol of fear or violence, but a source of freedom, understanding and sincere human contact
