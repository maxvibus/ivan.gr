Where is the world?

Published September 7, 2026 · 9 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

There is a strange ex­per­i­ence most people know without giving it much thought: you look at a pho­to­graph of your­self and know that it is you. You were there, you re­mem­ber when it was taken, perhaps you took it your­self; there is no doubt about the iden­tity of the person you see, and yet some­thing in you reacts in­stantly: that is not what I am like. You do not need to examine the nose, the eyes or the shape of the face to reach this judge­ment. Usually the judge­ment has already arrived, and only af­ter­wards do you begin looking for its reason. You look more closely, try to identi­fy what does not fit, but what came first was not ana­lys­is. It was a re­cog­ni­tion that did not quite come about.

Until re­cently, this could have re­mained a minor pe­cu­li­ar­ity of pho­to­graphy. The lens freezes a frac­tion of a second, dis­tance changes pro­por­tions, the face we know in motion is im­mob­il­ised in an ex­pres­sion perhaps no one would have had time to see while stand­ing before us. Ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence, however, has added a pos­sib­il­ity that makes the problem harder. It can now gen­er­ate an image of you from a moment that never happened, in light that never fell on you, in a place you never visited, with a minute al­ter­a­tion of the face cor­res­pond­ing to no pho­to­graph­ic ex­pos­ure. And you may look at this image, which has no ex­ist­ence in history, and without think­ing ex­per­i­ence the op­pos­ite: I re­cog­nise myself.

Two con­di­tions thus appear side by side that we would nor­mally expect to agree. On one side is a real pho­to­graph, pos­sess­ing the full truth of its origin but somehow failing to return to a person the pres­ence they re­cog­nise as their own. On the other is a syn­thet­ic image cor­res­pond­ing to no event, yet capable of pro­du­cing that re­cog­ni­tion. The first can answer “did it happen?” with an un­ques­tion­able yes and “is it me?” with a strange no. The second almost re­verses the terms. The moment did not happen, but some­thing in the form is re­cog­nised.

It would be easy to at­trib­ute this to re­semb­lance and leave it there. One image cap­tured the fea­tures better, we might say, while the other dis­tor­ted them. Yet the ex­per­i­ence does not seem to operate so ana­lyt­ic­ally. Re­cog­nising a face does not usually wait for us to com­plete a check­list of mor­pho­lo­gic­al tests. We do not suc­cess­ively examine eyes, nose, mouth, jaw angle and the dis­tance between eye­brows before finally de­cid­ing whom we see. The face reaches us whole. Af­ter­wards we can take it apart, measure its pro­por­tions and de­scribe its fea­tures, but the first ap­pre­hen­sion has already oc­curred. Some­thing has presen­ted itself as someone before we can explain why.

Ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence sud­denly makes this old op­er­a­tion of per­cep­tion visible because it can create some­thing we had until now en­countered mainly in paint­ing, dreams or ima­gin­a­tion: a human face with the par­tic­u­lar­ity of a spe­cif­ic person, without the person bearing it ever having needed to exist. We do not see a generic “man” or “woman”, but someone of a par­tic­u­lar age, with a par­tic­u­lar gaze, asym­met­ries, wear­i­ness or calm, even that faint im­pres­sion that some­thing pre­ceded the moment we see and some­thing will follow it. Except there may have been neither a before nor an after. There was only the image.

What exactly exists, then?

The ques­tion becomes harder when the syn­thet­ic form does not leave us in­dif­fer­ent. A face that may cor­res­pond to no human being can evoke fear, at­trac­tion, trust or an un­ex­pec­ted sense of repose. It can become as­so­ci­ated with music, with a period of one's life, with a dif­fi­cult night, and years later the sight of it may still carry that re­la­tion­ship. If it is later es­tab­lished that the face was created en­tirely by an AI model, that in­form­a­tion cor­rects what we can claim about its origin, but it does not travel back in time to cancel what happened when we saw it. Perhaps the person did not exist. But the en­counter with their form did.

This does not mean that whatever we see exists because we see it. A dream does not prove that its char­ac­ters are walking some­where in the world, just as a syn­thet­ic image does not acquire an ori­gin­al with a bio­graphy because it moved someone. The same applies to emotion. The fact that some­thing gives me repose does not prove that its object exists outside me in the way I per­ceive it. But if, afraid of con­fus­ing the real with the ima­gin­ary, we go to the op­pos­ite extreme and say that nothing happened, we have made a dif­fer­ent mistake. For the repose oc­curred, the form was ap­pre­hen­ded, the memory took shape, and a real person's lived world changed. The origin may be syn­thet­ic without the event of ap­pre­hen­sion being syn­thet­ic.

Perhaps we need to sep­ar­ate two ques­tions that usually reach us joined to­geth­er. One is whether some­thing is true. The other is what must already have happened for us to be able to ask whether it is true. The second comes first, not because it matters more, but because truth needs some­thing upon which judge­ment can be made. To say “this person does not exist”, “this person” must already have existed for me. Denying their ex­tern­al ex­ist­ence does not abolish the phe­nomen­on about which the denial is uttered. On the con­trary, it pre­sup­poses it.

Here, form pre­cedes the person. Not as some meta­phys­ic­al entity waiting outside the world to acquire a body, nor as an idea secretly ex­ist­ing before things. It pre­cedes as a pos­sib­il­ity of ap­pear­ance. Before I know who it is, before I know whether they exist, before I es­tab­lish whether they were pho­to­graphed or gen­er­ated, some­thing has already ap­peared as a person. Truth does not create this ap­pear­ance after the fact; it comes to examine it.

And then a problem arises that is no longer par­tic­u­larly about ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence. If a form can appear, be ap­pre­hen­ded and enter the lived world without ne­ces­sar­ily cor­res­pond­ing to a person some­where outside us, where exactly does its ap­pear­ance happen? We could answer that it happens on the screen, but on the screen we find only points of light. Looking closer, we find nu­mer­ic­al values, elec­tric­al states, cir­cuits and com­pu­ta­tions. On the other side we find photons reach­ing the retina, elec­tro­chem­ic­al activ­ity, neural net­works and a brain pro­cessing in­form­a­tion. All this can be de­scribed, and the more we un­der­stand it, the more ac­cur­ate the de­scrip­tion becomes. Yet some­thing strange remains: nowhere among these things do we find, as one more object in the chain, the gaze that looked at us.

Pixels do not look. Photons are not peace­ful or threat­en­ing. A number is not beau­ti­ful, and a neuron is not a man stand­ing before us. We need not con­clude from this that there is some im­ma­ter­i­al region where forms reside. We need only refrain from pre­tend­ing that we have solved the problem because we have de­scribed the ma­ter­i­al events ac­com­pa­ny­ing it. Some­where in this process, more than the trans­mis­sion of in­form­a­tion oc­curred. For the one who saw, a world ap­peared, and within the world someone ap­peared.

Perhaps this is where the word “world” begins to become un­stable. We use it every day as though we knew exactly what it meant. The world is everything that exists, everything around us, the Earth, the uni­verse, reality. But if one asks not what the world con­tains but where the world is, the answers soon begin to col­lapse. It is here. Where is here? On Earth. The Earth is in the solar system, the solar system in the galaxy, the galaxy in the uni­verse. And where is the uni­verse?

The ques­tion sud­denly seems mis­taken, because every place we could offer as an answer is already within what we are trying to locate. If we say the world is every­where, we have not solved the problem; we have merely turned “where” into “every­where”. And one can persist with the most child­like and perhaps most dif­fi­cult ques­tion: yes, every­where—but where?

There need not be an answer. Perhaps the mistake lies in the very demand to locate the world as though it were one more object in a larger space. But then the ques­tion's failure reveals some­thing usually hidden: whenev­er we ask where some­thing is, we have already ac­cep­ted, without ne­go­ti­ation, a world in which “wheres” exist. The world does not appear as one of its objects. It is already there whenev­er any object can appear.

And perhaps that is why ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence, with no in­ten­tion of doing philo­sophy, has brought us before such an old ques­tion by an en­tirely new route. Until now, the chief anxiety about syn­thet­ic images has been whether we will be able to dis­tin­guish true from false. The anxiety is jus­ti­fied and will prob­ably grow. But there is some­thing before it that we risk missing because we rush to verify. Before de­cid­ing whether a face is real or syn­thet­ic, we have already seen it as a face. Before check­ing whether a moment happened, the moment has already ap­peared. Before de­cid­ing what stands before us, we are already within a world in which there can be a “before us”.

This does not equate a human being with their image, nor an ex­ist­ing person with a syn­thet­ic face. A human being has a body, a history, con­tinu­ity; they can suffer, desire, refuse, leave, die. We have no reason to at­trib­ute any of these things to a face pro­duced by a machine. The dif­fer­ence is enorm­ous and must remain. The strange­ness lies else­where: this dif­fer­ence becomes avail­able to our judge­ment after some­thing has already reached us as a form. The de­cision “this is real” does not come first so that we may then be per­mit­ted to see it. First it appears, and then the work of dis­tin­guish­ing begins.

Perhaps this is why a real pho­to­graph can fail where a nonex­ist­ent moment suc­ceeds. It is not because the second is truer. It is because truth and pres­ence are not syn­onyms. One con­cerns, among other things, what happened and what exists in­de­pend­ently of our ap­pre­hen­sion. The other con­cerns the fact that some­thing has already reached us and become part of the world we ex­per­i­ence. Con­fus­ing them is dan­ger­ous, but equally dan­ger­ous is the demand that the second dis­ap­pear because it failed the test of the first.

A person who never existed may never acquire a life because someone looked at them. But they may already have ac­quired a place in a life. And a person who un­deni­ably stood before a lens may look at the exact record of that moment and fail to find in it what they re­cog­nise, without thought, as them­selves. If these two things can happen, perhaps the age of syn­thet­ic forms obliges us not merely to become better at de­tect­ing false­hood. It obliges us to look at some­thing that was there long before ma­chines, and that ma­chines—pre­cisely because they can now con­struct forms with no pre­ced­ent in the world—make harder to ignore.

Before the ques­tion of whether what I see is true, the fact that I see some­thing has already happened. Before the who, there is the this. Before veri­fic­a­tion, there is ap­pear­ance. And before we can even ask where what ap­peared is located, we have already used what we have never managed to locate.

The world.
