The Unseen Continuity of Dependence: From Kapodistrias to the Eurozone

Published on June 22, 2025 · 2 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The history of Greece after the Re­volu­tion of 1821 is often presen­ted as a journey towards in­de­pend­ence. Yet behind the of­fi­cial nar­rat­ive, a core of en­dur­ing de­pend­ence remains: on foreign Powers, foreign In­sti­tu­tions, and today — on supra­na­tion­al mech­an­isms of control.

Ioannis Ka­pod­istri­as, the first Gov­ernor of free Greece, en­vi­sioned an in­de­pend­ent state. From the outset, however, his vision ran up against the in­terests and am­bi­tions of the three Great Powers. His as­sas­sin­a­tion in 1831 was not simply the fall of a man, but the col­lapse of the pos­sib­il­ity of found­ing a state with a will of its own. His re­place­ment by the un­der­age Bav­ari­an Otto, and the im­pos­i­tion of the Regency (1833–1835), em­bod­ied the trans­ition from an attempt at self-in­sti­tu­tion to a regime of ex­tern­al ap­point­ment.

From its very first steps, Greece became an “in­de­pend­ent state under guar­an­tee”: a paradox of polit­ic­al philo­sophy. It did not de­term­ine the terms of its own in­de­pend­ence.

The truth is that this logic never dis­ap­peared. It merely changed form.

In the modern era, Greece’s mem­ber­ship of the European Union, and es­pe­cially the Euro­zone, offered sta­bil­ity and in­ter­na­tion­al support. But along­side these, it also entails:

  • the loss of mon­et­ary policy,

  • de­pend­ence on European fin­an­cial dis­cip­line,

  • the ob­lig­a­tion to im­ple­ment supra­na­tion­al de­cisions.

Par­tic­u­larly from 2010 onwards, when Greece entered the Memor­anda pro­grammes, it was placed under en­hanced sur­veil­lance — not very dif­fer­ent, in its logic, from the Regency. There may be no foreign king, but there are ex­tern­al “tech­nic­al teams” that de­term­ine what can be done.

This de­pend­ence is not merely eco­nom­ic; it is in­sti­tu­tion­al, polit­ic­al and — ul­ti­mately — cul­tur­al. Greece seems to re­pro­duce the same mech­an­ism in a new guise: it does not for­mu­late the Logos of its in­sti­tu­tions for itself, but adapts to logics already ar­tic­u­lated else­where.

The crucial ques­tion is no longer “whether” we are in­de­pend­ent. The ques­tion is:

can we ar­tic­u­late an in­sti­tu­tion­al Logos of our own?

Ka­pod­istri­as tried. He did not have time. Today, despite our de­pend­ence, we have a voice. Will we use it for sub­stance or for su­per­fi­cial changes of power?

The his­tor­ic­al con­tinu­ity of de­pend­ence can be broken only through in­tern­al change. Not against others, but in support of our self-aware­ness as a com­munity with a Logos.
