When Grace Reaches You First

August 11, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

Perhaps you have received something you neither asked for nor expected.
It may have been a look, an act of care, forgiveness.
And if you are honest with yourself, perhaps you remember the awkwardness you felt.
Why? Because receiving without having “earned” it exposes you—and exposes you at your core.

Theology calls this “something” grace.
And it places it not at the end of a moral effort, but at the beginning; grace reaches you before you take the step.
It is not your reward; it is the ground on which you already stand.
If you think about it, this is what unsettles you: you do not hold the starting point of the relationship.
Someone calls you before you have a chance to call them.

Philosophy, on the other hand, tries to grasp the gift without religious terms.
Jean-Luc Marion spoke of the “given phenomenon”—something that appears without our being able to control it or give something in return.
Jacques Derrida went so far as to say that the true gift is one we cannot recognise as a gift at the moment it is given, because otherwise we enter a logic of exchange.

Psychology, more down to earth, says that when I receive something, I reveal to myself and to the other person that I am not self-sufficient.
And this gives rise to vulnerability.
Yet vulnerability itself is the bridge that brings intimacy.

If you bring all this together, you will see that awkwardness before a gift is not a sign of ingratitude; it is the price of truth.
Because the truth is that your worth is grounded not in your actions or your self-sufficiency, but in the fact that you already belong within a relationship.
Ontologically, grace is not a “thing” you receive, but the very fact that your existence is a continual response to a call that precedes it.

The difficult thing is to stand there without diminishing it.
Without trying to balance it out by giving something back immediately.
Without covering it with words so that your emotion will not show.

Perhaps this is precisely the challenge: to let grace find you unconditionally.
Not because you are passive, but because only in this way can you see your true measure:
the measure of a being that exists not thanks to its actions, but thanks to the relationship that gives birth to it and sustains it.

And if someone today looks at you or speaks to you in a way that leaves you defenceless, do not rush to regain control.
Stay a little while in that awkwardness.
Because it may be the clearest moment in which you have seen who you are.
