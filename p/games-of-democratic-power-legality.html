Games of Democratic Power: Legality, Morality and the Silent Will

Published on March 7, 2026 · 5 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Demo­cracy as we un­der­stand it is not simply the direct ex­pres­sion of society’s will, but a complex system of pro­ced­ures, rules and le­gis­lat­ive con­struc­tions. Elec­tions, quotas, seats and rules of par­ti­cip­a­tion all make up a power game that often sep­ar­ates of­fi­cial le­git­im­a­tion from genuine social consent. The ques­tion of ab­sten­tion, blank and invalid ballots, and the dis­tri­bu­tion of seats among small parties reveals not merely stat­ist­ic­al or nu­mer­ic­al issues but also a pro­found moral and ex­ist­en­tial in­equal­ity.

In prac­tic­al terms, seats in Greek elec­tions are al­loc­ated on the basis of valid ballots, while blank and invalid ballots are re­cor­ded only stat­ist­ic­ally. Ab­sten­tion is not even in­cluded in the of­fi­cial pro­por­tion of seats. This means that a party’s share of 40% of valid ballots, in an elec­tion with 50% turnout, trans­lates into just 20% actual social support. The same applies to all the other parties. The result is that a small per­cent­age of cit­izens can decide for the ma­jor­ity, while the rest remain silent or in­vis­ible in the of­fi­cial results.

This dif­fer­ence between nu­mer­ic­al leg­al­ity and moral au­thor­ity is fun­da­ment­al. While the system appears to turn the will of the minor­ity into lawful power, it does not reflect real social strength. Ab­sten­tion, which in the most recent elec­tions reached around 47% of re­gistered voters, to­geth­er with blank and invalid ballots, reveals a silent protest that nev­er­the­less remains polit­ic­ally in­vis­ible. Society shows that its rep­res­ent­a­tion in­cludes neither the choice it made nor the choice it did not make — that is, silence and the refusal to par­ti­cip­ate are equally im­port­ant, but are not in­sti­tu­tion­ally re­cog­nised.

More im­port­antly, the system does not merely le­git­im­ise the leading party. It also pro­duces other parties with small shares of seats — parties that receive 1.5%, 3%, 6% or 10% of re­gistered voters. In prac­tice, these parties do not sub­stant­ively rep­res­ent the people, but add mouths to the game, giving the il­lu­sion of demo­crat­ic par­ti­cip­a­tion. This con­di­tion creates a deeper moral problem: the de­cisions made by these small parties appear to carry the same au­thor­ity as those of the leading party, when in reality their social base is limited. Equal­ity in rep­res­ent­a­tion is only ap­par­ent — a struc­tured false­hood the system uses to project le­git­im­acy.

The moral in­equal­ity becomes ap­par­ent when we ask: does a de­cision by a party that ac­tu­ally rep­res­ents 20% of society carry the same moral au­thor­ity as one by a party that rep­res­ents 1.5%? The same applies to all the others. The le­gis­lat­ive process appears fair, but in reality it le­git­im­ises de­cisions that carry no social weight. The result is that the demo­crat­ic process becomes a game in which numbers produce lawful power without genuine social consent. This is the deeper moral scandal you are point­ing to: laws and rules were not made to serve the people, but to create le­git­im­ised pro­ced­ures, re­gard­less of who ac­tu­ally sup­ports them.

The silent protest of cit­izens who abstain or cast blank or invalid ballots is not mere inertia. It is a con­scious choice not to re­cog­nise the de­ceit­ful game. Cit­izens see that, even if they vote, de­cisions will con­tin­ue to be made by a small minor­ity, and the re­main­ing par­ti­cipants add no genuine rep­res­ent­a­tion, only ap­par­ent le­git­im­acy. Here, ab­sten­tion becomes a form of polit­ic­al ex­pres­sion showing that the moral au­thor­ity of power is not re­cog­nised.

The con­sequence of this system is twofold. First, it reduces cit­izens’ trust in in­sti­tu­tions, since cit­izens un­der­stand that their par­ti­cip­a­tion changes es­sen­tially nothing. Second, it strengthens the con­cen­tra­tion of power in small minor­it­ies, which appear nu­mer­ic­ally but not morally le­git­im­ised. Through this ac­count­ing ap­proach, demo­cracy appears to func­tion while in sub­stance by­passing the moral di­men­sion of social consent.

What makes the game par­tic­u­larly de­ceit­ful is le­git­im­a­tion through false weight­ings: the system presents de­cisions by small minor­it­ies as equi­val­ent to de­cisions that would have a broader social base, while the actual numbers and the silence of the ma­jor­ity are dis­reg­arded. The leading party, with 20% actual support, and the others, with per­cent­ages car­ry­ing minimal social weight, decide for every­one else, cre­at­ing a moral deficit that remains in­vis­ible amid the system’s ap­par­ent sta­bil­ity.

The moral di­men­sion is crucial. It is not enough to look at who won, but at who has the right to decide for every­one else. The system does not re­cog­nise the social reality of ab­sten­tion and small per­cent­ages, and thus trans­forms a minor­ity into power. The result is that leg­al­ity and moral au­thor­ity diverge dan­ger­ously. Ab­sten­tion, blank ballots and small per­cent­ages are not merely nu­mer­ic­al phe­nom­ena: they reveal a chal­lenge to the frame­work itself.

Demo­cracy as prac­tised today is at once nu­mer­ic­al and ex­ist­en­tial: the arith­met­ic appears to work, society sees a system that “works”, but the ex­ist­en­tial and moral di­men­sion reveals that the real will remains silent or ex­cluded. The games of demo­crat­ic power concern not only who gets elected, but also how society is rep­res­en­ted in sub­stance, and what moral au­thor­ity belongs to the power pro­duced by lawful yet de­ceit­ful pro­ced­ures.

Taken to­geth­er, the full picture is this: demo­cracy is a le­git­im­ised pro­ced­ure in which small minor­it­ies can decide for every­one, while the re­main­ing par­ti­cipants func­tion as “sym­bol­ic rep­res­ent­at­ives”, without real social strength. The silence of ab­sten­tion and the cal­cu­lated par­ti­cip­a­tion of small parties reveal the moral deficit and show that society’s actual will does not co­in­cide with of­fi­cial le­git­im­a­tion. Demo­cracy appears to func­tion, but in reality it plays a de­ceit­ful game in which arith­met­ic re­places mor­al­ity, and sym­bol­ic voices le­git­im­ise de­cisions with no real social base.
