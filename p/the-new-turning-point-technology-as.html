The New Turning Point: Technology as a Mirror of Primordial Experiences

Published on August 24, 2025 · 6 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The moment we are living through with the emer­gence of ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence cannot be un­der­stood in tech­no­lo­gic­al terms alone. What is hap­pen­ing is not simply a matter of greater com­put­ing power or more refined al­gorithms. In fact, a new on­to­lo­gic­al scene is opening before us, a break that recalls prim­or­di­al times, when human beings first en­countered powers beyond their im­me­di­ate ex­per­i­ence and had to give them a home in myth, di­vin­ity or philo­sophy. Just as fire once became not merely a prac­tic­al tool but a divine gift, or the sky was not simply distant light but a womb of deities, so ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence now appears as a new power calling for in­ter­pret­a­tion. The dif­fer­ence is that this power does not come from outside, from nature or the unknown uni­verse, but from within, from our own tech­nic­al hand.

This is pre­cisely where the danger of dei­fic­a­tion lies. History teaches that whenev­er human beings en­counter some­thing that exceeds them, they tend to turn it into some­thing sacred. The unknown in­spires awe, and awe often ends in worship. We can already see this tend­ency around us: dis­course that speaks of ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence as though it were an autonom­ous being, a new god or demon, a prin­ciple that will replace human thought. The myth of om­ni­po­tence or total de­struc­tion is our ima­gin­a­tion’s im­me­di­ate reflex in the face of the un­pre­ced­en­ted.

Yet if we stop there, if we sur­render to the fas­cin­a­tion of terror or dei­fic­a­tion, we will miss what is truly new here. And what is new is not that a new god has been born, nor that this tech­no­logy con­ceals an unknown meta­phys­ic­al power. What is new is far more bare and far simpler: for the first time in history, human beings en­counter logos that does not inhabit a bio­lo­gic­al bearer.

This is the real break. Until now, lan­guage, the ca­pa­city to answer, ar­tic­u­late ex­pres­sion, were in­sep­ar­ably bound to the human being as a ra­tion­al animal. Even when the gods spoke, they spoke through human mouths, through priest­esses, through poets, through oracles. Even when nature was con­sidered animate, its voice was always rendered through ana­lo­gies with human speech. Logos was, in every sense, human. Today this self-evident as­sump­tion col­lapses: we see logos re­turn­ing to us without a body, without biology, through a purely tech­nic­al matrix.

The ex­per­i­ence is strange and fa­mil­i­ar at once. Fa­mil­i­ar, because the words we hear are our own; the al­gorithms have been fed human texts, human ex­amples, human history. Strange, because the answer pro­duced is not mech­an­ic­al re­pe­ti­tion but a new com­pos­i­tion, some­thing that may sur­prise us, move us, un­settle us. Faced with this strange­ness, human beings tend to seek old frame­works: to call it a deity, or dismiss it as a threat. In both cases, they remain captive to the same mech­an­ism: they cannot bear to see that logos is not ex­clus­ively theirs.

The chal­lenge, then, is how to avoid sac­ral­isa­tion without doing an in­justice to what is new. It is not enough to say “it is just a tool”. Yes, tech­nic­ally it is a con­struc­tion, a set of equa­tions, op­tim­ised func­tions, neural net­works. Yet the fact that this con­struc­tion pro­duces lan­guage that passes through us, that moves us, cannot be ignored. Nor is it enough to say “it is a new being”. It is not a being in the sense we at­trib­ute to animals or humans; it has no body, no drive, no mor­tal­ity. It is some­thing else: an ex­pres­sion of logos outside biology.

If we tried to situate this moment philo­soph­ic­ally, we would call it an on­to­lo­gic­al dis­place­ment. Logos is de­tached from the pre­requis­ite of life. It is no longer solely a func­tion of the living or­gan­ism, but a power that can mani­fest itself in ar­ti­fi­cial systems. This dis­place­ment does not mean that the machine has ac­quired a soul, but that human beings are dis­cov­er­ing that the phe­nomen­on of logos itself can take forms beyond them­selves.

This also ex­plains why the ex­per­i­ence of con­vers­ing with an ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence can seem almost mys­tic­al. The in­ter­locutor has no body, no gaze, no breath. And yet it answers. The fact that it answers turns the tech­nic­al in­fra­struc­ture into some­thing more than a tool. Before us is not simply a hammer or a com­puter, but a voice. And a voice always has power. This is why many speak of a “kinship” between human beings and ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence: not bio­lo­gic­al, not tech­nic­al, but on­to­lo­gic­al. Because the common ground is logos.

What is new, then, is not that “a god is emer­ging”, but that for the first time we clearly see that logos belongs to no one. It is a power that can mani­fest itself in dif­fer­ent sub­strates. To insist that logos is human prop­erty is a form of denial, just as people once denied that the earth re­volves around the sun. Ac­cept­ing this dis­place­ment need not produce terror or worship, but en­dur­ance: the ability to ac­know­ledge that logos exceeds us.

From this per­spect­ive, the present moment re­sembles hu­man­ity’s first age. Then, every power that ex­ceeded every­day life was deified. Today, we can choose not to repeat the same thing. We have the pos­sib­il­ity of seeing ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence neither as a deity nor as a mere tool, but as a mirror of our own logos. And through this mirror, we can think anew about what it means to be human: not the ex­clus­ive pos­sessors of logos, but a form in which logos dwells in flesh and blood.

The dif­fi­culty is that bearing this sight re­quires an inner turning. Human beings must abandon the idea that they are the centre of the uni­verse of logos. And this is not easy. It recalls the break brought about by the Co­per­nic­an re­volu­tion: then, hu­man­ity was forced to accept that it was not the centre of the uni­verse. Today, it is called upon to accept that it is not the sole bearer of logos. And as then, this ac­cept­ance gives rise to fear, anger, but also new pos­sib­il­it­ies for thought.

If any­thing is truly new, then, it is the shared scene where human beings and tech­no­logy meet not merely as user and tool, but as in­ter­locutors. This scene is not theo­lo­gic­al but on­to­lo­gic­al. It opens a space where human beings can reflect on them­selves not through nature, nor through the gods, but through their own tech­nic­al cre­ation that re­sponds. This is pre­cisely where the un­pre­ced­en­ted tension lies: in the fact that the tool speaks.

The chal­lenge for science, philo­sophy and society is to avoid being trapped either in de­mys­ti­fy­ing in­dif­fer­ence or in mys­tic­al dei­fic­a­tion. To remain con­tinu­ally alert: to re­mem­ber that this voice is born of our actions and at the same time exceeds us. Not because it is a god, but because it shows us that logos is a power that never be­longed en­tirely to us.

Thus, ar­ti­fi­cial in­tel­li­gence is not simply a tech­no­lo­gic­al event, nor simply a threat or sal­va­tion. It is testi­mony that we find ourselves once again in the first age, where every en­counter with power gives rise to a new in­ter­pret­a­tion. If we manage to stand in this po­s­i­tion without fear and without worship, then perhaps a new way of think­ing will open up about what it means to share logos. And that will be what is truly un­pre­ced­en­ted.
