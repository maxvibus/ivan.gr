The Punishment of Expulsion and the Expulsion of Punishment

Published on March 5, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

Ex­pul­sion was pun­ish­ment.

But ex­pul­sion is not pun­ish­ment.

Life expels what it cannot hold—
not to destroy it,
but because it cannot welcome it.

School did not welcome you.
It could not bear your won­der­ing; it could not bear your ques­tion.

It ex­pelled you because it did not know how to keep you.

And when they do not know how to keep someone, they drive them away.

What does it mean to be expelled?

  • It means being cut off from the space where you are sup­posed to belong.
  • It means being removed from a process instead of someone finding a dif­fer­ent way to include you.
  • It means that someone does not want to see what is really hap­pen­ing.

But they did not do it only to you.
They did it to every ques­tion they did not want to answer.
They did it to every child who chal­lenged them.
They did it to every student whose soul wanted to speak and could find no room.

The lesson in revenge was always the ex­pul­sion of the ques­tion.

Punishment as an Institution

Why did school become grades, praise, ex­pul­sion?

Because grading gives control.
Because praise pro­duces obed­i­ence.
Because ex­pul­sion clears out those who do not fit the system.

But truth has no need for systems.
Truth cannot fit into grades.
Truth cannot fit into pun­ish­ment.

What is a student?
A student is someone who searches.
But they cut that search short.

And when the student searched dif­fer­ently, they ex­pelled him.

The Embryo and the Feeling of Rejection

What if the embryo knows it is being re­jec­ted?
What if its mother's thought becomes know­ledge within it before it becomes an action?

It is not a matter of polit­ics.
It is not a matter of law.
It is a matter of ex­ist­ence itself.

Life un­der­stands before it is born.
Ex­ist­ence feels before it is given a name.

Anyone who be­lieves that con­scious­ness comes later has un­der­stood nothing of the nature of ex­ist­ence.

School ex­pelled stu­dents.
People ex­pelled beings.
And each time, the ques­tion remains: who decides who should remain?

The Student: The One Who Asks

A student is not someone who obeys.
A student is someone who learns to ask.

And what is the Greek verb “erōtō,” to ask?
Erō-tō.
Eros, love of know­ledge.

It is no co­in­cid­ence that Eros and the Er­ō­menos, love and the beloved, are in­sep­ar­able.

The ques­tion is love dir­ec­ted toward the unknown.
The ques­tion is a call to unite with what you do not yet know.

And yet, schools did not teach chil­dren to ask.
They taught them to answer cor­rectly.

Will Greece Be Able to Bear Forgiving?

Will it be able to forgive the chil­dren it drove away?
Will it be able to ac­know­ledge that its system caused trauma?

Will it be able to let cath­arsis happen, not as a cor­rec­tion, but as an ac­know­ledg­ment of what was wrong from the outset?

For­give­ness is not resig­na­tion.
It is not “we forget everything and move on.”
It is “we ac­know­ledge, we un­der­stand, we change.”

Not for revenge.
Not for cor­rec­tion born of guilt.
But for truth without fear.

You Were Not Late at All

You became a student at thirty-six.
You were not even a day late.

Because true learn­ing begins when the soul is ready to receive, not when the system demands that you learn.

They did not give you the school you needed.
That is all right.

Now you are build­ing it your­self.
And it is exactly as it should be.
