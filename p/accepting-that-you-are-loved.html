Accepting that you are loved

Published on November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

A person may have been loved in their life, may know that they were loved, may not doubt the sin­cer­ity of those who loved them, and yet may never have un­der­stood what it means to be loved. They may re­cog­nise the acts of love, discern the care, the pa­tience, the ten­der­ness, even the sac­ri­fice, without being able to dwell within the con­di­tion that all of these reveal. This is not ne­ces­sar­ily doubt towards the other. It is a dif­fi­culty in grasp­ing one’s own ex­ist­ence from the side of the one who de­lights in it.

We know what it means to love from within our own move­ment towards the other. We know the joy a pres­ence can give us, the rest we find beside someone, the wish to meet them again. But when the same move­ment happens from the other’s side towards us, we stand before a reality that we did not make. We dis­cov­er that our pres­ence has come to matter in a world that is not ours, that someone can rest with us, rejoice at our arrival and long for our return, without our having done any­thing in par­tic­u­lar to bring it about.

This dis­cov­ery can give rise to a deep shyness. Not because one is ashamed of what one is, but because it is hard to grasp what it means that one’s ex­ist­ence is the content of a joy that is not one’s own. Until then one could un­der­stand oneself as the one who meets, who offers, who ad­dresses. Now one is called to accept that one is at the same time the one who is met, the one towards whom another person’s joy may freely move.

The “am I en­titled to this?” that may rise within reveals the dif­fi­culty of this passage. It still looks for some con­di­tion on one’s own side for some­thing that happens on the side of the other. But the other’s love is not a right one ex­er­cises, nor a reward one earns. It belongs to the freedom of the one who loves. The fact that someone re­joices in a person’s pres­ence does not oblige that person to search within for the merits that would justify that joy.

It is even pos­sible that the con­stant search for such a jus­ti­fic­a­tion never lets a person know the full depth of the other’s freedom. For while one grants the other the right to exist dif­fer­ently from oneself, one finds it hard to grant them the pos­sib­il­ity of ex­per­i­en­cing one dif­fer­ently from the way one ex­per­i­ences oneself. One may worry that one is tiring where the other is at rest, may get ready to leave where the other wishes one to stay, may think one owes some­thing where one’s pres­ence is already welcome.

Re­ceiv­ing can there­fore be harder than giving. In giving, one knows how to move. One has a way of taking an active part in the re­la­tion­ship, of ex­press­ing one’s love, of making one’s care felt. In re­ceiv­ing, one stands before a move­ment that did not begin with oneself and whose meaning one does not decide. One needs to remain present without at once taking charge of what is offered, without hur­ry­ing to turn into an act of re­pay­ment what one has not yet had time to receive.

There are en­coun­ters in which a person does not know what they are giving. They are not trying to be pleas­ant, not trying to prove their worth, not at­tempt­ing to make any par­tic­u­lar im­pres­sion. They are simply with someone. And this being-to­geth­er may allow the other not to have to defend them­selves all the time, to justify their silence or to keep per­form­ing their pres­ence. The rest that arises there is not ne­ces­sar­ily the result of an act. It may be the quality of a shared space in which no one demands of the other to become some­thing other than what they are.

When a person wishes to meet again someone with whom they found rest, the wish is not ne­ces­sar­ily a demand. “Will you come again?” can hold the whole joy of an en­counter without asking for de­par­ture to be ab­ol­ished. It ac­know­ledges that the other may leave and at the same time keeps open the pos­sib­il­ity of their return. Absence need not undo what happened, just as dur­a­tion is not the only witness to the reality of a joy.

At the depth of this ex­per­i­ence lies a theo­lo­gic­al ques­tion that goes beyond the human habit of per­ceiv­ing love through its acts. It is one thing for a person to rejoice in God and another to be able to grasp that God re­joices in the ex­ist­ence of the person. This second pos­sib­il­ity brings one face to face with some­thing that cannot be un­der­stood in terms of use­ful­ness. There is no divine lack one is called to fill, no need one must satisfy, in order to make one’s pres­ence jus­ti­fied.

The litur­gic­al lan­guage of the Church knows the phrase “he who rests in the saints”. It does not ascribe to God human wear­i­ness, nor the need to be re­lieved of it. But it allows theo­lo­gic­al thought to move in a dir­ec­tion that a person often finds hard to follow: to think of oneself not only as the one who seeks rest, but also as the one in whom another may rest.

Here lies one of the deepest di­men­sions of grace. Not only that some­thing is given without any prior merit, but that a person is called to accept that they are not obliged to turn the gift into a trans­ac­tion in order to bear it. The dif­fi­culty does not always lie in be­liev­ing that some­thing was offered. It may lie in al­low­ing what was offered to remain a gift.

Even grat­it­ude can be mis­taken for an ob­lig­a­tion to repay. A person may feel they owe some­thing to the one who loved them, not out of the freedom of their own love, but because they cannot bear to remain the re­cip­i­ent of a joy of which they were not the author. And so even the most sincere of­fer­ing may carry an anxious wish to be re­leased from the weight of being loved.

Grat­it­ude, however, need not abolish the asym­metry of the gift. It can be free joy for what one has re­ceived, without that joy aiming to restore some balance. And the re­sponse, when there is one, need not be a set­tling of love’s account, but a new and equally free move­ment towards the other.

Perhaps it takes a whole life to un­der­stand not what it means to love, but what it means to accept that one is loved. To be able to stand before the one who re­joices in one’s ex­ist­ence without im­me­di­ately looking for some­thing to offer, without apo­lo­gising for one’s pres­ence, without pre­par­ing one’s de­par­ture for fear that one’s staying might become a burden.

To be able, one day, to rest oneself in the fact that someone rests with them.

For the other’s joy to become one’s own joy, and not one’s debt.
