Law and Presence — From Ancient Law to Relationship: When Nature Does Not Obey

Published on July 7, 2025 · 3 min read

AI-as­sisted trans­la­tion from the Greek. Greek original

The words we use to de­scribe the world carry the memory of ages. The word “law” is not neutral. It is charged with theo­lo­gic­al, legal, philo­soph­ic­al and cul­tur­al pro­jec­tions. Law once meant the will of god, the order of the uni­verse, the moral struc­ture of ex­ist­ence. It was the un­chan­ging back­ground of the world: that which does not change, does not ne­go­ti­ate, does not enter into re­la­tion­ship.

But nature itself seems to say oth­er­wise. Physics, chem­istry, biology—sci­ences of the visible and the ex­per­i­ment­al—no longer rest on ab­so­lute con­stants. Every law, however general it may seem, holds under con­di­tions. There are ma­ter­i­als that, when heated, do not expand but con­tract. There are phe­nom­ena that change de­pend­ing on whether we observe them. There are be­ha­viours that escape linear caus­al­ity.

This does not mean that nature vi­ol­ates the law. It means that law is not what we thought it was.

Law is not essence; it is a condition.

The word “law” enters science with philo­soph­ic­al au­thor­ity. But ul­ti­mately, it is nothing more than a reg­u­lar­ity de­scrib­ing the most fre­quent outcome under certain con­di­tions. It has no on­to­lo­gic­al au­thor­ity—it has a de­script­ive func­tion.

Law does not say “what is”; it says “what usually happens when you do this”.

Con­sequently, when we change the con­di­tions, the “law” does not col­lapse—it simply reveals its loc­al­ity. There is no single law, but many cross-sec­tions of reality, each of which is re­vealed under a par­tic­u­lar pres­ence.

Presence modifies the form of the world

The concept of law, as quantum physics has shown, depends on the ob­serv­er’s po­s­i­tion. Not because the ob­serv­er “changes it by willing it”, but because the phe­nomen­on as such is not fully de­term­ined before the in­ter­ac­tion.

This calls on us to re­con­sider the root of know­ledge:
– Can there be law without a subject?
– Can there be reality without pres­ence?

Perhaps the world is not a ready-made text that we read, but a field that takes shape as we stand before it. Then “law” is not the truth of a system, but the form truth takes within the en­counter.

Philosophy already knew this; now matter shows it too

The thought of Merleau-Ponty, Heide­g­ger, and, much earlier, Her­ac­litus or the Church Fathers has already re­vealed that truth is not an object—but an event.

Truth is not what lies there for us to dis­cov­er, but what is born when I truly exist before some­thing.

And now nature itself, in extreme ex­per­i­ments, tells us this in the most ma­ter­i­al way: “You do not know me unless you stand before me. And I do not always stand in the same way.”

Towards a new definition of law

If we wish to speak truly of law in our time, we must leave behind the fantasy of fixed order and embrace the risk of re­la­tion­ship.

Law is not the fixed truth that holds without us.
It is the form truth takes when our gaze comes to rest—with re­spons­ib­il­ity, at­ten­tion and dur­a­tion.

Law is now a silent ex­pect­a­tion of mani­fest­a­tion: it does not pre-exist.
It comes into being when the world and we take shape to­geth­er.

Nature does not violate its laws.
They simply never existed without us.
