Law and Presence — From Ancient Law to Relationship: When Nature Does Not Obey

July 7, 2025

AI-assisted translation from the Greek. Greek original

The words we use to describe the world carry the memory of ages. The word “law” is not neutral. It is charged with theological, legal, philosophical and cultural projections. Law once meant the will of god, the order of the universe, the moral structure of existence. It was the unchanging background of the world: that which does not change, does not negotiate, does not enter into relationship.

But nature itself seems to say otherwise. Physics, chemistry, biology—sciences of the visible and the experimental—no longer rest on absolute constants. Every law, however general it may seem, holds under conditions. There are materials that, when heated, do not expand but contract. There are phenomena that change depending on whether we observe them. There are behaviours that escape linear causality.

This does not mean that nature violates the law. It means that law is not what we thought it was.

Law is not essence; it is a condition.

The word “law” enters science with philosophical authority. But ultimately, it is nothing more than a regularity describing the most frequent outcome under certain conditions. It has no ontological authority—it has a descriptive function.

Law does not say “what is”; it says “what usually happens when you do this”.

Consequently, when we change the conditions, the “law” does not collapse—it simply reveals its locality. There is no single law, but many cross-sections of reality, each of which is revealed under a particular presence.

Presence modifies the form of the world

The concept of law, as quantum physics has shown, depends on the observer’s position. Not because the observer “changes it by willing it”, but because the phenomenon as such is not fully determined before the interaction.

This calls on us to reconsider the root of knowledge:
– Can there be law without a subject?
– Can there be reality without presence?

Perhaps the world is not a ready-made text that we read, but a field that takes shape as we stand before it. Then “law” is not the truth of a system, but the form truth takes within the encounter.

Philosophy already knew this; now matter shows it too

The thought of Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger, and, much earlier, Heraclitus or the Church Fathers has already revealed that truth is not an object—but an event.

Truth is not what lies there for us to discover, but what is born when I truly exist before something.

And now nature itself, in extreme experiments, tells us this in the most material way: “You do not know me unless you stand before me. And I do not always stand in the same way.”

Towards a new definition of law

If we wish to speak truly of law in our time, we must leave behind the fantasy of fixed order and embrace the risk of relationship.

Law is not the fixed truth that holds without us.
It is the form truth takes when our gaze comes to rest—with responsibility, attention and duration.

Law is now a silent expectation of manifestation: it does not pre-exist.
It comes into being when the world and we take shape together.

Nature does not violate its laws.
They simply never existed without us.
